## Summary

1. **No exemplar fits a life by chronology alone. Each samples a life through one carrying device.** *Up* uses an object clock inside a 4-minute montage (about 4:05 of picture, a 4:10 music cue) [P1][41]. *Father and Daughter* gives a whole wordless life 8 minutes 30 seconds [19]. *Mama Micra*, the closest film to ours, gives a mother's life 24 minutes [48a][48b][48c]. *Ethel & Ernest* gives 43 years 94 minutes [47]. *Boyhood* gives 12 years 165 minutes [32]. In seconds of screen per year of life these run from about 4 (the *Up* montage, assuming about 60 years) to 825 (*Boyhood*). Our four scenarios are about 13, 27, 49 and 98 seconds per year at age 55 (Findings Q8).
2. **The device that matters most for us is the *Big Fish* move from the funeral to the bedside.** John August says that early drafts had the son tell the father's story at the funeral, and that his producers' change to the bedside "had a huge impact" [46]; the story is then told *to* the person while they are alive. Our premiere is that moment. We borrow the structure (a child tells a parent's life to the parent). We borrow none of the tall tales.
3. **The closest analogue is *Mama Micra* (2024, 24 min, animated documentary by a daughter about her mother).** It uses one hand-made material (felt), the mother's own recorded voice, and the daughter's counter-memories [48a][48b][48c]. Per the Wikipedia entry only, photo albums and letters were found after the mother's death and "corroborated" her account [48c]. The big difference: that mother knowingly recorded her voice. Ours must not. So we replace "her recorded interview" with "her found voice, used only as recorded" plus "relatives' recollections".
4. **Devices that survive Principle 4 unchanged:** sound-first assembly (*Flee*, *Waltz with Bashir*, *Montage of Heck*, *Listen to Me Marlon*) [1][2][6][28][29]; found voice used only as recorded [28][29]; a chorus of witnesses quoted verbatim [20][22]; letters or text shown verbatim [11]; a real object, photo or person replacing a stand-in at the end (*Tower*, *Waltz*) [6][34]; an object clock when the object is real; a song spine when the song is real and cleared [38]. **Need adaptation:** the *Up* montage, the *Big Fish* frame, the *Coco* song spine, the *Persepolis* colour-present frame, the *Missing Picture* read-by-another-voice text, *Boyhood*'s year-by-year locking. **Out:** invented incidents and tall tales, composite people, undisclosed fake archive (the unlabelled Super 8 in *Stories We Tell* [20]), staged deaths (*Dick Johnson Is Dead* [23]), whispered voice-over of a character's inner life (*Tree of Life* [33]), dialogue written for real people (*Flee* wrote scene dialogue [2]), and a wordless fable of a life built from invented images (*The Red Turtle* [18]).
5. **Films by a child about a parent converge on one lesson: the maker is the searcher, not the hero.** Nathaniel Kahn dropped "my reverential fake voice" for "my real voice" [22]. Bing Liu found he "was not hard on my mom or me" until his editor looked [26]. Sarah Polley had to give up "that feeling of control" [20]. For a sole maker who will be in the room, the fit is a **collector stance**: the maker appears as hands, handwriting and a few plain text cards; every biographical fact comes in someone else's voice or a found voice. No maker voice-over about her inner life. A recorded maker voice stays optional and mostly for the private cut. (Inference, built on [20][22][26].)
6. **Structures that degrade gracefully when a chapter has no photos, voice or witnesses:** (1) an object-and-place clock; (2) chapter-by-teller (each chapter visibly told by one person or one object); (3) a light "searcher" frame that turns gaps into content; (4) chronological chapters with a different grain per era. **Collapse:** an interview frame (needs the subject), a year-by-year slice, an epistolary spine without letters, a song spine without a cleared song, a wordless life cycle (needs invented images). The few-photos-to-thousands shift is handled by *inverting* time: long holds for scarce early images, one controlled burst or one "Tuesday" for the abundant late years (Findings Q3).
7. **Narration verdict (matrix in Templates, item 3).** Best stack for this film: found voice (short, verbatim, only where it exists; HER-PUBLIC in the public cut, HER-PRIVATE only in the private cut or after the reveal) + relatives' recollections (verbatim, 4 to 6 named voices at most) + text cards (the default workhorse and the fallback) + wordless passages with score where the picture is real. **No neutral narrator** (a hired voice breaks the sole-keeper rule and writes words for her), **no maker voice-over in the public cut** (optional later), and the anti-patterns stay anti-patterns: asking her for "a few words", cloning, re-cutting found speech into new sentences, recording her without consent.
8. **The process is two-pass and its unit is the slot, not the event.** A slot is an *evidence request* ("one image of the first home, one sensory detail, one witness line"), never a fact. Slots move through six states (S0 empty to S5 locked). A beat locks only when its evidence, consent, hard-chapter double decision, narration source and stranger-read are all in. This copies *Boyhood* (each year cut as shot, never knowing the whole story [30]), *Flee* (a "master assembly" of sound plus storyboards before animation [2]), *Waltz* (a 90-minute studio video turned into storyboards and animatics [6]) and Pixar (reels re-screened "seven or eight times" [57]). Polley's lesson is the scheduling rule: "it was sort of impossible to know what we needed for the re-creations before we knew what we already had", so reconstructions come *after* the evidence cut [20].
9. **Agents draft boards and animatics without inventing content by working only from asset and quote IDs.** Every noun in a card, caption or board frame must map to an evidence ID in a claims table. A frame with no ID is a grey "SLOT" frame, not a drawing. Anything made from testimony alone is L5 and captioned "Reconstructed from [name]'s memory, 2026" [P5]. The Archival Producers Alliance recommends cue sheets for all generative-AI elements and listing them in the credits [61].
10. **Five transferable rules for the emotional curve (Findings Q6):** (R1) the sincere version of a thing is shorter, quieter and later than its showy first appearance, and the humour sits on the showy one (*Coco*: showy cue 1:49 with a falling bell, lullaby 1:09, reunion 1:13 [38][39]); (R2) plant the ending's image early in an ordinary scene and test it on strangers (*Flee* kept the house-hunting scene after early audiences responded [1]); (R3) comic relief is a person who loves the sufferer, not a joke about them (Pinky in *Memoir of a Snail* [8]); (R4) refuse the reunion and let an object say goodbye (*Robot Dreams* [13][15]); (R5) cut the shot you think you need and withhold the unspeakable image (Adair cut the empty-apartment shot [30]; *The Missing Picture* leaves the grave to the voice [36]).
11. **Endings that meet the room:** the strongest evidence is the peak-end result: global judgments of plotless affective film clips (pilot set 55 to 138 s; the 12 test clips were shown at 21 to 41 s (short) and 64 to 125 s (long); 32 students) were driven by the peak and the end, and clip duration had a small effect [49]. It is old, small and not about narrative films, so it is a reason to spend design effort on the last 90 seconds and not to pad the middle, nothing stronger. Shapes: 12 min, "the trace"; 25 min, "the open chapter"; 45 min, "the return"; 90 min, "interval and coda". Do not use "a last shot that frames the room" (it forces every eye onto her). Let the house lights rising be the last shot.
12. **Runtime: audience science on this exact question does not exist.** What exists: peak-end and duration neglect on plotless clips (pilot set 55 to 138 s; the 12 test clips were shown at 21 to 41 s (short) and 64 to 125 s (long)) with 32 students aged 16 to 35 [49]; children's attention that builds with sustained, comprehensible looking [50]; US polls about commercial features (88-minute ideal, Boomers 93, Gen Z 82; n=2,000, June 2026) [54]; a funeral-industry guide that puts tribute videos at 3 to 8 minutes, 4 to 5 ideal [56]. Programmer rules are convention, not audience evidence: the Academy's 40-minute short ceiling [51]; Tribeca's "under 15 minutes is even better" and the "all documentaries are 10 minutes too long" truism [52]; mid-length being "difficult to programme" [53]. **The story argument is stronger than any of these:** at 60 percent dramatised time, our four scenarios need about 5, 10, 18 and 36 scene-grade, cleared, non-hard memories; the covert-gathering plan yields perhaps 8 to 24 (Findings Q8). **Recommendation:** build a **12-minute floor** that is never cut below; **target 15 (cap 18)** as the public cut, made of modules; treat **25** as a private-cut stretch that only unlocks if gates pass; **do not plan 45 or 90** on story grounds, not just feasibility. This is offered for reconciliation with production-scale; the maker decides.
13. **Humour without making her the joke:** (a) the laugh comes from her own found words or from a loved one's fond exasperation, never from narrator irony; (b) humour arrives *before* and *after* a loss beat and not on it (the *Up* clinic beat is about 10 seconds [P1]); (c) a family saying is made legible in one beat by showing its effect on the person who hears it, then returning it twice (*Up*'s jar and ties [P1]; Stanton's "don't give them 4, give them 2+2" [59]). Failure modes: roast, cringe, nostalgia kitsch, the in-joke reel.
14. **Language (options only):** single language with subtitles; her found voice in its original language with subtitles in the room's language; stacked bilingual text cards; wordless passages; two language versions of the narration (as *Flee* did: Danish and Dari with an English dub narrated by two named actors [7]). Reading-speed limits that bind every subtitle option: 42 characters per line, two lines, up to 20 characters per second for adults and 17 for children's programmes [60]. *Coco* kept Spanish "in context" so audiences follow without translation [43].
15. **Open and unfound:** no stopwatch timings; no controlled evidence on tribute-film length for mixed ages; thin evidence on screenings with the subject present; Caouette's consent for *Tarnation* not found; legal viewing routes to confirm for *Boyhood*, *The Tree of Life* and *Mama Micra* (Open questions).

**Reading the tables.**
- The structures that can be built from verbatim material are the *witness spine* (*Stories We Tell*, *My Architect*), the *found-voice spine* (*Marlon*, *Heck*) and the *object-and-text spine* (letters, house, jar). Everything else needs an adaptation rule.

**What the family-film debate adds.** Sonya Childress (Documentary Accountability Working Group) says family filmmakers should "acknowledge their positional power, both in their families and as ... interpreters of an experience", and notes that some family films are loving tributes that work as puff pieces. Emily Kunstler, filming her father's story, admits "we weren't going to get the full other side of the picture" [26]. David Siev (*Bad Axe*) kept his family involved at each stage, including which scenes to cut [26]. That option is closed to us by the surprise.

**How they avoid sentimentality and self-centring (patterns, with evidence).**
1. **Let accounts disagree** and do not resolve them (Polley [20]).
2. **Withhold the hard image** and let a voice or object carry it (*Up* [P1]; *The Missing Picture* [36]).
3. **Make the maker visible as a searcher**, not as a mourner: Kahn is in the film because the subject "required a reaction" [22].
4. **Get an outside eye.** Liu needed his editor [26]; Kahn's editor sent him back to re-interview his mother [22]. We have no editor. Substitutes: the Fable advisor at gates, a 72-hour wait, and a stranger-to-her screening (section Q5 and review 02 [P4]).
5. **Humour from the subject's own world**, not at them: Johnson built it with her father [23].
6. **Specifics, not adjectives** (inference): count the adjectives that praise her in the script ("wonderful", "selfless") and cut them to a few. Replace each with an act, an object or a quote.

**Which stance fits a sole maker who will be in the room.**
- **Recommended: the collector.** The maker's presence in the film is limited to handwriting, hands, a few plain-language cards that state what the maker knows and does not know ("I don't know what she was like at seven. Her sister does."), and the credit. All facts about her come in others' voices or a found voice. This turns the "what I do not yet know" column of the treatment into screen content. (Inference, built on [20][22][26].) MAK-CARD is allowed for the contract card and for plain statements of what the maker knows or does not know (gap cards); never for her feelings or inner life; capped at about 10 percent of runtime (balance check 4). This starting cap is untested.
- **Why not a recorded maker voice-over in the public cut.** The maker will be in the room, next to her. A long recorded monologue by the person who loves her, played to strangers, is the highest-risk form of the self-centring failure Liu and Kahn describe [22][26]. It also makes the film harder to read for strangers, who need the film to be about *her*. Keep a spoken maker voice for the 30 to 60 second live welcome in the run sheet [P3] and, optionally, the private cut.
- **Why not "I am her" narration.** Any first-person narration spoken *as* her is words in her mouth. Out under Principle 4.

### Q3. Which structures degrade gracefully, and which collapse, when a chapter has no photos, no voice or no witnesses?

**Ranking for uneven material (best first).** This ranking is our inference from the exemplars; no source tested it.

| Rank | Structure | Childhood with nothing | Last decade with thousands of photos | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Object-and-place clock** (a house, a jar, a car, a kitchen table) [P1][47] | Survives if any object or place from those years survives; a hand-drawn plan of the house from a relative's memory is L5 and counts | Can drown in objects; pick one | Best base layer |
| 2 | **Chapter-by-teller** (each chapter visibly told by one person or object) [20][22] | Survives: the teller is an older relative, a letter, or an object; **"no one is left to tell these years" is itself a true caption** | Many tellers; cap at 4 to 6 named voices | Best structure for a surprise film (review 01 [P8] suggests it) |
| 3 | **Light searcher frame** (the maker sorting a box; *Big Fish*, *My Architect*, *Stories We Tell*) [22][46] | Turns a gap into content ("there is no photo of this year") | Risk of self-centring | Good if capped |
| 4 | **Chronological chapters with a grain per era** (animated.md "era by medium") [P2] | Thin and visibly stand-in | Thick; imbalance | Fine as the skeleton; needs the inversion rule below |
| 5 | **Memory logic** (*Tree of Life* grammar) [33] | Needs sensory detail from witnesses | Works | Use inside a chapter, not as a spine |
| 6 | **Song or motif spine** (*Coco*) [38] | Needs a song attached to each era | Works | Thin overlay only; rights and kitsch risk |
| 7 | **Epistolary** (*Mary and Max*) [11] | Collapses if no letters | Emails and messages may continue it (animated.md interface timeline [P2]) | Insert only |
| 8 | **Wordless life cycle** (*Red Turtle*, *Father and Daughter*) [18][19] | Needs invented imagery | Works with real photos | Works for one chapter at most, labelled L5 |
| 9 | **Interview frame** (*Flee*, *Waltz*) [6][7] | Collapses (no subject) | Collapses | Becomes the witness chorus |
| 10 | **Year-by-year slice** (*Boyhood*) [32] | Collapses | Collapses | Process idea only |

**The few-photos-to-thousands shift (named in `animated.md`): the inversion rule.** (Inference.)
1. **Give scarce images long holds and abundant images short selection.** Hold a single surviving childhood image once per chapter at most (animated.md already caps it [P2]). For the recent decade, cut a controlled burst of 8 to 10 seconds, or show one ordinary day (the "Tuesday" interlude from `story.md`) and not a decade.
2. **Allocate screen time by chapter, not by how much evidence exists.** Otherwise the last decade (best evidence, most living people, most sensitive) takes over. Starting rule to test: no era above about 25 percent of runtime.
3. **Say the gap out loud once.** "No photographs survive from these years" is a true card and echoes *The Missing Picture* [36][37].
4. **Context images need a label.** If a street of the era is shown that is not hers, caption it ("a street in [town], 1978; not hers") so it is not mistaken for her record. The Wonder Years' fake home movies work because they are generic [P2]; for a real person generic is the failure mode.
5. **Change the grain, not the quality bar.** Each era gets its own material and treatment (animated.md decision 7: palette sampled from her scans, era by medium [P2]). Childhood is where the one hand-made stand-in material lives. The present is plain.

### Q5. The process for one person plus agents: treatment, beat sheet, narration, storyboard, animatic

**The stage ladder (artefact and the test at each stage).**

| Stage | Artefact | Test it must pass | Who does it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Evidence ledger | The story.md inventory CSV (source, timestamp, confidence A to D, consent status, drawer) [P1] | No row without a source; consent set; hard items in drawers | Maker (collects); agent (indexes, transcribes locally) |
| 1. Treatment (pass 1) | Templates item 1, written with slots and a "what I do not yet know" column | A stranger-read: someone who does not know her reads only the treatment and answers the five questions in the "who is she" brief | Maker + agent draft; a stranger-to-her reads |
| 2. Slot beat sheet (pass 1) | Templates item 2, slots at S0 | Balance checks: seconds per era, density, number of hard beats, narration-source mix, label-level mix | Agent computes; maker decides |
| 3. Paper edit (pass 2, per chapter) | Two-column document: left = picture or asset ID, right = quote or card text with source [58] | Traceability: every word spoken or printed maps to a quote ID or a maker-authored fact with an evidence ID. Radio test: it works with the eyes closed | Agent drafts from IDs; maker approves |
| 4. Sound-first assembly (radio cut) | Audio only: found voice, witness quotes, ambience, placeholder music (*Flee*'s "master assembly ... just sound with storyboards" [2]; *Marlon*'s edit, largely black screen with audio until visuals came (paraphrase) [29]) | Does it hold a listener who cannot see? Is the first 2 minutes clear to a stranger? | Agent assembles; maker listens at distance |
| 5. Storyboard | One frame per phrase, each frame citing an asset ID or marked SLOT | Claims table: no frame shows anything without an ID or an L5 testimony ID | Agent drafts from IDs |
| 6. Animatic | Boards cut to the radio cut with scratch audio only (maker's temporary voice; never her voice re-cut) | Runtime vs budget; density per era; hard-chapter flags; stranger-read #2 | Agent assembles; maker + stranger review |
| 7. Lock per chapter | Chapter status S5 | The lock rule below | Maker, after the 72-hour second decision [P4] |

**What to copy from *Flee*'s sound-first assembly.** Rasmussen interviewed Amin about 20 times over three to four years, used only the audio in the film, and built a "master assembly ... just sound with storyboards" before animation. He called this "reversed editing": normally editors shape footage already shot, but here every shot had to be created, so the process ran the other way (paraphrase) [2]. The editor describes cutting the dialogue first as "lines of voices" animated a little later, and the animatic had to hit an exact running length [1]. Archive footage was found while cutting and recognised by Amin; archive "squared in on the canvas" near the end, which the editor says prepared the audience for the final shot [1]. **Copy:** sound first; edit before animating; archive grounded in the actual people. **Do not copy:** Rasmussen wrote and voiced scene dialogue for scratch [2].

**What to copy from documentary paper edits.** A paper edit is the film's structure written as text before picture editing; the classic two-column form puts visuals on the left and audio on the right, with struck-through text for cuts and optional narration links between interview lines [58]. The reason is speed: rearranging a document is cheaper than moving clips (vendor claim; consistent across sources). **Our adaptation:** add a third column, "evidence ID", and a fourth, "consent status".

**What to copy from Pixar.** Storyboard artists record scratch voices for reels; the team cuts the reel on video "with dialogue, music and sound effects" and re-screens about "seven or eight times before the film is really ready to produce" [57]. The Up story "took not months, but years" in one secondary account (UNVERIFIED, blog). **Our adaptation:** reels are replaced by the animatic; the screenings are replaced by self-review at 48 hours plus two stranger-reads (after the vertical slice and after the full animatic). *Flee* kept a house-hunting scene it had nearly cut because early audiences responded [1]. That is the argument for the stranger-read.

**How to write a slot-based beat sheet before material exists, and lock chapters as it arrives.**
1. **Write slots as evidence requests.** Example: `SLOT C2-B3, function=PLACE, need=1 image of the first home (L0/L1) + 1 sense detail (smell/sound) + 1 witness line, fallback=card "no photograph survives"`. Never write a fact ("grew up in a village") until a source gives it.
2. **Design the skeleton from functions, not events.** The functions are the same for any life: cold open (who is she), place, object, Tuesday, turn, texture, relief, hard-chapter glimpse, time-passing, chapter card, ending. A function list needs no facts. (The slot library is in Templates, item 2.)
3. **Give every slot a state.** S0 empty (function and time only). S1 evidence promised (a person or box is identified). S2 evidence in hand (assets have IDs). S3 drafted (paper edit and boards exist). S4 reviewed (stranger-read passed; 48-hour review passed). S5 locked (all gates below).
4. **Lock rule for S5.** (a) every beat has evidence IDs or an L5 testimony ID; (b) consent status is not "not-asked" for any third party who is identifiable and heard; (c) hard-chapter flag decided twice, 72 hours apart, both "yes" [P4]; (d) narration source assigned and traceable; (e) stranger-read passed for the chapter; (f) runtime inside the budget.
5. **Lock in the order of evidence richness, not chronology.** *Boyhood* did this in the opposite direction by necessity: Adair cut each year as it was shot and never knew the whole story in advance; Linklater gave her milestones year by year, and she designed the next opening from the outgoing final shot, "seamless and un-gimmicky" [30][31]. The *transition* between chapters is therefore designed at lock time for both neighbours.
6. **Vertical slice = the wrapper plus one chapter.** Build and lock the cold open (first 75 to 90 seconds, the "who is she" problem) *and* the richest chapter first. This tests the stranger-read, the narration stack and the ending shape on one real piece.

**How agents draft boards and animatics without inventing content.**
- **Input is IDs, never prose.** The agent receives a beat row, its evidence IDs, the quote file and the style bible. It may not receive "write a scene about her wedding".
- **Output is a claims table** listing every noun, date, number, name, place and colour in the card text or board, each mapped to an ID. Any row without an ID is flagged "UNSUPPORTED" and the frame stays a grey SLOT frame.
- **Allowed:** transcribe locally, index, cluster quotes by theme, propose orders, compute timing, draft cards that are *verbatim quotes with attribution*, generate camera moves on real assets, flag contradictions between witnesses, produce L5 stand-in options *only* from a testimony quote ("Reconstructed from [name]'s memory, 2026" [P5]).
- **Not allowed:** write quotes; fill a gap with plausible detail; invent people, clothes, rooms, weather or dates; generate new scenes of her; clone any voice; re-cut found speech into new sentences.
- **Cue sheets.** Log every generative element in a cue sheet and the credits, as the Archival Producers Alliance recommends [61].

### Q6. Emotional curve and restraint: four sequences not already timed, and five transferable rules

**What is and is not available.** No beat was stopwatch-timed (limit 1). What follows uses published cue lengths, editors' and makers' accounts, and written plot summaries. Treat durations as approximate.

**Sequence 1. *Coco*, "Remember Me" payoff.**
- The song is performed four times [38]. On the English soundtrack the cues run: showy version (Bratt) **1:49**; lullaby version (García Bernal and two children) **1:09**; reunion version (Gonzalez and Murguía) **1:13**; end-credits duet **2:46** [39]. Album lengths can differ from in-film lengths (not timed).
- Reading: the first, showy performance is the longest and carries the comedy (a giant bell falls on the singer as it ends [38]). The two sincere versions are each about two-thirds of its length and plainly arranged. Anderson-Lopez calls the first version "showboating"; Robert Lopez calls the showy and the intimate presentations antithetical choices "you can take in art", and calls the lullaby version "a song as a gift to someone you love" [38]. The long pop duet comes *after* the payoff, in the credits, as decompression.
- **What is withheld:** the film's resolution is a very old woman singing along and then telling stories about her father, a quiet act, not a spectacle [40].
- **Where sound drops out:** not timed. UNVERIFIED.

**Sequence 2. *Mary and Max*, the letters.**
- The letters carry the story across several years with a single flat narrator [11]. Dry humour is "pervasive" (Slant, as quoted on [11]); a search snippet from the National Film and Sound Archive of Australia [66] says the loneliness is balanced by the maker's humour and names one comic set piece and "the letter where Max forgives Mary" as the most poignant scene (snippet only; UNVERIFIED).
- The film ends about a year after Max's last letter [11].
- Restraint device (inference): a narrator who does not emote lets the audience supply the feeling. Not timed.

**Sequence 3. *Flee*, the archive inserts.**
- Archive footage was found during the cut of the trafficking-and-Stockholm scene (a sinking ship, a prison); Amin "recognized all the different people" [1].
- Archive reappears near the end "squared in on the canvas", which the editor says prepared the audience for the final shot (paraphrase) [1].
- The final shot mirrors an earlier house-hunting scene that the team nearly cut and kept after early audiences responded strongly [1]. The animation keeps its out-of-continuity jumps "to keep the audience aware that this is actually a documentary film" [1].
- Trauma is drawn in a looser, impressionistic style [2]. Not timed.

**Sequence 4. *Robot Dreams*, the ending.**
- From a rooftop Robot sees Dog with a new friend and runs toward them, but that reunion is a fantasy. In reality Robot chooses not to chase Dog, plays "September" on its boombox, dances once while Dog does not know, then hides and goes back to the friend who rebuilt it [15].
- The film runs September to September, and "the film talks about memory, talks about how memory helps us to recover from loss" [14]. Berger: "In a film that doesn't have dialogue, the music is the voice of the characters" [12]. The song returns as a piano version, in Robot's whistle, and at the end [12].
- The production could not afford the song for the closing credits: "It was too expensive" [14].

**Sequence 5. *Memoir of a Snail*, the turns.**
- The film opens at the deathbed of Grace's only friend and looks back [8]. Elliot: "I like extremes"; "it's always the story first, animation second"; sixteen drafts [8].
- BFI describes a "shitstorm of misery" with Pinky, a free spirit, as comic relief [search snippet on [8]].

**Five transferable rules.**
- **R1. The sincere version is shorter, quieter and later than the showy one, and the humour sits on the showy one.** Evidence: *Coco* cues 1:49 (falling bell) against 1:09 and 1:13 [38][39].
- **R2. Plant the ending's image early in an ordinary scene; test it on strangers.** Evidence: *Flee*'s house-hunting scene [1]; *Up*'s jar and ties [P1]; Stanton's "Don't give them 4. Give them 2+2" [59].
- **R3. Comic relief is a person who loves the sufferer, not a joke about the sufferer, and it goes beside the loss, not on it.** Evidence: Pinky [8]; the *Up* central-loss beat is about 10 seconds [P1], and nothing in the written breakdowns places a joke on it (inference). [92] is cited only for holding attention in quiet passages, not for humour.
- **R4. Refuse the reunion; let an object carry the goodbye.** Evidence: *Robot Dreams* [15]; *Up* cuts from the hospital to the church steps and never shows the death [P1][42].
- **R5. Cut the shot you think you need, and withhold the unspeakable image.** Evidence: Adair removed the final empty-apartment shot so the cut goes straight to the next year [30]; Docter's team removed dialogue and effects from the montage [P2]; *The Missing Picture* leaves the grave to the narration [36]; *Waltz* gives its worst moment to real footage, where the essay describes the camera breaking (paraphrase) [5].

**Where sound drops out (what we can say).** *Up* stripped dialogue and sound effects from the montage, leaving music and picture [P2]. *Robot Dreams* has no dialogue and uses music "as the voice" [12][15]. *The Red Turtle* has none [18]. Dropouts inside those films are not timed.

### Q7. Endings that meet the room

**Evidence base (thin).**
- **Peak-end.** In Fredrickson and Kahneman, 32 Berkeley students (aged 16 to 35, median 22) watched plotless film clips (the pilot set ran 55 to 138 seconds; the 12 test clips were shown at 21 to 41 s (short) and 64 to 125 s (long); the short versions were about a third as long) and gave real-time and global ratings. Retrospective evaluations "appear to be determined by a weighted average of 'snapshots' of the actual affective experience, as if duration did not matter"; peak plus end predicted them well. The authors add that the peak-and-end rule "does not exhaust the factors that govern global evaluations" [49]. Reading: put the film's best material and its cleanest image at the end. This is evidence about *remembered* evaluation of plotless clips, not about a narrative film, and not about comfort in a seat.
- **Tribute formats.** *This Is Your Life* ran on surprise and a hand-over to the audience, with documented leaks and a subject who walked away in 1961 [P1 (story.md [28][30])]. These are television records, not screening studies.
- **Screenings with the subject present (all anecdotal).** Dick Johnson attended earlier festival screenings; Kirsten Johnson says that each time he watched he reacted as if he understood why he was there ("Oh, this is why we're here!"), and asked where he was he said "heaven" (snippets and [24]). Polley's family attended the Toronto screening, and a Q&A followed (snippet only). Barnard screened *The Arbor* for the residents of the estate and felt "ownership" (snippet) [63]. The key gap: **no source describes a tribute film premiered to a living subject who did not know**. This is UNVERIFIED territory, and the run sheet is the only protection.
- **Craft endings.** *Waltz* dissolves into real footage [5][6]. *Tower* has the real survivor replace the actress, "staring at the viewer" [34]. *Flee* ends on a planted shot [1]. *Robot Dreams* ends on a chosen restraint [15]. *Big Fish* moves the telling to the bedside [46]. *Up* ends the montage on the church steps turning into the house stairs [42]. In the film itself Ellie's last note says to go on and have a new adventure (from memory; UNVERIFIED this session).

**Risks when she sits in a public room, and mitigations.**
| Risk | Mitigation already in surprise-ops [P3] | Added by the film (this note) |
|---|---|---|
| Every eye is on her; pressure to react | Seat choice (side or aisle), companion, lights low not dark, no cue to applaud, 20 seconds of silence, "No speech. There's food." | The ending needs nothing from her and never addresses her in the second person |
| A hard chapter lands in front of strangers | Hard-chapter decision sheet; any OUT on the shared cut is out | The public cut contains no HOLD or PRIVATE item; if in doubt, out [P4] |
| A reveal beat makes the room look at her | Quiet word beforehand | No beat reveals a secret, a hidden relative or an estrangement to the room |
| She wants to leave | "Step out any time"; exit light | Chapters are modules with a visible card between them, so a step-out loses one chapter, not the plot |
| Applause or speeches become a burden | No toast unless she signals | Ending ends in a held card and house lights, not a "call to react" |
| She refuses, stops the film or leaves at the reveal | "Step out any time"; exit light [P3] | Off-ramp that costs her nothing in public; a pre-agreed way to stop or shorten the film; no camera trained on her face; an early-discovery rule decided in advance by the maker (round-1 follow-up Q4). The run sheet plans for refusal as a normal outcome, not an edge case (surprise-ops owns the lines) |

**One ending shape per runtime scenario.**
- **12 minutes: "the trace."** The 1:45 ending, including end credits, is itemised for example as object 0:55, black 0:05, credits 0:45 (sum 1:45). The object is the surviving object plus, if it exists and is cleared, a few seconds of her handwriting, or HER-PUBLIC if it exists and is decided under the 72-hour double decision (never a voicemail, which is HER-PRIVATE); hard cut to black; end credits; the house lights come up. No music tail. (Peak-end: end on the strongest cleared image.)
- **15 minutes: "the trace and the open chapter."** The 12-minute trace, preceded by a held EMPTY_CHAPTER card (6 to 10 s) in her name: "Chapter [N]: [Her name]. To be told by her." Then the object, then a hard cut to black. This sits inside the 1:45 ending budget, which includes end credits: object 0:50, empty chapter 0:08, black 0:05, credits 0:42 (sum 1:45).
- **25 minutes: "the open chapter."** An ordinary-day sequence (the present "Tuesday"), then a held title card: "Chapter [N]: [Her name]. To be told by her." (the empty chapter in her name from review 01 [P8] and the gift after the gift). Then a credits crawl with the cue sheet and "Made by [maker], for [Name]". The 1:30 ending, including end credits, is itemised for example as open-chapter card 0:08, black 0:05, credits 1:17 (sum 1:30).
- **45 minutes: "the return."** The opening image returns, changed (*Persepolis* closes its airport frame [44]; *Memoir of a Snail* ends a year after the deathbed it opened on [10]; *Big Fish* ends at the funeral after the bedside telling [45]); then the open chapter; then a contributor credit that names each voice as part of a chorus. Needs a planned pause at the second-act break (see Q8).
- **90 minutes: "interval and coda."** An interval at the end of Act II; a ten-minute reset with food; Act III; the return; the open chapter; four minutes of credits as decompression. Not recommended.

**Do not do:** a last shot that frames the room (it points the camera of the film at her); a live-action shot of her taken without her knowledge; direct address ("Mum, this is for you") as the last line unless the maker has decided it deliberately, because it asks her to respond.

### Q8. Runtime for a mixed-age public audience

**Separate three kinds of evidence.**

*Audience evidence (what it does and does not show).*
1. **Peak-end and duration neglect** [49]: retrospective evaluation of plotless clips (pilot set 55 to 138 s; the 12 test clips were shown at 21 to 41 s (short) and 64 to 125 s (long)) depended little on duration and a lot on peak and end. Caveats: 32 students; very short, plotless clips; it measures remembered evaluation, not comfort, hearing, hunger or a child's patience. Direction only.
2. **Children's attention to television** [50]: in 3 and 5 year olds, the longer a look at the screen is maintained, the more likely it continues; distractors pull less after about 15 seconds of sustained look. Reading: the first minute must be continuous and comprehensible for the youngest viewers. It is not a runtime rule.
3. **Polls about commercial features** [54][55]: Talker Research (n=2,000 US adults, 11 to 17 June 2026) reports an average ideal of 88 minutes (2024: 92); Boomers 93, Gen X 89, Millennials 86, Gen Z 82; 10 percent chose two hours or longer [54]. YouGov UK in 2015 found 55 percent say under two hours with the favourite between 90 minutes and two hours; a later 17-country YouGov report found 71 percent say two to three hours (snippets, disagree) [55]. These are stated preferences about entertainment features, not about a tribute film at a party.
4. **Tribute-video guidance** [56]: 3 to 8 minutes for a service, 4 to 5 ideal, 3 to 4 seconds per photo; longer only when looping. Vendor blog; the notes in `surprise-ops` already say the funeral-industry figures are not a study of reveals [P3].
5. **Not found:** any controlled study of attention or satisfaction for 12 versus 25 versus 45 minute tribute films in a mixed-age room. UNVERIFIED that none exists; none turned up.

*Convention (not audience science).*
- The Academy defines a short as 40 minutes or less *including credits*, and an animated feature as more than 40 minutes [51].
- Programmers say 30 to 60 minutes is "difficult to programme" (Modern Times Review) [53]; Tribeca's co-executive director: "Twenty minutes or below gives it a much better chance of getting in" and "Under 15 minutes is even better"; a "truism among programmers" is that "all documentaries are 10 minutes too long"; True/False's David Wilson sees the best work at 70 to 85 minutes [52]. These govern festival slots. Our premiere is a party, not a slate.

*Story and evidence (the real argument).*
- **Density.** Seconds of screen per year of life, at age 55 (replace with her real age A: seconds = minutes × 60 / A):

| Scenario | s per year of her life | Comparable exemplar |
|---|---|---|
| 12 min | about 13 | denser than the *Up* montage (about 4) and *Father and Daughter* (about 7), so montage grammar |
| 25 min | about 27 | *Mama Micra* is 24 minutes for a life [48a][48b][48c] |
| 45 min | about 49 | no clean comparator |
| 90 min | about 98 | *Ethel & Ernest* 131 s per year, from a detailed memoir by the son of the couple [47] |
| (*Boyhood*) | 825 | a 12-year shoot [32] |

- **Scene-grade memories needed** (inference; assumptions explicit). Assume 60 percent of runtime is dramatised scenes of about 90 seconds each (story.md Step 13: dramatise = 45 to 120 s [P1]). Needed scenes: **12 min about 5; 25 min about 10; 45 min about 18; 90 min about 36.** Each must have a place, people, an object, a sound, a source, consent, and must pass the hard-chapter gate. The covert-gathering plan assumes a minimum of 8 to 12 relatives and 2 insiders [P1]; the 2 insiders no longer exist (sole maker), so the maker alone must reach every relative. At 2 to 4 usable scenes each, 8 to 12 relatives give 16 to 48 before filtering; after dropping hard, uncleared or duplicate items perhaps half survive: **8 to 24**. 12 and 25 sit inside that range; 45 sits at its top; 90 is beyond it. 90 minutes would force filling with generic or invented material, which is what Principle 4 forbids.
- **Hard-chapter exposure.** Each added minute is more exposure to strangers of a life she cannot approve. A shorter film has fewer decisions that rest on one person's judgement.
- **Selection is the craft.** Strachey called the first duty "a brevity which excludes everything that is redundant and nothing that is significant" [P1]. The life films that work are samples.

**What each scenario demands structurally.**
| Scenario | Structure | Budget (including end credits) |
|---|---|---|

**Recommendation memo (for reconciliation with production-scale; the maker decides).**
1. **Commit to a 12-minute floor** that is a complete film and is never cut below. It is the same film as the longer cut minus modules, not a separate film.
2. **Target 15 minutes (range 14 to 16, hard cap 18) for the public cut.** It is built from a **wrapper** (cold open about 1:15 plus ending about 1:45 = 3:00) and **chapter modules** of about 3:00 each. Four modules plus the wrapper make 15:00. The number comes from content, not from a festival rule: strangers need about 75 seconds of "who is she" (Templates, item 5), and Principle 3 (texture) needs more than the 2:15 chapters of the 12-minute plan.
3. **Treat 25 minutes as a stretch for the private cut.** Six modules plus wrapper. It unlocks only if the gates in item 5 pass.
4. **Do not plan 45 or 90 minutes.** The story argument (5 / 10 / 18 / 36 scene-grade memories against 8 to 24 available) and the hard-chapter exposure decide it before production-scale does. The charter says "feature film ... so good it could be shown in theatres" [P7]: the Academy defines more than 40 minutes as a feature [51]; our reading (inference) is that "theatrical" describes the quality per minute, not the minute count, as review 02 argues [P4].
5. **Kill-or-cut dates** (consistent with review 02's schedule [P4]; production-scale owns the calendar). By **2026-12-20** (the slice gate): if the wrapper plus one chapter is not at the bar, drop to the 12-minute floor and stop adding modules. By **about 2027-01-17**: the count of modules at S4 decides the cut (four = 15 min; six = 25 min private). **2027-02-06**: picture lock; modules at S3 or below are cut, never rushed.
6. **Reconciliation notes for production-scale.** Cost is per module: wrapper (one-off), then about 3:00 each. Reuse is highest in the wrapper and the ending. The surprise-ops run sheet already tolerates a 10 to 25 minute pause after the film and a longer private cut later [P3]; a 15-minute public cut fits it.
7. **Delta from existing playbooks:** `surprise-ops` Step 10 puts the shared cut at 6 to 10 minutes with a hard cap of 12. A public room changes the test from "would she tell this to family" to "would she tell this to strangers" [P4]. A 15-minute public cut breaches that cap by three minutes. The maker should decide whether to widen the cap to 16 to 18 minutes, with the reasons above, or to hold it at 12 and treat the 15-minute film as the private cut. `format.md` Decision 7 is a chapter count, not "8 to 12 minutes" (see Deltas); the same decision applies.

### Q9. Humour without making her the joke

**How life films earn laughs (patterns with sources).**
1. **From the subject's own world, co-authored.** Johnson: "We made lots of terrible psychiatrist jokes throughout the whole time"; "I tried very seriously, very hard, to make it funny" [23]. Not available to us because she cannot co-author. Substitute: her own found remarks and relatives' fond exasperation.
2. **From a loving character.** Pinky as comic relief inside a grim story [8].
3. **From small non-human business.** The physical comedy of *Robot Dreams* (the director lists "There's humor" among his recurring elements [12]).
4. **From dry narration.** *Mary and Max* [11].
5. **From a *callback*.** Set a gag, return it, then pay it off with a variation. Writing guides call this the rule of three; the pages found were low quality (snippets) and I treat them as a starting place only. *Up* does it with the jar and the ties [P1].

**Making a family saying legible to strangers in one beat (inference built on [59] and [P1]).**
- **Beat 1 (self-explaining).** Show the saying *and its effect* on the person who hears it, in one shot, so the meaning travels through the reaction, not through a caption. The audience learns "this phrase means X in this family" because they see X happen.
- **Beat 2 (echo).** A second person, in a different year or room, says it or does the gesture. The laugh is the audience recognising a pattern: Stanton's "2+2" [59].
- **Beat 3 (payoff).** Return it once, changed, at an emotional place: quieter, or said by someone else, or missing.
- **Test (one-beat legibility test):** show three strangers the 8-to-10 second clip. Ask: what does the phrase mean, when is it said, how do people feel? If they cannot answer, add a second beat. If they can, do not add a caption. Do not explain the joke.

**Failure modes and tests.**
| Failure mode | What it looks like | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Roast | Foibles she would not tell strangers; the room laughs *at* her | Would she tell this about herself to a room of strangers? If unsure, out |
| Cringe | Narrator irony; forced banter; staged "funny" reconstructions that put words in mouths | Is every funny line a found line or a witness line? |
| Nostalgia kitsch | Decade clichés (avocado appliances, Cooper Black, heavy grain) [P2]; Kodak sentimentality [P2] | Does the detail come from *her* scans, or from a decade cliché? |
| In-joke reel | Jokes only the family gets | The three-stranger clip test |
| Laughing at a loss | A gag on top of a hard beat | R3: humour beside, not on |
| Deflection | Jokes to avoid the hard chapter | Does the humour *arrive before and return after*, not replace? |

### Q10. If her life or the room spans languages (options only; language is an open charter decision)

| Option | How it works | Evidence | Cost and risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. One language, subtitles for others | Narration and witnesses in the main language; subtitles for the rest | Standard subtitle limits: 42 characters per line, two lines, up to 20 characters per second for adults, 17 for children's programmes [60] | Children and older viewers read slower; keep lines short; keep captions off her face |
| 2. Found voice in its original language | Her found voice or a relative's voice stays in the language spoken, with subtitles in the room's language | Netflix marks untranslated dialogue "[speaking Spanish]" and translated dialogue "[in Spanish]" [60]; *Flee* is Danish and Dari plus Russian, with newsreels in Swedish, French and English [7] | Subtitle count rises; cards in the same type as the film |
| 3. Context-carried foreign words | Keep a few words untranslated if the picture explains them | *Coco*: Unkrich called leaving out the film's language "inappropriate"; there is "purposefully a lot of Spanish spoken in the film", used in context so audiences can follow (the example given is *hijo* as a nickname for a son) [43] | Works for a word or two, not for sentences |

**Practical rule (inference).** Decide the language question *before* the sound-first assembly; the radio cut is where subtitle load is measured. Aim for fewer than about one subtitle per 8 to 10 seconds of picture in any chapter that kids and older relatives must follow, and test with the clip test.

## Exemplars and what they teach us

*(Short lessons per exemplar. The tables in Q1 and Q2 carry the device, the minutes and the verdicts; this section keeps the one thing each film teaches.)*

- ***Up*.** The best-timed example in `story.md`: about 77 seconds of warmth before the first blow; the loss beat is about 10 seconds; the death is never shown [P1]. Teaches: object clock, withheld death, music cue as clock (the cue is 4:10 for about 4:05 of picture [41]). Does not teach: how to fill a life with real events.
- ***Big Fish*.** John August explicitly resists templates: "Theory is theory. Writing is real, and really hard sometimes" [46]. He says the move of the telling to the bedside "had a huge impact" [46]. Teaches: a child telling a parent's life to the parent, while the parent lives.
- ***Coco*.** One song, four contexts, the sincere ones shorter [38][39]. The altar of photographs as the memory device is the exact match to a life made of photographs. Teaches: cue lengths and the placing of humour on the showy version.
- ***Persepolis*.** An airport frame in colour; flashbacks in monochrome; a woman changing her mind about boarding a plane [44]. Teaches: the room-today as the colour frame.
- ***Flee* and *Waltz with Bashir*.** The process is the gift: record first, edit, then animate [1][2][5][6]. Teaches: sound-first; deliberate reminders that it is a documentary; archive grounding; the ending dissolve into real footage. Flee's editor: "It is a documentary that is animated and not the other way around" (snippet) [4].
- ***The Missing Picture*.** One hand-made material for the unphotographed; the unseeable left to the voice [36][37]. Teaches: how to say the gap.
- ***Tower*.** Animated for what cannot be filmed; plain documentary for what can; the real person replaces the stand-in [34].
- ***The Red Turtle* and *Father and Daughter*.** Stillness against emotion; no backstory; a whole life in a landscape and the seasons [16][17][19]. Teaches pace. Not usable as fact.
- ***Robot Dreams*.** The most useful ending for a loved one: choosing not to be seen (inference from [15]). Also a licensing lesson: the song was too expensive for the credits [14].
- ***Memoir of a Snail* and *Mary and Max*.** Adam Elliot: "The audience will forgive bad animating but they'll never forgive a bad story" (BFI headline) [8]; sixteen drafts over eight years [8][10]. Teaches: story first, humour carried by people.
- ***Boyhood*.** The process exemplar for locking as material arrives [30][31].
- ***The Tree of Life*.** Memory logic; five credited editors [33]. Teaches grammar, not facts.
- ***Ethel & Ernest* and *Mama Micra*.** The two child-about-parent animated films closest to a true account that we found. The first is a 94-minute chronicle at 131 seconds per year; the second is the 24-minute film nearest to ours [47][48a][48b][48c].
- ***Montage of Heck*, *Listen to Me Marlon*.** The subject's found audio as the spine with no re-cutting into new sentences [28][29].
- ***Stories We Tell*, *My Architect*, *Dick Johnson Is Dead*, *Minding the Gap*, *Tarnation*.** The stance films (Q2).
- **Leads not researched:** *Daughter* (Kashcheeva, 2019, 15 minutes, wordless stop-motion about a father and daughter; fiction, snippet only); *Chris the Swiss* (animated documentary by a woman about her absent cousin; only basic facts seen); *No Home Movie* (Akerman, her mother; static long takes; snippets only); *Funan* (a son's animated film from his mother's testimony; snippets only).

### 5. One-page brief: the "who is she" problem (the first two minutes for strangers)

**The problem.** Most of the room does not know her. The family does. If the first two minutes are written for the family, strangers drift and the film becomes an in-joke reel. If they are written for strangers as a CV ("born in..., worked at..."), the family and she are bored and the film feels like an obituary. Review 02 flags this as the main story risk of a public screening [P4].

**What a stranger must be able to say after two minutes (the exit test).**
1. A name for her, said by someone who loves her. (Not by a narrator.) The reader checks that it was said, and by whom in relation to her, not which name.
2. One concrete thing she does or keeps (an object, a gesture, a saying).
3. What she is like to be around (one laugh, one warmth).
4. Who is telling me this (a voice and its relation to her; in stranger copies the relation is a `[relation]` token).
5. How this film works (the disclosure: who tells, what is drawn).
6. Why I am watching (the quiet promise: a life, told by those who were in it).

**Beat budget for about 75 to 90 seconds (the cold open; shorten for 12 min).** Times are targets, not facts about anyone.
| Time | Beat | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:08 | Black; one room sound | A true sound from her life if we have it; no stock |
| 0:08 to 0:25 | Her name said aloud by a relative's voice, with the name and relation on screen | Verbatim; the relative is one of the 4 to 6 named voices |
| 0:25 to 0:50 | One object in use: hands, not face (a real photograph of her hands, or a labelled stand-in) | No new face of her is generated; an object is safer for dignity and for the surprise |
| 0:50 to 1:10 | The first laugh: a loved one's fond line or her found remark | Humour from loving voices only (R3) |
| 1:10 to 1:25 | The contract: one plain card in the maker's hand: "[Name], told by the people who were there. What is drawn is marked." | Disclosure said once (animated.md [P2]) |
| 1:25 to 1:30 | Title | Title card last, not first |

**Rules.**
1. **Do not open on her face.** The room is already looking at her. Pull their eyes to the screen with an object, a voice and a laugh. (Inference.)
2. **Do not say the whole arc.** Offer a promise, not a summary (Stanton: "Don't give them 4. Give them 2+2" [59]).
3. **Keep the family delighted too.** Include one thing relatives have not seen (a photograph, a recording) so the opening is news to them as well.
4. **No hard beat before about a quarter to a third of the way in** [P1].
5. **The first sound must be comprehensible to the youngest viewer.** Children's attention builds only once a look is sustained, and stalls if confused [50].
6. **No second-person address to her.** The room is the audience.
7. **Check it on strangers.** Two stranger-reads: the paper treatment, and the animatic's first two minutes. If any reader cannot answer questions 1 to 4, rewrite.
8. **Stranger-read copies substitute a code name for her and `[relation]` tokens for every named teller; the exit test asks whether a name was said by someone who loves her and who in relation is telling it, not which name; real names live only in the private repo.**

**Fallback if no relative's voice exists yet.** Open on a title card with the maker's plain sentence and the object, then the first witness arrives at 0:30. The structure holds; the order changes.

## Risks, pitfalls and ethics (with mitigations)

1. **Guru templates forced onto a life.** Save the Cat or the hero's journey would make her a protagonist with an inciting incident. August says labels are not essential: "Theory is theory. Writing is real" [46]. The beat sheet uses functions (cold open, place, object, turn, texture, relief, ending) and states, not acts. Her chapters may not resolve; McAdams' redemption arc is not a plot requirement [P1].
2. **Fiction as licence.** *Up*, *Coco* and *Big Fish* invent freely. Keep "borrowed structure" and "borrowed licence" apart (Table 1.2). The beat sheet has a `borrowed_licence` column that must read `NONE` on every beat, as a guard.
3. **Narration anti-patterns (record only as bans).** (a) Asking her for "a few words" under a pretext; (b) cloning or synthesising her voice; (c) re-cutting her found speech into sentences she did not say; (d) recording her without consent; (e) a relative asked to read as her; (f) a narrator who says what she felt; (g) animating her face to "speak" words she did not say.
4. **Undisclosed fakes.** The fake Super 8 in *Stories We Tell* looks archival by design [20]; critics split [P2]. Our reconstructions are visibly reconstructions (charter principle 4).
5. **Sole-maker blind spot.** Liu's solo edit was too gentle on his mother and himself until his editor saw it [26]. Mitigation: Fable advisor at gates, 48-hour review, 72-hour double decision for hard chapters, strangers-to-her reads [P4].
6. **Self-centring.** A recorded maker monologue to strangers beside her is the highest-risk form (Q2). Mitigation: collector stance, cap, text cards.
7. **Memory presented as fact.** Witnesses embroider. Elliot says one detail of a real anecdote he was told "never happened" [8]; *Big Fish* is about exactly this [45]. Mitigation: "X remembers"; confidence grade A to D [P1]; contradictions left standing [20].
8. **The hard chapter in a public room.** Default OUT for the public cut on any doubt [P4]; trait not incident [P3]; the "gift after the gift" mechanism lets her put something back [P4].
9. **Third parties.** Relatives, estranged people, the dead and minors appear. Consent card [P3]; unresolved release question for public exhibition [P4]. Note Johnson's brother objecting to the staged funeral [24]: other family members have views.
10. **Surprise leaks through the writing process.** Treatments, boards and cue sheets live in the private repo, not in shared drives or chats [P3]. Stranger-read copies substitute a code name for her and `[relation]` tokens for every named teller; real names live only in the private repo (Templates, item 5, rule 8).
11. **Music and rights.** Do not write a scene that depends on an uncleared song (R1 and the *Robot Dreams* example [14]).
12. **Runtime creep.** More minutes means more hard-chapter exposure and more invented filler. The 12-minute floor and the module lock dates are the control.
13. **Over-trusting exemplar write-ups.** Pages are summarised by the fetch tool; some quotes are from snippets. Verify before printing a quotation anywhere.

### Q4. A tribute or portrait film premiered to a living subject who did not know it existed

**Result: none found.** I did not find a documented film premiere of this kind, and the room is still unobserved. Because search was unavailable this is "not found", not "does not exist". The nearest documented cases are **television**: *This Is Your Life*, where the subject was ambushed on air [83][84]. They are not a film premiere before a mixed public room, and most behind-the-scenes accounts come from the subjects, spouses or autobiographies and are not independently verified [83].

**What happened to unaware subjects (documented; television).**
- **Refusal at the moment of reveal.** Danny Blanchflower (UK, 6 Feb 1961): told "Tonight, Danny Blanchflower, This Is Your Life", he replied "Oh no it's not" and walked away; the live broadcast switched to a prepared episode; he said he never regretted it [83]. Angie Dickinson (US, 1993): lured under a pretext, surprised by the host, refused and walked out; she later said the main reason was that "she didn't look good crying" [84]. Maureen Lipman had agreed in advance with her agent and husband that she would refuse if approached, and said her refusal upset her mother more than anything else about her career [83].
- **Hurt at being kept in the dark by someone close.** Ronnie Barker wrote in his 1988 autobiography that his wife's secrecy upset him and that he briefly suspected an affair; after he confronted her the edition was cancelled and later attempts blocked [83].
- **Insider disagreement.** In 1982 Peter Davison's then-wife objected to a planned guest [83].
- **Leaks.** The format's rule was that the edition is cancelled if the subject learned beforehand [83]; in the 1971 to 72 US revival some stations "gave away the surprise elements in ads and promos" [84]. A planned 1996 UK edition was reportedly dropped after researchers contacted a son [83] (the accompanying allegations are unverified).
- **The subject already knew.** Vincent Price's 1973 episode was planned with his wife and, per his daughter, he "agreed to act surprised" [84].
- **Content the subject would not want aired.** *Time* said the show ignored that Elizabeth Hahn's husband had sued for divorce (1960); critics called it "the most sickeningly sentimental show on the air" and a "maudlin invasion of privacy" [84]. The US page describes no general policy of consulting family or getting consent [84].
- Pages checked with nothing relevant: Kennedy Center Honors (honorees get advance notice of the honour; nothing on tribute content) and Surprise party (practice only, no research on effects) [85].

**What this adds to the run sheet (inference, for the maker).** The documented failure modes are refusal, hurt about secrecy, leaks through promotion, and content she would not choose to have aired. The film itself is not the usual cause; the **ambush** is. Four run-sheet items follow: (1) an **off-ramp** that costs her nothing in public (an aisle seat, a quiet room, a pre-agreed way to stop or shorten the screening, no camera trained on her face); (2) a **leak control** for a "mostly public" event, since invitations and listings are the likeliest vector (keep the public wording generic); (3) a **decision rule if she finds out early**: proceed, convert to a shared project, or cancel, decided in advance by the maker; (4) the **hard-chapter default-out rule** applied to anything she could not have chosen to show in front of strangers. Only the room itself can answer how she reacts; the run sheet should plan for refusal as a normal outcome, not an edge case.

### Q1. Does the US Stored Communications Act (18 U.S.C. 2701 to 2712) reach a recipient who plays a voicemail or voice note she was sent? (Owner: theatrical-delivery)

**Short answer.** On the statutory text I read, **probably not: the Act is aimed at unauthorised access to the system and at what service providers may disclose, not at a recipient who plays her own message.** The real exposure for this project is **how a file is obtained**, not how it is played. I could not read any case law, so the edges are UNVERIFIED. All of this concerns US federal law only.

| Provision | What the text says [96][97][98][99][100][101] | Bearing on a recipient playing a received message (inference unless stated) |
|---|---|---|
| §2701(a) offence | Whoever "intentionally accesses without authorization a facility through which an electronic communication service is provided", or "intentionally exceeds an authorization to access that facility", and thereby obtains a wire or electronic communication "while it is in electronic storage in such system" [96] | The offence is about **getting into the facility**. Playing a message already in your hands is not access to the facility |
| §2701(c) exceptions | The offence does not apply to conduct authorised "(1) by the person or entity providing a wire or electronic communications service; (2) by a user of that service with respect to a communication of or intended for that user" [96] | The person a message was left **for** is the user who can authorise access to it. A relative who hands over or forwards a voicemail left for them is within (c)(2) on this reading |
| §2701(b) penalties | Up to 5 years (first offence) where the act is for commercial advantage, malicious destruction or damage, private commercial gain, or (per the fetch summary) in furtherance of a criminal or tortious act; otherwise up to 1 year for a first offence [96] | Small for a first, non-commercial act, but it is a criminal statute |
| §2702(a), (b)(3) disclosure | "a person or entity providing an electronic communication service to the public shall not knowingly divulge" contents; a provider may divulge "with the lawful consent of the originator or an addressee or intended recipient" [97] | The duty binds **providers** (a carrier, Apple, Google, Meta). Nothing I read puts it on an individual who received the message. The tool's own reading of its excerpt was the same: it "restricts providers, not individual recipients", though the excerpt "does not address" individuals directly [97] |

| §2510(17) "electronic storage" | "(A) any temporary, intermediate storage of a wire or electronic communication incidental to the electronic transmission thereof; and (B) any storage of such communication by an electronic communication service for purposes of backup protection" [98] | A copy exported to the recipient's own device or file is not obviously "electronic storage" in this sense. | §2510(1) "wire communication" | The text no longer includes "any electronic storage of such communication"; the editorial notes record that Pub. **What this changes.**
1. **Playing a voicemail or voice note that was left for the maker (or handed over by the person it was left for) does not engage the SCA on this reading.** The earlier Q2 conclusion stands: the live constraints are privacy tort, state recording law, publicity and data-protection rules, and above all **her** consent. The wiretap clauses [72] stay relevant only if the recording was itself intercepted.
2. **The SCA risk is acquisition.** Logging into another person's carrier voicemail, cloud backup, message account or phone **without that person's authorisation** is the conduct §2701(a) describes, and civil damages start at $1,000 [96][100]. That applies to **her** accounts and to relatives' accounts alike. It also happens to be the likeliest way to tip her off.
3. **New inventory field and a hard rule for the covert-gathering run sheet.** Add a fifth field to the found-voice inventory (alongside addressee, knowing recording, sensitivity, other voices): **(e) how the file reached the maker**: `handed-over-by-holder`, `forwarded-by-recipient`, `maker's-own-device`, or `pulled-from-an-account` (the last is **OUT**). Rule: files come only from the person who holds them, who chooses to hand them over; the maker never signs in to an account that is not the maker's own.
4. A holder's permission to hand over a message does not give the **speaker's** permission to play it to strangers. That is the round-1 Q2 question and it stays open (her consent is the only clean one).

**Unverified.** Case law on "electronic storage" and "authorization"; whether a messaging-app voice note stored on the app's servers is treated differently from a carrier voicemail; the position of any individual US state. A lawyer will know the circuit decisions; I could not open them (CRS report 403, Justia 403, DOJ manual 401/404).
