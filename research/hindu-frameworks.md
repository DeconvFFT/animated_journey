## Summary

1. **The sixteen samskaras are a menu, not a script.** The Grhyasutras list 12 to 18 and the Gautama Dharmasutra 40 [28]; the "sixteen" is one tradition's count. For a woman the only Vedic samskara is marriage; Manu 2.66 says her other rites are done "without sacred incantations" and the Manu verse that follows, as summarised by a scholarly essay, makes marriage her equivalent of upanayana [30][31]. So no thread ceremony, no invented naming rite: a marker exists only where a photo, object or witness exists (Principle 4). The `evidence_id` column stays empty until inventory v1 (Sun 13 Dec).
2. **The rites that are hers cluster in three places.** As a child (naming, first rice, ear-piercing, first letters), as a young woman (the southern puberty rite, then marriage) and as a mother (the pregnancy rite and every rite she arranged for her children). In the traditions I could read, the *mother often does not officiate* at her child's rites (in Bengal the maternal uncle feeds the first rice, in Kerala the father, an uncle or grandfather, in Nepal the mother does) [32], so the marker is her hands preparing or holding, not "she performed".
3. **Puberty rite and widowhood markers go on the one-key-three-guards sheet as HOLD by default.** The half-saree and manjal neerattu vizha are real, celebrated milestones, and also the thing a girl later says exposed her ("nobody asked what I wanted") [36][37]. A public cut may show "her first sari" as an object only if the maker, as proxy, decides she would tell it herself. The rule is written in Finding 2 and Template G, now, not on the day.
4. **Ashrama: she is grihastha, and grihastha is the load-bearing stage.** Manu 3.77 says every other order depends on the householder as living creatures depend on air [30]; the Tamil moral text *Konrai Vendan* says no good dharma exists apart from home life [50]. Manu's vanaprastha criterion is wrinkles, grey hair and a grandchild, and 6.3 then says "leaving the wife with the sons" [30]; Manu 5.148 says a woman should not seek independence [30]. Those are the lines a public room hears as diminishing: the film imports none of them. Her mother-standing comes from the Taittiriya Upanishad (1.11.2) [40], with Manu 2.145 only as background.
5. **Rasa: the memory list was right on seven of nine, ambiguous on one (śṛṅgāra) and wrong on one (vīra, not gold).** The Natyashastra's colour verse (6.42 to 43, Ghosh's translation) gives Erotic light green (*śyāma*, dusky), Comic white, Pathetic ash-coloured (*kapota*), Furious red, Heroic light orange (*gaura*, not gold), Terrible black, Odious blue, Marvellous yellow [18]. Other sources translate *śyāma* and *gaura* differently [19][20], so colour names are the film's choice anchored to the Sanskrit term, not "what the Natyashastra says". Śānta is not in the main text; Ghosh footnotes it as a later addition and calls one edition's passage spurious [18].
6. **Vatsalya is the film's native key, borrowed honestly.** Rupa Goswami's scheme has five primary rasas (śānta, dāsya, sakhya, vātsalya, madhura) and seven secondary [21]. In that scheme vātsalya is the devotee's *parental* stance toward a child-God (Yashoda and Krishna). The film uses the tradition's own name for a mother's love, and says in the notes that it is borrowing the word, not claiming her as a devotional type.
7. **Dates are verified for New Delhi and Chennai, and checked for Toronto, London and New York.** Every brief lead was checked [3]. Corrections: Vaikuntha Ekadashi is Sun 20 Dec (not end December); Magha Purnima is Sat 20 Feb (not 21); Lohri is Thu 14 Jan on Drik (other sites say Wed 13) [3][17]. A Delhi date is a day off elsewhere in at least four places: Karva Chauth in San Jose, Pongal in London, the Diwali cycle in Toronto and London (three of five days), and Dev Uthani Ekadashi (twin Smartha and Vaishnava days) [3][7][9][11][14].
8. **Seven collisions with the register matter.** Diwali Fri 6 to Wed 11 Nov (before P-LOCK); Chhath Fri 13 to Mon 16 Nov (P-LOCK day is Kharna, G-READY is the main day) [2][15]; Dev Uthani and Tulsi Vivah Fri 20 and Sat 21 Nov; Sankranti and Pongal Thu 14 to Sun 17 Jan (the Fri 15 Jan hard deadline is Pongal day, G-MID is Kaanum Pongal) [3][4][5]; picture lock Sat 6 Feb is Mauni Amavasya [3]; Vasant Panchami Thu 11 Feb [8]; and a new find: **the her-turn conversation, Sat 6 Mar 2027, is Maha Shivaratri** [12].
9. **27 Feb 2027 is unremarkable in every city checked.** Krishna Paksha Saptami until 21:54 IST, nakshatra Vishakha then Anuradha, month Magha (amanta) or Phalguna (purnimanta); only minor observances (Shabari Jayanti; the monthly Krishna Janmashtami fast) [6]. The weekend does sit between Yashoda Jayanti (Fri 26), Shabari Jayanti (Sat 27) and Janaki Jayanti (Sun 28) in the Delhi listing [3]: a calendar fact for the maker only, never a reason printed on screen.
10. **The invocation shortlist has seven text-verified items, three disputed, and two corrections to the brief.** The Annapurna Stotra's closing verse reads *mātā me pārvatī devī* ("my mother is the goddess Parvati") in the Complete Works text, not *mātā ca*; and the encoders note that "the last three verses" (on a 12-verse page, verses 10 to 12) come from the Annada Kalpa Tantra and are inserted after verse 10 in some printed editions, so the Shankara attribution is mixed [43]. *Janani janmabhumi* is absent from the Critical Edition (per Wikipedia, not checked against Baroda) and carries a political charge; *sarve bhavantu sukhinah* has no secure source; *tvameva mata* is the Pandava Gita, spoken by Gandhari, and addresses God as mother, which makes it wrong for a card honouring a human mother [46][47][48].
11. **Cap the sermon.** Default two invocation cards (a title epigraph after the first true sound, and one end card); never more than five; far under W-35's 10%. Print the Sanskrit plus the maker's own plain-English line: published translations (Olivelle, Madhavananda, Ghosh) are presumed in copyright (UNVERIFIED, not legal advice), and a third-party transliteration page is not an edition. Avoid mortality lines (*mṛtyor mā amṛtaṃ gamaya*, Gita 2.22) at the open and the end: a mixed room that does not know her can misread a film for a living woman as a memorial.
12. **Objects.** Nineteen candidates are tabled with meaning, daily-hand status and a never-show rule. The most contested in public is the thali/mangalsutra (a 2022 Madras High Court remark made it a news item; Herwad's 2022 ban on removing it as a widowhood rite) [61][62]. Kerala's *minnu* appears in one source as a *Christian* Keralite bride's pendant, so it is flagged out for a Hindu Kerala household [62].
13. **Hard chapters in Hindu terms: show what is kept, never the event.** The garlanded photograph, the lamp, the annual shraddha meal and the food put out for the crows are present-tense and hers if she keeps them; the pyre, the ashes and their immersion, and the mourning impurity are the family's [59][60]. Illness is a vrat kept for someone and a thread tied on a wrist, not a hospital. No card preaches moksha. Poonthanam's *Jnanappana* (a father's grief at his infant son's death, held by devotion to the child Krishna) is the tradition's own model of consolation without a lesson [52].
14. **Flags under D-28.** Thirukkural's author is claimed by Jain, Hindu, Buddhist, Shaiva and secular readings and has been politicised: default out [54]. Shyama Sangeet has Tagore (Brahmo) and Nazrul (Muslim) among its composers: restrict to Ramprasad and the Shakta line [53]. Salabega, the best-loved Odia Jagannath bhajan-writer, was Muslim by birth [54]. Ragamala and Kangra are Hindu in subject but Mughal and Deccani in lineage; Oudh Ragamalas added Judeo-Christian imagery [22][24]. Drik's own calendar lists Guru Nanak Jayanti (24 Nov), Guru Gobind Singh Jayanti (15 Jan), Buddha Jayanti (20 Oct) and Jain observances beside Hindu ones: none is a motif here [3].
15. **Everything is keyed on O-36 to O-41.** Eight fillable templates (A to H) light up when the maker answers: marker table, rasa sheets, invocation shortlist, festival table, object table, six region/sampradaya/deity/festival/hands/song decision tables, the hard-chapter addendum and a 36-question "make it hers" list. The questions need only the maker's own knowledge or a look around the house, and would tip nobody off if a note were found.

## Findings by question

### 1. Which samskaras are performed for a girl and a woman, under what names, which are not, and which did she perform for her children?

**How many and which.** The "sixteen" is one count among several: the Grhyasutras give between 12 and 18 and the Gautama Dharmasutra lists 40 [28]. The Himalayan Academy's *What Is Hinduism?* describes fourteen: seven primary personal rites (Anna Prasana, Karnavedha, Chudakarana, Vidyarambha, Upanayana, Vivaha, Antyesti) and seven others (Keshanta, Ritu Kala, Samavartana, Nishchitartha, Punsavana, Simantonnayana, Jatakarma) [29]. The film therefore never says "her sixteen samskaras"; it asks which rites left a photograph or object.

**What the texts say about women.** Manu 2.66: "for women these purificatory rites for the body should be performed entirely without sacred incantations (mantras), at the appropriate time and in due order" (Devanagari and IAST read at enjoylearningsanskrit.com, which names no translator) [30]. Hari Ravikumar's essay in *Prekshaa* (20 Nov 2018) summarises the next verse, 2.67, as making marriage the one Vedic samskara for women, and distinguishes *brahmavadinis* (women who underwent upanayana and studied) from *sadyovadhus* (a bare upanayana shortly before the wedding), noting that the practice had probably lapsed by Manu's time [31]. Wikipedia says women who did not undergo upanayana had it at their wedding [28]. I could not open a source for the claim that some reform movements (for example the Arya Samaj) give girls the thread: UNVERIFIED. If a thread-wearing photograph of her exists, it is evidence, not an assumption.

### 2. The puberty rite for a public cut: what Principle 5 and D-04 protect, and the handling rule

**What it is.** In South India (and in Assam as *tuloni biya*) the rite is a real, celebrated milestone: a half-saree first, then, in the second part of the ceremony, a first full sari given by her maternal uncle; she wears half-saris until marriage [35]. In Tamil Nadu it is sometimes a large public event with printed invitations, a hall, banners with the girl's photograph, professional photographers, the girl dressed like a bride and blessed to be a *dheerga sumangali*, a long-married woman [36].

**Two honest readings.** A 2018 letter in the *Indian Journal of Endocrinology and Metabolism* argues the function can protect a girl from remarks and, paradoxically, give a modicum of privacy; many families now hold it quietly with close relatives [37]. A 2023 *News Minute* piece quotes women who felt exposed and displayed ("Nobody comforted me or asked what I wanted"; "Any form of ceremony is an assertion of power") and notes the event signals marriageability [36]. Both are true of different families. That is exactly why it is the maker's call as her proxy and not the team's.

**Handling rule (default on the one-key-three-guards sheet, D-04), written now:**

The widowhood customs are visibly changing: Herwad village in Kolhapur district (Maharashtra) passed a gram panchayat resolution in May 2022 banning them, and a state notification on 17 May 2022 asked all gram panchayats to follow [61, search result only]. A public room cannot tell which stance a family holds, so the film does not take one on her behalf.

### 3. The 50+ mother in the ashrama scheme, her standing, avoiding pativrata and stridharma framings, and the 60th-birthday rite

**The scheme as the sources describe it.** Britannica defines an ashrama as one of four stages a Hindu ideally passes through (student, householder, forest-dweller, renouncer), and the householder stage is marked by marriage, children and supporting priests and holy men [39, search result only: Britannica returned HTTP 403 when opened]. Patrick Olivelle's *The Āśrama System* (OUP, 1993; AAR award, 1994) argues the system is primarily a theological construct, to be kept distinct from the social institutions it describes [39, search result only]. So "she is in stage X" is a framing choice, not a fact about her.

**What the texts give us for the 50+ mother.**
- *Grihastha is load-bearing.* Manu 3.77: "Just as all living beings depend on air, so all other stages of life depend on the householder" (paraphrase of the translation on the page) [30]. The Tamil moral text *Konrai Vendan* (credited to Avvaiyar; Tamil Wiki dates it to the 12th century and does not say which Avvaiyar) has: "இல்லறம் அல்லது நல் அறம் அன்று" (*illaṟam allatu nal aṟam aṉṟu*), roughly "apart from home life there is no good dharma" (my gloss) [50].
- *The vanaprastha criterion is not an age.* Manu 6.2: when the householder sees "wrinkles and gray hair on himself, and his child's child", he may resort to the forest [30]. Whether she is at that point is the maker's knowledge, not the text's. And Manu 6.3, "leaving the wife with the sons, one should go to the forest, or perhaps with her" [30], is exactly the framing the film must not import.
- *Framing for the film (inference, mine):* **grihastha, still holding the house up, with the world widening around her.** Not "retired", not "in the third stage". No card names a stage.

**Her standing as a mother.**
- Taittiriya Upanishad 1.11.2: **mātṛdevo bhava**, the first of four injunctions (mother, father, teacher, guest) in a teacher's address to a departing student [40]; a Mother's Day post on anantrambachan.com (author not confirmed from the snippet) reads *deva* from the root *div*, "to shine", one who deserves reverence, and warns against "Mother is God" as the only rendering [40, search result only].
- Manu 2.145: a teacher is more venerable than ten ordinary preceptors, a father than a hundred teachers, "a mother surpasses a thousand fathers in honour" (paraphrased from the page) [30]. One 19th-century reading treats the verse as part of a debate resolved in the next verses (search result only, UNVERIFIED) [30]. **Do not quote Manu on screen**: it is a law code whose other verses (below) are the diminishing ones. The Upanishad is the source of choice.
- Manu 3.56, *yatra nāryas tu pūjyante ramante tatra devatāḥ*, is the verse most often quoted in praise of women [30]; it is one verse in a text that elsewhere says what 5.148 says. Same rule: not on screen.

**What to avoid and why.**
- Manu 5.148: "In childhood she should remain under the protection of her father, in youth under her husband, and after her husband's death, under her sons. A woman should not seek independence" [30]. A mixed public room hears this as diminishing her.
- Karva Chauth is, in Drik's own words, "observed by married women for the long life of their husband" [9]. If she keeps it, the sieve, the moon and the thali are hers; the caption "for her husband" is not, and a widowed, separated or unmarried-by-choice reading must never be forced on her.
- Words to keep off cards as her definition: *pativrata, sati, sumangali, ardhangini, dharmapatni*. Words to keep: her name, what she does, keeps, lights, teaches. **Annapurna and Lakshmi are the presiding deities of a room and a house, never titles for her** (W-34's kitchen set is the case in point).

**Does a 60th-birthday rite matter?** *Shashtyabdapurti* (also *shashthipurti*) marks the completion of sixty years; it is a two-day event with a *shanti* part (including Ugraratha Shanti) and a *kranti* part centred on a re-enacted wedding and a reaffirmation of marriage [38]. It is therefore a **couple's** rite, usually organised by the children; sources disagree on who performs it and one popular source calls it *Sadabhishekam* in some regions while others place *Sathabhishekam* at 80, *Bhimaratha Shanti* at 70 (*Ugraratha Shanti* is part of the sixtieth-year rite on the Wikipedia page [38]; I found no source for a rite at 55) [38, secondary and search result only]. Her age is unknown, so it matters only if her 60th year is 2026 or 2027, and only if she is married and the family keeps it (O-45). It is a marker row (Template A) with `kept_by_her` blank. The maker may notice, privately, that the premiere is a child doing for a parent what this rite has children do for parents; that stays out of the film (Principle 4: do not invent a connection).

### 4. The nine rasas, colours and deities, vatsalya and bhakti, how artists use them, and the life-film rule

**The primary text (Ghosh's translation of the Natyashastra, vol. 1, Asiatic Society, 1951; read from the archive.org OCR, verse numbers as in Ghosh) [18].**
- 6.15: "The eight Sentiments recognised in drama are: Erotic, Comic, Pathetic, Furious, Heroic, Terrible, Odious and Marvellous." 6.17: the eight Dominant States are love, mirth, sorrow, anger, energy, terror, disgust and astonishment. Footnote 15: "The later writers on Skt. poetics add one more (śānta)" (quotations here are from an OCR text, lightly corrected where the OCR garbled a word) [18].
- 6.42 to 43: "The Erotic Sentiment is light green (śyāma), the Comic white, the Pathetic ash-coloured (kapota), the Furious red, the Heroic light orange (gaura), the Terrible black, the Odious blue and the Marvellous yellow." 6.44 to 45: Viṣṇu is the god of the Erotic, the Pramathas of the Comic, Rudra of the Furious, Yama of the Pathetic, Śiva (Mahākāla) of the Odious, Yama (Kāla) of the Terrible, Indra of the Heroic and Brahmā of the Marvellous [18].
- Ghosh's note to the end of chapter 6: Benares (B) "adds here a spurious passage on śānta rasa" [18].

**Vatsalya and bhakti.** Rupa Goswami's *Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu* (English by Tridandi Hrydaya Bon Maharaj, hosted by the Bhaktivedanta Memorial Library) says rasa "can take twelve different forms, five primaries and seven secondary": the primaries are śānta (tranquillity), dāsya (servitude), sakhya (friendship), **vātsalya (parental tenderness toward the Lord)** and madhura (conjugal love); the secondaries are hāsya, karuṇa, raudra, vīra, bhayānaka, bībhatsa and adbhuta [21]. A claim that Viśvanātha's *Sāhityadarpaṇa* makes vātsalya a tenth rasa could not be confirmed: UNVERIFIED. **Honesty note for the film:** in the bhakti scheme the *devotee* is the parent and God the child; the poets (Surdas on Yashoda and Krishna, Purandara Dasa's *Jagadoddharana*, Poonthanam) drew on human parenting [52][54]. The film uses vātsalya as the tradition's own name for a mother's love and says it is borrowing the word. Vātsalya has no Natyashastra colour; it is not in that scheme [18]. The film samples its accent from the thing she made (O-40), never from a colour table.

**How artists use rasa (what I could source).**
- *Dance and theatre:* rasa theory underpins Bharatanatyam, Kathakali, Kathak, Kuchipudi, Odissi, Manipuri and Kudiyattam; expressing rasa is *rasa-abhinaya* [20].
- *Painting:* a Ragamala is a "garland of ragas" in miniature, each painting capturing a mood, linked to a season, a time of day and a deity (Raga Bhairava as Shiva); the bhakti movement shifted the imagery from deities to human figures in landscapes [22][23]. Kangra painting's focal theme is *śṛṅgāra* rasa driven by bhakti, with Radha-Krishna and Jayadeva's *Gita Govinda* as sources [24]; the Met's Tehri Garhwal *Gita Govinda* folios are dated about 1775 to 80 (search result only) [25]. **Flags (D-28):** Ragamala sets were made in Rajput, Pahari, Deccani and Mughal courts and the Oudh sets of the early 1800s added Judeo-Christian imagery [22]; Kangra's founding painters were Kashmiri artists "trained in the Mughal painting style" [24]. Both are Hindu in subject and mixed in lineage: use as an *idea* (mood, time, season), not as the film's visual style, unless the maker rules (this is the visual tracks' call).
- *Cinema:* *Navarasa* (Netflix, 6 Aug 2021) is a Tamil anthology created by Mani Ratnam in nine films, one rasa each, directed by nine directors, made to raise funds for FEFSI workers [26]; scholarship treats Satyajit Ray's *Apu Trilogy* and *Jalsaghar* through rasa (a chapter in *The Cinema of Satyajit Ray*, CUP 2000; a 2024 Newcastle thesis; an Edinburgh thesis on rasa-based criticism) [27, search result only]. That a recently bereaved household may keep *no* festivals for a longer period (some families skip them for a year) is UNVERIFIED general knowledge; it goes into O-39 as "any festival she is not keeping because of a loss".

**Obituary misread.** The premiere is for a living woman among strangers. A garland on a photograph, *mṛtyor mā amṛtaṃ gamaya*, Gita 2.22 or *oṃ śāntiḥ* at the end can make the room think she has died (film-story Template 5 already warns the opening must not feel like an obituary). The mitigations: a garlanded photograph, if shown at all, is of the person who died, labelled by relation, and is never her; the open-chapter card in her name ("To be told by her") ends the film in the present tense (W-36); the end card is not the shanti formula if the room could misread it.
