## Summary

1. **The ethical line can be stated as a test, and research ethics already uses it.** One university IRB policy, reproducing the American Psychological Association's ethics standards, separates "minor deception" (information withheld, but what you did say is generally accurate) from misleading or untrue statements, and asks: would participants decline if they knew what was withheld, could it upset them, and will they be debriefed? [13] Mapped to this project: omission is acceptable, untruths and secret recording are not; make every pretext *real* (you actually do the photo-labelling afternoon or the family-stories evening and she gets the result); never lie when she sincerely asks (the "no-lie floor"); debrief at the reveal and let her withdraw her words [13][8].
2. **There are three truthful windows for asking her directly**, and none needs a false reason: labelling and dating old photos with her (a real, bounded task), a genuine family-history conversation that also covers her parents and grandparents, and an announced family-stories recording at a holiday table where everyone, including her, knows the recorder is on [9][10][11]. Everything else about her life comes from other people and from objects.
3. **Kits are scene-based, short, and per relationship.** StoryCorps suggests 6 to 8 questions in a roughly 40-minute session [1][5]; Smithsonian folklorists warn that broad prompts ("tell me about your childhood") produce names and dates, and recommend props such as albums and letters, and open-ended follow-ups [9]. T1 gives 5 to 8 prompts for each of seven relationships, a 20 to 30 minute shape, and one "only you" ask.
4. **Remote voice collection works best when the contributor records themselves.** The Oral History Association's remote resource says audio-only is often the least stressful option and can give higher-quality files, phone-based recording is "likely the most secure remote option", earbuds with mics beat laptop mics, keep originals and avoid re-compressing [6]. iPhone Voice Memos and the Pixel Recorder both export an `.m4a` file [16][17]. Do **not** default to StoryCorps Connect: it needs an archive account, cannot be paused, and publishes to the archive, and a Library of Congress copy persists whatever the privacy setting [1][2][3][4].
5. **Recording law is a checklist, not a conclusion.** US federal law is one-party consent; about 11 US states require all-party consent; some states differ between in-person and phone conversations (Missouri and Oregon strict in person, Connecticut and Nevada strict by phone); the guide itself says it is not legal advice [14]. This project's rule is stricter than any of them: explicit spoken consent every time, and no recording of her without her knowledge and a spoken yes. The only planned cases are an announced recorder at a family-stories evening (Card E) and a wide shot of the room at the reveal, if she agrees in the quiet word. Apple's built-in call recording is consent-by-design: both parties hear a repeating audio notice, and Apple tells users to get explicit consent first [15].
6. **Photograph album pages in place before any print is removed.** Old magnetic and sticky albums can bond to prints, so removal tears them [55]. Vendor figures put phone scanning at about 15 s per photo and flatbed at 30 to 60 s plus handling [52]; a sheet-fed FastFoto is rated about 1 photo per second at 300 dpi but Epson publishes no 600 dpi speed and it takes loose prints only [54]. My estimate for six albums is 3 to 5 hours of phone work (inference, T4).
7. **Lab turnaround runs from one to ten weeks and holiday cut-offs are unpublished.** Listed 2026 figures: about 1 week (small orders, one vendor), 2 to 3 weeks (Capture, self-reported), 3 to 4 weeks (ScanCafe, ScanDigital), 4 to 6 weeks (Legacybox, Southtree), about 6 weeks (Legacy Digital), Pro8mm 5 to 7 business days scheduled but 4 to 8 weeks on its home-movie discount queue [43][44][45][46][47][48]. Working back from the week-15 gate (Sun 2027-01-24) with two weeks of transit and two of holiday margin (my assumption): the ship-by date for a slow lab is **Fri 2026-11-13**, for a mid lab **Fri 2026-11-27**, and for a fast lab **Fri 2026-12-18**. Ship-by is the Friday the parcel leaves; log the lab's confirmed receipt date when the lab gives it. Send one pilot tape by **Fri 2026-10-30**. The covert catch: a mail-in lab keeps the item away from her house for 4 to 10 weeks, so only send items she will not miss.
8. **Real slips are mundane and device-shaped.** Alexa+ read a parent's recent Amazon orders aloud when a child asked [29]; Google and Apple both auto-create a shared Family calendar [31][32]; iCloud Shared Library lets every participant add, edit and delete and can auto-share from the camera [18]; Google partner sharing shares photos as they are backed up [19]; Dropbox emails the requester on every upload [34]; and the human classics (a hidden ring found while cleaning the oven, brothers who vanished, a last-minute change of plan) [35][36].
9. **Contributor brief: one channel, no new websites for older relatives.** A February 2026 first-hand account concluded "don't ask seniors to use a website they don't already use" [37]; an institutional brief for a surprise tribute asked for under 20 seconds, landscape, quiet room, eye level, photos with a short description, or a few written sentences [39]. T2 is a full template with a version for people who dislike being recorded.
10. **Hard chapters: a proxy helps, but proxies are wrong about a third of the time.** A systematic review of 16 studies found surrogates predicted patients' preferences with 68% accuracy (medical hypotheticals, so an analogy only), and neither choosing the surrogate nor prior discussion helped [60] (Pallimed notes that the discussion finding rests on two uncontrolled trials). So use the "what would *she* choose" standard (substituted judgment [61]), two keys not one, a default of out or private when unsure, and the reveal as the first consent point with a dated veto window [8].
11. **Dignity techniques have convergent practitioner support.** Describe a difficult parent by trait, not incident, pair each honest sentence with warmth, hint rather than detail, and do not invent a sun [56]; life-story work says the person decides what goes in and who sees it now and later [58]; the documentary field's rule is protect the vulnerable and do not leave them worse off [62]; do not synthesise a voice [63]; animated photos of the dead split people between moved and unsettled (press, anecdote) [64].
12. **Estranged relatives: ask the proxy, never ambush, never stage a reconciliation.** The cautionary precedent is in story.md ("This Is Your Life" and the reported Mullard case). Etiquette columnists treat the guest list as the honoree's or host's call [74]; a family-history society code asks for specific consent from living people before information about them is shared further [78].
13. **Reveal design (mostly inference, because no experiments exist).** Shared cut about 6 to 10 minutes, hard cap 12; private cut 20 to 30. Practitioner guidance for tribute videos clusters at 3 to 8 minutes, maximum 10 [67][68]. About one in three US adults aged 65 to 74 and nearly half of those over 75 have hearing loss [66], so burn in captions, mix speech forward and seat grandparents near the speakers. Give her a quiet word 10 to 20 minutes before, a stop signal, her choice of seat and companion, no speech, camera on the room not her face unless she says yes [42], and a 15-minute pause before the party resumes. Windows can switch Do Not Disturb on automatically when duplicating the display, which suits a laptop-to-TV reveal [24].
14. **The calendar has cultural collisions.** Feb 27, 2027 falls inside Ramadan on the UAE Astronomy Centre's projection (start about Feb 8, dependent on moon sighting) [72]; Lunar New Year is Feb 6 [73]. These are questions for the maker's checklist, not assumptions about this family.
15. **The gift after the gift is where Principle 5 closes.** Keep two copies on different media and make new copies at least every five years [70]; prefer static QR codes to subscription "dynamic" ones [71]; 38% of webpages that existed in 2013 were gone by 2023 [69], so a private web link is a convenience copy, not the archive. Offer her the Oral History Association's review menu (delete, restrict, redact, add clarifications, correct mistakes, close until a date) [8], hold a recorded conversation about a week after the reveal, keep a corrections ledger, and publish "her version".

## Findings by question

### Q1. Per relationship: which prompts draw out texture, how to ask for "the one story only that person knows", and how to collect it remotely as a usable recording

**Design logic (sources, then my adaptation).**
- *Scenes, not tributes.* Smithsonian folklorists advise against broad prompts such as "tell me about your childhood" (answers become names and dates) and recommend starting from a topic the person has already talked about, using evocative open-ended questions and props such as letters, albums and home movies, and letting tangents run [9]. FamilySearch's relative-interview tips agree: avoid yes/no questions, prepare a few but build on what you hear, and expect trust to take several visits [10]. StoryCorps keeps its lists open ("use the ones you like and … come up with your own") and groups them by relationship, including Friends or Colleagues, Parents, Grandparents, Growing Up and Work; it has no separate sibling list (siblings sit under Growing Up) [5]. I took those relationship groupings and rebuilt the prompts around *scene, sense, object, saying, place* using the texture codes from story.md (R routine, S sense, O object, P people, W words, L place, M money/work, B body, X one-time scene). The prompts are new wording and are my inference; the principles are sourced.
- *Length and shape.* StoryCorps' own guidance is 6 to 8 questions over about 40 minutes [1]; story.md protocol says 45 to 60 minutes with a break, 30 to 45 for frail narrators. The brief asks for 20 to 30 minutes, which fits a *covert-kit* interview with a relative who is giving a favour (inference); T1 times each part.
- *Confirm they want to be recorded.* Baylor's Institute for Oral History puts this first: make sure the relative actually wants their story recorded, because a reluctant relative derails the project [12]. St. Louis County Library's family guide adds: do not surprise the narrator with unexpected topics, make them a partner in choosing topics, do not mislead them, discuss topics they want to avoid, and offer a chance to review [11].
- *Group beats solo for some stories.* The Smithsonian guide says group settings are especially productive because one story leads to another and relatives correct each other; if no natural gathering occurs, hosting one is acceptable ("induced" setting) [9]. Use this for siblings and old friends, with the recorder announced to everyone (T7, card E).
- *Interview her parents separately from each other.* A commercial documentary-course page makes the same point for life documentaries (each parent has their own perspective; decide the audience before you start because it shapes how openly they speak; ninety minutes is a ceiling; sessions are tiring for elderly parents) [40]. Commercial source, anecdote.

**Relationship table (what each holds, risk, best channel).** Prompts are in T1.

**"The one story only that person knows."** Practitioners do not publish a script for this phrase; this is my construction (inference) from the sources above plus the drawer test (story.md Q7):
1. Ask once, plainly, with a time-box: "You were the only person in the room for at least one story about [NAME]. Which one is yours?"
2. If they say "I can't think of one", move to a ladder rather than repeating: "When were you alone with her?" then "Where were you?" then "What happened next?" (scene, place, sequence).
3. Close the loop with two questions that do the shared-cut sorting at the source: "Would she tell this one herself, to a room?" and "Is there a version you would be happy for the whole family to hear?" Record the answers; they feed the T6 decision sheet.
4. Always give an exit: "If this one is better kept between us, say so and I will keep it out." (story.md Recipe 4 and Q7: bottom-drawer material is not solicited and not used.)

**Recording her (Summary 5).** None of these channels records her unless she knows and says yes on the spot. The only planned cases are an announced recorder at a family-stories evening (Card E) and a wide shot of the room at the reveal, if she agrees in the quiet word.

**Phone placement (one paragraph a contributor can follow).** Sit in a quiet room with soft things in it (sofa, curtains), away from the fridge, clock and TV. Put the phone flat on a folded towel or cushion on a table, with the bottom edge (where the main microphone usually is) pointing at your mouth, about a foot away. An institutional brief for a tribute video uses the same "about a foot from your face" instruction [39]; story.md Q8 says 6 to 12 inches, 5 to 6 inches for one speaker. Switch on Do Not Disturb so a call does not cut in, say a spoken test sentence, play it back once. Then record.

**One-line consent request for using the voice** (full wording in T1, "Consent card"):
"May I use your recorded voice in a private film and keepsake for [NAME], shown to family and given to her, with you able to skip questions, ask me to stop, and have anything taken out afterwards?"
This follows the OHA four-point process (consent before the interview, goals and risks, a proviso on how it can be used, a chance to review) and its warning not to promise what you cannot keep [8]; spoken consent recorded at the start is acceptable where signatures are not feasible [8].

### Q2. What can the maker ask her directly without raising suspicion, under which truthful pretexts, and what do practitioners and ethicists say about pretexts, secret recording and the line between a surprise and a deception she would resent?

**What practitioners say about honesty with a narrator.** The OHA ethics statement requires the interviewer to communicate the project's goals and risks and the proviso about future use before recording, and to avoid promises they cannot keep [8]. St. Louis County Library's family guide says not to mislead narrators and to offer a chance to review [11]. FamilySearch: explain your purpose and what to expect [10]. None of these sources contemplates a surprise; their default is full disclosure. That is why this project borrows the *research-ethics* rule for incomplete disclosure.

**The research-ethics rule, adapted.** The Iowa State IRB document (reproducing APA Standards 8.07 and 8.08) defines deception as intentionally misleading or withholding information, and separates:
- *Minor deception*: information is withheld or omitted, and what was described is "generally accurate, but some details are withheld".
- *Not minor*: participants are "intentionally misled, tricked or told something untrue", or the deception might upset them.
Reviewers ask: is it necessary, would people be less likely to take part if they knew, could it upset or harm them, will they be debriefed? APA 8.07(c) then requires explaining the deception as early as feasible, "preferably at the conclusion of their participation", and permitting participants to withdraw their data; 8.08 requires a prompt opportunity to learn the nature and results and, where information is delayed, reasonable steps to reduce harm [13]. (This is a research code, not law, and a family gift is not a study. I am borrowing its structure, not claiming it applies.)

**Philosophy, briefly (snippet only).** A search-result summary of Derek Parfit's 2002 Tanner lecture reports that he treats a lie to conceal a surprise party as morally innocent because the person would consent if asked; the same summary notes Kant's opposing view that deception and consent are incompatible [76]. A Leiden doctoral thesis abstract separates "surprise" (response to unexpectedness) from the later good or bad valence [75]. I could not open the Parfit PDF (HTTP 404 on fetch), so I do not rely on it. The practical hinge, visible in all of it: hypothetical consent works only if she *would* consent. Whether she would is the one fact the maker must establish through an insider before anything else (story.md Recipe 4, gate 0).

**Do some people dislike surprises? Anecdote only.** Search results gave opinion columns, an advice column and a first-person piece (guest of honor wants control of the day, social anxiety, performing happiness for an audience, resentment afterwards) and one UK newspaper survey claim of 60% of women hating surprises, none of them research I could open (snippet only) [81]. Treat as a prompt for the suitability question, not evidence.

**The test we use** (inference, grounded in [8][13]): a pretext is acceptable only if all six hold.
1. *Real*: the thing you say you are doing, you actually do, and she gets the result (the labelled photos, the family page).
2. *Accurate as far as it goes*: nothing you say is untrue; you omit the purpose.
3. *Stoppable*: she can decline or stop at any time and you stop.
4. *Not for the heavy material*: nothing she says under the pretext is used in a hard chapter, and nothing she volunteers from the middle or bottom drawer is used without the proxy and her own post-reveal OK (story.md Q7 drawers).
5. *Disclosed afterwards*: at the reveal you tell her what her words and captions became, and she may withdraw them (APA 8.07(c) analogue [13]).
6. *No recording of her without her knowledge and a spoken yes*; taking notes in your own words after a chat she knew was a conversation is fine.

**What the maker can ask her directly (card set, T7).**

| Ask | Truthful frame | What makes it real | Red flags |
|---|---|---|---|

| "Tell me about Grandma" | A real family-history page about *her* parents and grandparents (FamilySearch's relative-interview guidance [10]; Smithsonian [9]) | You do write that page, and it also feeds the film | Only ever asking about [NAME], never the others |

**Secret recording and the law (flag, do not conclude).** The Reporters Committee guide explains that federal law is one-party consent for in-person, phone and electronic communications; states may be stricter; about 11 states are described as primarily all-party (California, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan in some cases, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Washington); Missouri and Oregon are all-party for in-person talk but one-party by phone, and Connecticut and Nevada the reverse; Hawaii and Maine are all-party only in particularly private places; and it "should not take the place of legal advice from a lawyer" [14]. I found nothing reliable for the UK or EU (an ICO page on the household exemption did not address recording a call, snippet only [87]) and nothing on calls across state or national borders: UNVERIFIED. Apple's call recording is not available everywhere [15]. **Maker's checklist:** where do she, the maker and each contributor live? Is it a call or in person? Is anyone abroad? Which app? Do I have their *spoken* yes on the recording? The project rule is simpler than all of this: spoken consent every time, no recording of her without her knowledge and a spoken yes, no silent third-party call recorders [15].

**Surprise versus a deception she would resent (inference from the precedents in story.md Q3).**
- Resented: being tricked into a performance (Blanchflower, 1961, "Oh no it's not"), partners' secrecy read as infidelity (Barker), exposure of private history, being filmed without an off-switch, and discovering her own words used for something other than what she was told.
- Forgiven (anecdote): a purely logistical fib about a coffee date, followed by a joyful party, in a family where the mother had foiled the previous surprise (blog post, 2012) [35]. Note this account ends "No more lies. We promise" [35]; the family treated the lie as a debt to repay (my reading, inference).
- The difference is agency after the fact: what she may refuse, correct or take back (T9).

Worked example (inference, not sourced): six albums, 20 spreads each, 4 prints per spread = about 480 prints. Photographing 120 spreads at 25 s is about 50 minutes of camera time, plus about 30 minutes per album for set-up, the as-found photographs and the return record, so **3 to 5 hours over two or three visits**; splitting spreads into single prints is scripted afterwards (ai-imagery R1 multi-print split; Photomyne's album mode does the same for a fee [53]). Loose prints (say 300) on a flatbed are 5 to 8 hours; on a phone, about 1.5 hours.

**Album-in-place against removal.** Magnetic-page adhesive can fuse to prints and tear them on removal [55]; other search results I could not open (a scanning-software vendor's blog and a forum thread, listed under [55]) disagree about whether removal gives sharper scans. My rule (inference): photograph every spread *in place first* (it doubles as the order record), then remove only prints that lift easily and whose scan quality matters.

**Recording order and return exactly (the chain-of-custody sheet, T4).** Photograph the shelf or drawer as found, each album's cover and spine, and every spread before touching anything. Do not write on or tag the items; keep loose items in a labelled envelope kept *outside* the original housing; log time out, time back and who handled it. Return in reverse order and compare against the as-found photographs.

**Where scanning happens if she lives with the maker (options).** (a) At a place she does not go: a friend's, the maker's workplace, a library or makerspace; (b) while she is out on a fixed routine (a MetaFilter commenter scanned their mother's photos each morning while she was at her exercise class, then made a photo book, 2019 thread, snippet only) [41]; (c) phone only, with auto-upload and shared-library camera sharing switched off first (Q4); (d) never on the shared Windows PC unless that PC is in a private room under its own account (Q4). Libraries sometimes run self-service labs: DC Public Library's Memory Lab takes VHS, VHS-C, MiniDV, 8 mm film, photos and slides in three-hour bookable blocks, but Video8, Hi8 and Digital8 are "currently unavailable", and the page I fetched does not say whether it is free or open to non-residents [49]. It is an example, not a recommendation: the maker's location is unknown.

### Q5. The contributor brief

**What the sources and precedents support.**
- *Short, specific and time-boxed.* A university alumni brief for a surprise tribute video: deadline stated; video under 20 seconds, landscape, quiet room, no bright light behind, phone at eye level; audio under 20 seconds, about a foot from the mouth, built-in recorder app named; photos with a short description (date, place, event); or a few written sentences; one submission form plus an email for help; friendly tone; no explicit confidentiality line (the title carries "Surprise") [39].
- *Use channels people already know.* A February 2026 account: she texted friends to record 15 to 60 seconds through a selfie-video website; several older contacts could not use it or declined; takeaway "Don't ask seniors to use a website they don't already use"; a later FaceTime call, recorded, drew out spontaneous stories [37].
- *Thresholds and reminders.* A MetaFilter thread (2016, snippet only) advises simple step-by-step instructions for both iPhone and Android, telling people you will only edit if enough respond, and sharing the final with all contributors afterwards [41].
- *Say who else is involved.* The university team that surprised a student found the subject's mother essential: she agreed to the surprise and helped gauge the reaction [38].

**What the brief must contain** (checklist): one-sentence purpose; deadline; exactly what to send and in which format; how to send (one main way, one backup, a human to phone); what NOT to say and to whom; a short, truthful line to use if she asks; what happens to their words (consent) and that they can withdraw; who to ask when unsure; a thank-you and what they will see afterwards.

**Tone for older and non-technical contributors** (inference): short sentences; at least 14 pt; a phone number before any link; "there is no wrong way"; offer to visit with a phone; never ask for a new account or app; offer to write down what they say and read it back for approval.

**Reminder cadence** (inference): day 0, a personal ask by call or text from the maker (not a mass message); day 3, only if no acknowledgement: "did my message get through?"; one week before the deadline: a gentle nudge with the easiest option restated; two days before: a last one-line message; after the deadline: one call, then stop. Maximum three written messages. "No" is an answer.

**For people who dislike being recorded**: Version B in T2 (written memory, photo with caption, or "tell me and I will write it down for you to approve").

### Q6. Hard chapters without her consent, family dynamics

**Choosing and briefing a proxy reviewer.**
- *The standard to give them.* Medical ethics uses "substituted judgment" for people who cannot decide: the surrogate reports what the person would have chosen, based on their known preferences and values and how confident the surrogate is that they apply; "best interests" applies only when preferences cannot be identified (Braun, Naik and McCullough, 2009) [61]. For a tribute, ask the proxy "what would *she* choose, and what have you seen or heard her say that tells you?", not "what would be best for her".
- *Why not rely on one person.* A systematic review of 16 studies (Shalowitz et al., 2006) found surrogates predicted patients' treatment preferences with about 68% accuracy, with designated surrogates no better than default ones, and prior discussion did not help (via a clinician's summary [60]; PubMed abstract page behind a cookie wall, snippet only). These are medical hypotheticals, not tributes, so the figure is an analogy; the lesson is not to treat one proxy as an oracle (inference).
- *Who.* Someone who knows her well and is discreet; not a party to the conflict the chapter concerns; no stake in how the chapter portrays them; able to say no to the maker; and ideally one of: partner, closest friend, sibling or adult child. Use **two** where possible (the "two keys" rule): one decides with the maker, a second checks only the hard chapters.
- *How to brief.* T6.
- *Life-story practice agrees on whose say it is.* The University of Stirling Dementia Services Development Centre's good-practice points (from an NIHR study of life story work): the person decides what to include and "who will see, it now and in the future"; where they cannot say, "someone who knows them well, or has welfare Power of Attorney, could make that decision"; life story work "can be emotional … perhaps revisit past trauma" [58]. An NHS case study found people with dementia sometimes recalled private memories that were not meant for any written product [59].

**The questions that decide inclusion** (the brief's three, plus support):
1. *Would she tell this story herself, to this room?* Evidence: something she has told these people before, or has said she is happy for them to know. If you can only say "she would if she were ready", the answer is no for the shared cut.
2. *Who else is in it?* An ex, a sibling, a child, a person who died: do they appear, and have they a say? A family-history society code asks for "specific consent from living people that they agree to further sharing of information about themselves" and says revealing "criminal, immoral, bizarre or irresponsible behaviour may bring hurt to family members" [78].
3. *Is it hers to share?* If the story belongs jointly to her and someone else, ask that person before the proxy.
4. *Is it load-bearing?* Does the piece become false without it? (story.md Q7, Pixar's restored miscarriage beat.) If not, hold.
5. *Which drawer?* Brooker's top, middle, bottom drawers (story.md Q7): bottom stays out unless she invites it.
6. *Do not leave her worse off.* The documentary field's study of ethics (45 filmmakers, 2009) gives the shared principle: protect vulnerable subjects and do not leave them worse off than when you met them [62].

**Shared cut against private cut.** Pattern (inference, extending story.md Recipe 4): three outcomes and a holding state for every hard item.
- **OUT**: appears in neither cut.
- **PRIVATE**: appears only in her cut, watched alone or with whom she picks.
- **SHARED-LIGHT**: appears in the shared cut as a glimpse, an object, or a neutral line, using her own or a relative's words.
- **HOLD**: undecided; stays out until she decides after the reveal.
Default when unsure: HOLD, not SHARED. The asymmetry is the argument: you can add something later, you cannot un-show it.
Precedent for private screenings and consent: in a 2023 Hong Kong documentary dispute a subject said she never agreed to public release, and the director said the film would not be shown publicly again unless all participants consented (RTHK news summary, snippet only) [82]; private showings do not substitute for consent to wider release.

**How dignified films and tributes show the dead and the difficult.** Synthesis of sources; items marked (inference) are mine.
- *Brevity and stillness.* The Up and Danny and Annie analyses in story.md Q7 and Exemplars 1 and 2: the loss gets seconds, the longest beat is a face at a window; the people living it narrate in their own words.
- *Trait, not incident; honest then warm.* A 2026 first-person eulogy for a difficult father: name his patterns (rigid, easily hurt by criticism, authoritarian), link them to his history, follow each candid line with a gentler one, withhold the worst on purpose ("with a velvet glove"), and treat it as "a process for grieving authentically" not "a platform for airing grievances"; other speakers thanked him for being honest about their own fathers [56]. Search summaries of other eulogy advice say the same (name the trait, follow an honest sentence with one of love; one writer says that if a parent was a shadow in your life you need not invent a sun) (snippet only) [86].
- *Name a death plainly or not at all, as the family decides.* Funeral and suicide-loss guidance (snippet only): it is the family's decision whether to say how someone died; one simple sentence, then return to the life; avoid anything that reads as method, blame or "a reason"; an Australian memorial guide advises against an open platform where anyone may speak [91]. For this project: never name a cause of death without the closest family's say.
- *No synthetic voices.* In 2021 a documentary used an AI-generated voice of Anthony Bourdain for about 45 seconds reading an email he had written; the director said he checked with the widow and literary executor, his ex-wife publicly disputed that she approved, and critics condemned it; the director only disclosed it in an interview [63]. Charter Principle 4 already forbids this; the case shows the cost of getting consent wrong.
- *Animated photos of the dead are contested.* Press coverage of a photo-animation service reports both tears and "spooky"; no study; anecdote [64]. Default (consistent with ai-imagery summary item 10: environment motion and parallax, not faces, for people who have died): no face animation of the dead; if wanted, only with the closest family's explicit yes.
- *Be careful with images of the dead across cultures.* Australian institutions warn that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people may be distressed by images, voices and names of deceased persons and that this can conflict with strongly held cultural prohibitions (AIATSIS notice, snippet only) [65]. This is one example of why the cultural checklist below is a list of *questions*.
- *Give the bereaved a heads-up and an exit* (inference): privately before the reveal, so no one meets a late parent's face unprepared; a seat near the door.

**Estranged relatives.**
- *Ask the proxy first, never contact cold.* story.md Q3 records the reported case where researchers contacted a subject's son and the programme was pulled; the lesson stands.
- *Four options per estranged person:* (A) include as a neutral past image only if she would tell that story herself; (B) exclude; (C) invite a private contribution for her private cut only, with her chosen proxy as the go-between; (D) in the room (rare, only if she has said she would welcome it). Default B or C.
- *Never stage a reconciliation on screen or in the room.* No ambush appearance, no "message from X" in the shared cut as a gesture. The guest list is the honoree's or host's call; a milestone-party etiquette column (snippet only) judged unauthorised invitations by a relative to be a breach [74].
- *If they contact you,* say thanks, promise nothing, and take it to the proxy.

**Step-families and families of choice** (questions, not rules): Whom does she call family, and which words does she use (Dad, step-dad, "my mother's husband")? Does each household want to speak for itself? Are any two households not on speaking terms? Does each parent figure get equal screen weight or equal absence? Avoid ranking.

**The recently bereaved.** Who has lost someone in the last two years, including the mother herself? Ask them privately whether they want to appear, to hear a passage first, or to sit it out. Check mourning customs and calendars.

**Cultural and religious checklist (questions for the maker).** The calendar fact: Feb 27, 2027 lies inside Ramadan on the UAE Astronomy Centre's projection (start about Mon Feb 8, dependent on moon sighting; Eid about Mar 9 or 10) [72]; Lunar New Year 2027 is Sat Feb 6 [73]; Ash Wednesday 2027 is Feb 10 and Easter is Mar 28 (M, UNVERIFIED). Ask:
1. Is Feb 27 her birthday, or a milestone with a name in her tradition (e.g., a 60th with its own rites)? Does her community celebrate birthdays at all?
2. Does the date collide with a fast, a mourning period, a festival or an observance (Ramadan, Lent, a death anniversary)? Would the meal, timing or alcohol need adapting?
3. Who is the head of the family, and should that person be told earlier, as a courtesy, even if others are told later?
4. Who may speak first, sit first or be served first? Is it appropriate for a child (the maker) to host and present to elders?
5. Are there norms (hers or her family's) about being photographed or filmed, about who may see her image, or about dress and modesty on screen?
6. May images, voices or names of the dead be shown? Are there rituals or warnings expected before they appear?
7. Which language(s) will the room speak? Do we need subtitles in more than one?
8. Which music is acceptable in her tradition? Any restrictions on music at certain times?
9. Are topics like divorce, remarriage, illness, miscarriage or debt taboo, shaming, or a source of pride when told right?
10. What is the right way to thank, and is a public speech expected or unwelcome?
These come from the brief; none is sourced as a rule for any culture, and none should be applied as an assumption.

### Q7. Reveal design for her comfort and agency

**Evidence status.** I found no study of tribute-reveal length, surprise-reaction filming or the comfort of the honoree. What exists: vendor and funeral-industry tribute-video advice, a hearing-loss fact sheet, a social-anxiety party guide, and first-hand blogs. Everything below marked (inference) is a design proposal to test in the rehearsal.

**Setting options** (inference; choose with the proxy using the questions underneath).

| Setting | Fits | Cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Living room, 4 to 8 people | A woman who dislikes being the centre; intimate chapters; mixed-age family | Little room for escape; tech is a TV and a laptop |
| Family dinner at home or private restaurant room, 10 to 20 | Typical milestone; people can sit | Hosting load on the maker; noise |
| Party or venue, 30+ | A woman who loves a party and a crowd | Highest social load; projector and PA; hardest to protect hard chapters |
| Two-step: private viewing first, then the room | A woman who dislikes surprises in public, or heavy chapters | Splits the surprise; needs one or two trusted people to hold the secret until the second step |
| A private gift, shown alone or with 1 or 2 people | Highly private woman | No community moment; contributors lose the shared screening |

Ask the proxy: how did she react to the last surprise or being the centre of attention? Does she cry in front of people or hate it? Does she like to give a speech or dread it? What is her hearing like in a crowd? What does she say about being filmed? (The 2012 blog's mother had foiled a 50th by driving past and was "priceless" when surprised at 60 [35]; the 2019 column's wife guessed from the cars [36]. A single anecdote each.)

**Length and pacing for a mixed-age room.** Practitioners' tribute-video guidance (funeral-industry, vendor and anecdote): most run 3 to 8 minutes with 4 to 5 the "sweet spot" for a service, up to 10 for a looping reception display, 3 to 4 seconds per photo, 5 to 7 seconds for group shots and title cards, one to three songs, never longer than 10 minutes [67][68]. These are memorial contexts. My recommendation (inference): **shared cut 6 to 10 minutes, hard cap 12**, with the first hard beat not before about a quarter of the runtime (story.md: Up gives 77 seconds of accumulation before the first blow, and Recipe 5 puts the first hard beat at 25 to 30% of runtime); **private cut 20 to 30 minutes** (story.md's treatment tiers) with the explorable or playable parts reserved for her alone, because a linear cut works for a mixed-age room and an interactive world does not (inference; format track decides).

**Hearing and the older people in the room (not her).** NIDCD: about one in three US adults aged 65 to 74 has hearing loss and nearly half of those over 75 have difficulty hearing; tips: face the listener, reduce background noise, choose quieter spots (page last updated 2023-03-17) [66]. Implications (inference): burned-in captions in each language the room needs; a speech-forward mix with no loud-music sting; test intelligibility on the room's real speakers with an older relative; seat older relatives near the speakers and with a clear sight line; no talking over the film.

**When and how to tell her what is about to happen** (inference): a private quiet word 10 to 20 minutes before, from the person she trusts most, in a separate room. Content: "We are going to watch a short film about your life, made by [MAKER] with many people who love you. It is about [N] minutes. The hard parts have been handled with [PROXY]. You choose where to sit and who sits with you. If you want us to stop or pause, [signal]. You do not need to say anything afterwards." Offer two choices: watch now with the room, or watch alone first and join the party after. Research ethics' debriefing logic [13] and the oral-history review principle [8] support telling her what her contributions became and giving her choices about it.

**Where she sits and with whom** (inference): her choice, with a chosen companion beside her; the screen, not the room, is the focus; an aisle or side seat with an easy exit; not front-and-centre facing a wall of eyes.

**Lighting and sound** (inference): living room: close curtains, lamp low (dim, not dark, so people can see each other), TV at eye level, sound bar or good speakers; test with the windows and neighbours' noise. Venue: projector contrast needs the lights lower, so keep exit lights and a small lamp near her; a PA with a monitor mix; a person on the sound desk who is not the maker; avoid music louder than the voices.

**Whether to film her reaction, and how to ask.** Vendor advice (VidDay): filming can pull attention into self-consciousness, so keep the camera out of the recipient's line of sight, start recording before and keep recording after the video ends, do not ask questions while it plays, if the recipient did not know they were recorded tell them afterwards and show them the footage, get her comfort before sharing it with contributors or online, and if she dislikes being filmed "consider skipping the camera altogether" [42]. A university team used two videographers from two angles [38]. My recommendation (inference): ask in the quiet word, "We would like to record the room, not close-ups of you. Is that all right?" Default to a wide shot of the room and the screen; no close-up unless she says yes; give her the footage and let her delete any of it.

**What to say afterwards and what not to ask** (inference):
- Say: "Thank you for letting us do this." "No speech needed. Come and eat." "The private version is yours whenever you want it."
- Do not say or ask: "Did you like it?", "Aren't you going to say something?", "Look at the camera", "Why are you crying?", "Did you know?", or anything that makes her perform gratitude.

**A technical fallback** (inference; the brief's "USB plus laptop plus offline copy tested the day before"): primary: laptop, local file, HDMI to the room's screen, captions burned in, volume set, Do Not Disturb (Windows: "when duplicating the display" is an automatic option [24]), notifications hidden on the lock screen [24]; secondary: the same file on two USB sticks in different pockets; tertiary: the file on a phone with a cast or cable; quaternary: a 3-minute audio-only version if video fails; last resort: postpone the viewing to a private evening and say so plainly. Test the *cold start* the day before on the real device, with the real cable.

**How the evening continues** (inference): a 10 to 15 minute pause with lights up, soft music, food and the companion beside her; no toast until she signals she is ready; optional contributors' toasts only if they have agreed in advance; no speeches about the hard chapters; end with an invitation to the private version (T5).

| A private web link | Easy for faraway relatives | 38% of pages that existed in 2013 were gone by October 2023 (Pew, 2024-05-17) [69]; hosts change terms; "anyone with the link" privacy. Treat as a convenience copy, not the archive; password and expiry; export the files to the drive |

**A concrete mechanism for her to add her own stories** (inference, using the OHA review menu [8]):
1. *The keepsake carries a one-page invitation:* "This is version 1. It is yours to correct and to add to. Here is how."
2. *A recorded conversation about a week after the reveal* (T9): her choice of place and time; she is the narrator and the maker the interviewer; this is the first recording in which she gives full informed consent [8].
3. *A corrections ledger* (T9): each item, what the film says, what she says, the action, the status.
4. *Her version.* Within six to eight weeks, v2 that folds in her corrections and, if she wishes, her own narration of chosen photos, labelled "her version", while the family version stays unchanged as a record.
5. *The OHA menu as her rights:* the right to delete, restrict and redact parts, add clarifications, correct mistakes, and keep material closed until a set date or decline release altogether [8].
6. *A standing low-pressure invitation:* one story per month, by call, voice memo or card; no schedule beyond hers.
7. *A StoryCorps-style archive option, only if she chooses it.* The StoryCorps privacy settings (Public, Community, Private) control visibility only [3]; a Library of Congress copy persists for any setting (search-result summary [4]); story.md [4] covers perpetual-licence terms. Alternative: a family archive on drives, and (her call) a copy placed with a local library or historical society (a university oral-history office suggests this; snippet only) [85].
8. *Closing the consent gap:* a Consent Ledger (T6) she signs off at about 30 days: each chapter kept shared, kept private or removed, her decisions overriding the maker's.

### T1. Covert interview kit, per relationship (Deliverable 1)

#### T1.0 Kit shell (use for every relationship)

**Before (24 to 48 hours).**
1. Ask personally (call, text or in person) using the T2 brief. Confirm they want to do it [12]. Tell them it is a surprise and what the secret rules are.
2. Offer the choice of channel: I visit with my phone; you record a voice letter; or we talk on the phone and I record with your OK.
3. Send the **prompt card**: three of the prompts below, not all. Offering questions ahead is recognised practice for reluctant narrators [11]; keep the rest in reserve (story.md: do not let them tell the whole story before you record).
4. Tell them how long: "about 20 to 30 minutes; we can stop whenever you like".

**Set-up (5 minutes; settings from story.md Q8).**
- Quiet soft room; fridge and clocks off; Do Not Disturb; phone plugged in or charged; phone on a folded towel 6 to 12 inches from the nearest mouth, hands off the phone; lossless or highest-quality setting; a second device if you have one. Test: speak, play back, adjust [6][story.md Q8].
- In-person with props: three photos or one object on the table; tea.

**20 to 30 minute shape.**

| Minute | Part | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:03 | Slate and consent | Say names, full date, place ("at [NAME]'s home" not an address), kit number. Read the **consent card** aloud; they say yes on the recording. Tell them they can skip, stop, or have anything taken out |
| 0:03 to 0:08 | Warm-up | One easy present-tense question: "Where are you sitting and what is in front of you?" or "How did you hear I was doing this?" |
| 0:08 to 0:22 | Core scenes | Two to four of the relationship prompts below, with follow-ups "Tell me more", "What did it smell/sound like?", "Then what?", and silence (count to ten) (story.md Recipe 3; FamilySearch also says avoid yes/no questions [10]) |
| 0:22 to 0:27 | The "only you" story | Ask once, plainly, using the T1.0 line below |
| 0:27 to 0:30 | Closer and sort | "Is there anything I should have asked?" "Is there anything you have said that you would rather I left out?" "Would [NAME] be happy for the whole family to hear any of it?" Thank them. Remind them of the secret rules. Keep the recorder running until they leave the room, then say goodbye off tape (story.md Recipe 3) |

**The "only you" line** (inference): "You were the only person in the room for at least one story about [NAME]. Which one is yours?" Ladder if blank: "When were you alone with her?" then "Where?" then "What happened next?" Then: "Would she tell this one herself, to a room?" "Is there a version you would be happy for the whole family to hear?"

**Do not ask** (inference): "What do you love most about her?" "Was she a good mother?" "Why did she…?" (blame); anything about money, health, romance, conflict or her faith unless they volunteer (then log it as `drawer: middle/bottom` and stop); anything that asks them to speak for her.

**Consent card (read aloud, on the recording; also fine as a message to confirm afterwards).** Adapted from the OHA four-point process [8] and story.md Recipe 3.

> "[MAKER] is making a private film and keepsake about [NAME]'s life as a surprise gift, for [date]. May I use what you say, and your recorded voice, in two places: a film that family will watch together, and a longer private version given to [NAME]? You can skip any question, ask me to stop, and ask me afterwards to cut anything. I will keep the recording private, on storage I control, and will not share it more widely without asking you again. I may use software, including AI tools [that run on my own computer / in a private online workspace: edit to match what really happens], to type it up and tidy the sound, but I will not change what you said or make a synthetic copy of your voice. Is that all right with you?"

Edit the bracketed line before first use so it describes what truly happens to the audio (charter: nothing personal goes to a third-party service without the maker's say; story.md Pitfall 4: narrators should know if a cloud AI is used). This is an open decision for the maker (see Open questions).

One-line version for a text or voice message: "May I use your recorded voice in a private film and keepsake for [NAME], shown to family and given to her, with you able to skip questions, stop, or have anything taken out afterwards?"

**After (within 24 hours; story.md Recipe 3 steps 21 to 25).** Copy to two places; name files `YYYY-MM-DD_[relation]-[initials]_kit##.m4a`; never edit the original; time-coded index; run local transcription; inventory rows with `drawer`, `consent_status` and `told_by_candidate` (yes, maybe or no, plus the chapter; the question: could this be the one story this person tells, in their voice, as the spine of a chapter?); send a thank-you (on paper for older relatives [10]) with a date when they will see the result; tell them how to withdraw.

**Children and teens (inference).** Under 14: a guardian present, one question, a drawing is fine. 14 to 17: guardian consent and a written release; the same card in plain words.

#### T1.1 Her parents (if living)

Channel and length: in person at their table; 20 to 30 minutes; props: three photos; seat them, sit beside not opposite; speak up for hearing and face them [66]; one at a time (a commercial documentary-course author interviews parents separately [40]). Frailty: stop if tired; Baylor says be patient with older narrators [12]. Brief on the secret late, in person, with the one-line rule.

1. Take me into the first home you brought [NAME] to. Start at the front door: what do you see, smell, hear? (L, S)
2. What was she always doing with her hands at six or seven? Where would I find her on a Saturday morning? (R, O)
3. What did she say or do that made the whole table laugh? Say it the way she said it. (W)
4. It is an ordinary school night. Where does everyone sit, what is on the table, who talks? (R, P, S)
5. Is there something she made, kept or carried that you can still picture? Where did it live in the house? (O)
6. When did you first see her do something and think "there she goes, that is exactly her"? What happened, step by step? (X)
7. What was your own day like then: where you worked, what you worried about, what things cost? (M, R) [the parents are narrators of their own lives too; story.md Recipe 1]
8. Optional, with an exit: what was the house like on the day she left home? (X, L)
**Only you:** "What is a story about your own life at her age that she might not know and would love to?"
Notes: her parents may mention it to her without thinking; brief them one line only: "It's a secret until [date]." If memory is fading, use photos and let a sibling sit in.

#### T1.2 Siblings

Channel: in person or a recorded call, **each alone first** (their versions differ [story.md Q9 #5]); then, optionally, a group evening later for the shared laughter [9].

1. Describe the room you shared or the wall between you: what could you hear through it? (L, S)
2. What were the rules of your games, and how did she bend them? (R, W)
3. The family car: who sat where, and what happened on the worst or funniest trip? (X, R)
4. What chore was hers and how did she get out of it? (R)
5. What names did you call each other, and what is the story behind them? (W)
6. When did you first notice she wasn't just your [big/little] sister but a grown-up? Where were you? (X)
7. Tell me about a time she stood up for you, or you for her. What exactly happened? (X)
8. Optional, with an exit: what did your parents argue about that you only understood later? (R; middle drawer)
**Only you:** "What is the story about you two that nobody else in the family knows?"
Notes: sibling accounts diverge; record the divergence and mark it in the inventory (story.md "confidence" column).

#### T1.3 Partner (current)

Decision before the interview: is [PARTNER] your **co-keeper**, your **proxy**, both, or neither? If they are both, do the interview *and* the proxy briefing as two separate sessions.

1. Walk me through an ordinary weekday morning: who is up first, what is the first thing she touches, what does she say? (R, S, W)
2. What does she hum, sing or whistle? What is on when she cooks or drives? (S)
3. What does she do when she thinks nobody is looking? (R)
4. Take me to the first time you knew. Where were you standing, what was she wearing? (X)
5. What does she do when things go wrong? Tell me one specific time. (X)
6. What is the sentence she says all the time, and when exactly does she say it? (W)
7. What is the object in the house that is most hers, and what is its story? (O)
8. What is her side of the bed, the sofa, the table? Where does she put her keys? (L, O)
**Only you:** "What is a moment between you two that nobody saw, which you would like her to hear about one day?" (exit offered; likely middle drawer)

#### T1.4 Ex-partner (decision gate, not a kit)

**Default: do not approach.** Ask the proxy: Is she on good terms with [EX]? Would she welcome their contribution? Is there a child in common who should decide? If any answer is no or unsure, stop. If yes, use only 3 to 4 neutral prompts: place and year you lived; what a Sunday looked like; a song, food or friend you both remember; what she was proud of making. Never ask about the end of the relationship. Give them an easy no. Include only in the private cut unless the proxy and [NAME]'s own history say otherwise (story.md Q3 Mullard precedent). #### T1.5 Her children (including the maker and siblings)

Channel: in person or voice letter. The maker is one narrator among them; record the maker's own memories too (a sibling or friend can interview you).

1. What did she do when you were sick? Walk me through it: what she brought, where she sat, what she said. (B, R, S)
2. What did her car, handbag or coat smell like? (S, O)

**Only you:** "What is a memory of her that you have never told her?" (offer to write it down instead)
Notes: grandchildren: one-sentence answers or a drawing, with guardians' OK. Heavy emotion is likely; allow breaks (story.md Recipe 3 "if someone cries").

#### T1.6 Oldest friends

Channel: call or in person; two friends together can work (group effect [9]) after each has been alone. Say at the start: "Tell me what to leave out; I will."

#### T1.7 Former colleagues

Channel: short voice memo (10 to 15 minutes) or call; no workplace confidences.

1. What was your first impression, and what did her desk or workspace look like? (L, O)
2. What did she do on a normal Tuesday? Where did she eat lunch? (R)
3. Tell me about a day it all went wrong and what she did, step by step. (X)
4. What phrase of hers became part of the office vocabulary? (W)
5. Whom did she look after at work (a new starter, an apprentice)? What exactly did she do? (P)

#### T1.8 Neighbours

**Only you:** "What story from the street do you think the family has never heard?" (exit offered)

**The quiet word (script).** "In a few minutes we are going to watch a short film about your life, made by [MAKER] with lots of people who love you. It runs about [N] minutes. ### T6. Proxy-reviewer protocol and hard-chapter decision sheet (Deliverable 6)

#### Protocol

1. **Choose two keys** (inference; analogy to surrogate-accuracy findings [60]). Score candidates 0 to 2 on: knows her well; discreet; unconflicted in the chapters concerned; will say no to the maker; trusted by her; available weeks 12 to 18. Choose the top two; a partner may be Key 1 but not the only key.
2. **Ask** (script): "I'm making a film about [NAME]'s life as a surprise. There are chapters she can't approve in advance, so I'm asking you to help me decide what she would want, not what I would want. It's a secret from her and everyone else. May I tell you what I mean?"
3. **Brief** (one page): purpose; confidentiality; the standard (**substituted judgment**: "what would she choose, and what have you seen or heard her say or do that tells you?" [61]); the asymmetry ("we can add later, we can't un-show"); what they will see (hard-chapter drafts only, not the whole film); what they decide (inclusion level for each item); time-box (about 45 minutes; weeks 12 to 17); what happens if the two keys disagree; and that **she will decide at the reveal and after** (the proxy is a bridge, not a final authority).
4. **Session.** Walk the decision sheet item by item. The proxy states their evidence. Record answers in the sheet. Do not argue the maker's case.
5. **Rules.**
   - If either key says "she would not want this in the shared cut", it is not in.
   - If the keys differ on PRIVATE, it is HOLD.
   - If the proxy is a party to the story, they **recuse** on that item; use the other key.
   - If the maker disagrees with a key, the maker keeps it out; the maker may not overrule toward inclusion.
6. **Not the proxy's job:** to approve the whole film, to plan the party, to judge the maker's craft, or to speak for contributors.
7. **After the reveal:** the proxy's decisions are provisional; she can overturn any (T9 ledger).

### T7. Truthful-pretext cards (supporting deliverable)

**Rule (no-lie floor).** You may omit; you may not state something untrue; you may not record her without her knowledge and a spoken yes; you stop if she says stop. If she sincerely asks "are you making something about me?", you do not lie.

**Card A: photo labelling.** Say: "The old photos are fading and nobody has written on them. Will you help me put names and years on them? I'll keep the labels and give you a copy." Do: actually label and keep them; give her a copy; write notes after, in your words; never record her. Must disclose at the reveal: the labels fed the film.

**Card B: "tell me about Grandma"** (a real family-history page for her parents and grandparents). Say: "I'd like to write a page about Grandma and Grandpa before we lose the details. What do you remember about them?" Do: actually write it, give her a copy; ask about the other ancestors too. (Genealogy interview practice [10].)

**Card C: recipes or a playlist.** Say: "I'd like to write down how you make [dish]." / "I'm making a playlist for the [holiday dinner]. What was on in the kitchen when you were 20?" Do: make the recipe card or the playlist and play it.

**Card D: my memory project.** Say: "I'm trying to write down my own early memories. Do you remember what the street looked like?" Do: write your own too.

**Card E: the family-stories evening.** Say, at the table: "Can I record a few family stories tonight? Everyone just tells their favourite memory of [Grandpa/this house]." Do: put the recorder where everyone sees it; ask each person's yes; say it is for the family's story archive and a family project (do not say "only an archive" if it will feed the film); she tells hers knowing. Use the Smithsonian's group-interview logic [9].

**If she asks: "Are you making something about me?"** Options, in order:
1. "Yes, something for you, and I'd love to keep the details until [date]. If you'd rather know more, tell me and I'll tell you." (honest; keeps the surprise unless she wants it spoiled)
2. "I'm working on a family project, and I've promised people to keep it quiet until it's done." (true)
3. Never: "No." Never invent a different project.
If she objects, stop and take it to the proxy (T8).

**If she asks a contributor,** use Version A's line: "[MAKER] asked me for a few memories for a family project, and I promised to keep it between us until it is finished."

### T8. Slip protocol (supporting deliverable)

1. **Stop.** Do not paper over with a lie.
2. **Assess in 10 minutes:** What did she see or hear? Does it point to the project, or just to "something"?
3. **Tell the co-keeper within an hour.**
4. **Choose** (story.md Recipe 4 gate 6: "if it leaks, tell the subject and convert to collaborative"):
   - *Carry on:* she saw only an ambiguity; the truthful line is enough.
   - *Partial reveal:* "There's a gift in progress; here's what you can know; the rest is a surprise. Tell me what you would like to know."
   - *Convert to collaborative:* she asks to be involved; give her the choice of which chapters to see; the ledger is hers from day 1.
   - *She objects:* stop and ask the proxy; later, her.
5. **Fix the leak** (setting, device, person).
6. **Log it** (what, how, fixed).
7. **If a contributor slipped:** thank them, no blame; ask exactly what she heard.
8. **If a child blurted:** stay calm; use the partial-reveal lines.

## Risks, pitfalls and ethics

| Risk | Mitigation | Fallback |
|---|---|---|
| **Anti-pattern: secretly recording her** (phone in pocket, call recorded without notice, a speaker listening). Violates Principle 5; the law varies [14] | Never; notes after a conversation she knew about are fine | If it happened by accident, delete and tell her at the reveal |
| **Anti-pattern: an invented reason that makes her act** (the Blanchflower approach, story.md Q3) | Truthful partial pretexts only (T7); real side-projects | Convert to collaborative if exposed |
| **Anti-pattern: using words she gave under a pretext in hard chapters** | Pretext answers are top-drawer only; anything heavier goes to the proxy sheet and her post-reveal review | Remove on request |
| **Anti-pattern: lying when she sincerely asks** | The no-lie floor (T7); partial reveal (T8) | Convert to collaborative |
| **Anti-pattern: synthetic voice of anyone** [63]; animating a dead person's face [64] | Charter Principle 4; use real audio, objects, places | With explicit consent only, and labelled (ai-imagery ladder) |
| **Anti-pattern: staging a reconciliation or ambush** | Ask the proxy; never in the room or on screen | Out |
| She dislikes surprises, speeches or being the centre | Gate 0 insider check; a two-step reveal; a private-gift option; stop signal | Convert to collaborative; postpone the shared viewing |
| The proxy is wrong (about a third of the time in a medical analogy [60]) | Two keys; default HOLD; veto window; reveal as consent point | Her decision overrides at any time |
| The partner as co-keeper strains the relationship (Barker, story.md Q3) | Decide what they will say; keep the proxy and co-keeper roles distinct if the relationship is fragile | Tell her early |
| Contributor consent drifts or contributor changes their mind | Consent card; withdrawal window to Feb 19; re-confirm Feb 15 [8][78] | Remove the clip |
| Estranged relatives contacted or invited | Proxy first; options A to D (Q6) | Out |
| Group-chat or shared-device leak | T3 | T8 |
| Mail-in absence (4 to 10 weeks) is noticed | Choose items she will not miss; photograph as found | Truthful line; the no-lie floor |
| Film or tapes recycled or lost | Written return terms; return shipping bought; track [46] | Insurance or a local lab (UNVERIFIED) |
| Vendor bias in price comparisons | Several sources; get quotes [43][44][45][46] | Re-quote before ordering |
| Anecdote taken as best practice | Labels (A, S); no claims from one blog | Test in rehearsal |
| Link rot and media obsolescence | Two physical copies, refresh at least every five years; static QR; no sole reliance on a link [69][70][71] | Reprint or re-burn |
| Holiday overload and extraction fatigue | Table E; listening only | Postpone to January |
| Culture and calendar collisions (Ramadan, Lunar New Year, mourning, observance) | The Q6 checklist; ask the proxy and elders | Move the viewing to a date that works for her; the build deadline (picture lock Feb 19) can stay |
| Emotional distress during an interview or the reveal | story.md Recipe 3 "if someone cries or goes quiet"; a quiet seat and a companion; no forcing speech | Stop, offer water and a break, move on only if they ask |
| Safe messaging around a suicide death | The family decides whether to name it; no method or blame; an open microphone is a risk [57][91]; give a support number where a wider audience might be affected | Leave out |
| Reveal-day technical failure | Fallback ladder (T5) | Postpone the viewing, keep the party |
| Filming her reaction | Ask in the quiet word; wide shot; she can delete [42] | Skip the camera |
| Music licensing and posting online | Private screening only; confirm licences (story.md Q9) | Royalty-free or her own recordings |
| Unverified legal assumptions | Ask a lawyer where it matters; flag everything stated as law | Stricter project rules |

**Ethics summary (for the Fable gate).** (1) Omission yes, untruths and secret recording no. (2) She decides what remains, who sees it, and for how long (OHA review options, Stirling's "who will see it now and in the future") [8][58]. (3) The proxy bridges the gap; it does not replace her. (4) Third parties are asked, not assumed [78]. (5) Do not leave her worse off than before [62].

### F1. Free, open-source encryption for the external drive (Windows 11 Home)

**Current version and date.** VeraCrypt **1.26.29**, labelled "Latest Stable Release", dated Tuesday 9 June 2026 on the project's download page and in its release notes [93][94]. The GitHub releases page shows 1.26.29 with asset timestamps of 12 June (three days later; it also lists two May 2026 pre-releases below it) [103]. I saw no newer stable release on 2026-10-18. Installers: EXE (x64 and ARM64), MSI (x64 and ARM64), portable, each with a PGP signature link [93].

**Licence.** The project's licence page says it is "multi-licensed under Apache License 2.0 and the TrueCrypt License version 3.0" [95]. Wikipedia calls it "free and open-source" but says the mixed licence is "widely but not universally considered to be free and open source" and that Debian does not treat it as free [102]; a Privacy Guides forum summary says the FSF counts TrueCrypt License 3.0 as non-free (snippet only) [108]. For this project that is a labelling quibble: it costs nothing and the source is public. (inference)

**Windows 11 24H2.** The supported-systems page says "Windows 11 (x64 and ARM64)" and "Windows 10 version 1809 or later" and does not list feature updates, so 24H2 and 25H2 are not named [96]. The release notes mention Windows 11 22H2 Secure Desktop fixes (1.26.7, 1.26.14), a note that Windows 10 1809 is the minimum (1.26.18), and, in 1.26.29, a hibernation-crash fix "on fresh Windows 11 25H2 installations" [94]. No 24H2-specific entry and no documented 24H2 problem. One German forum thread (snippet only) reports "files damaged, run chkdsk" warnings on a container on an external drive on 24H2 and 25H2, with a reply blaming unclean removal [109]. Verdict: supported as "Windows 11"; the 24H2-specific claim is UNVERIFIED, so rehearse on the real PC (mount, copy a file, dismount, unplug, remount) before real material goes in.

**Can a non-admin project user mount it?** Yes, with one-time admin set-up.
- An administrator must install VeraCrypt first, because it needs a device driver that standard users cannot install or start [97].
- A standard user can then "mount/unmount any type of VeraCrypt volume", load and save data, and create file-hosted volumes [97]. The multi-user page adds that non-admins can mount partition- or device-hosted volumes given the correct password or keyfile, and can unmount only volumes they mounted themselves [98].
- A standard user **cannot** encrypt or format partitions, create NTFS volumes, install or uninstall VeraCrypt, change passwords or keyfiles of partition/device volumes, back up or restore headers, or run portable mode [97]. Portable mode needs administrator rights [100].
- So: the maker, as administrator, installs it and creates the volume; the project account only mounts and dismounts.

**What the VeraCrypt documentation says that matters for secrecy.**
- "The content of a mounted VeraCrypt volume is visible (accessible) to all logged on users", Fast User Switching does not unmount it, and the password cache is shared by all logged-on users [98]. So the rule is: **dismount before leaving the desk or switching user** (inference from [98]).
- Fast Startup (described for Windows 8 and 10) can leave volumes mounted after a restart [99]; dismount by hand before shutting down.
- VeraCrypt can auto-unmount after inactivity (Preferences: "Auto-unmount volume after no data has been read/written to it for") and on entering power saving mode (Settings > Preferences > Unmount all when) [101][128]; another page lists logoff among auto-unmount triggers [101]. A "User logs off" checkbox in the dialog is UNVERIFIED here.
- VeraCrypt itself warns it is "not safe to work with sensitive data under systems where you do not have administrator privileges", because an administrator can capture passwords and data [97][100]. **If the mother or any other person is an administrator on this PC, VeraCrypt does not protect against that person.** Check who has admin rights.
- Hidden volumes are not needed here. 1.26.29 fixed a hidden-volume quick-format flaw (CVE-2026-54073) that affects hidden volumes in file containers created with 1.26.6 to 1.26.28 [94].

**The 2026 signing scare, and why it should not matter here.** On 30 March 2026 the lead developer wrote that Microsoft had terminated the account he used to sign Windows drivers and the bootloader, so he could not publish Windows updates; on 9 April he wrote that non-system volumes are not affected because the driver keeps working, while system (boot-drive) encryption would have trouble once the 2011 EFI certificate authority is revoked later in 2026 [104]. TechCrunch reported the same and quoted "there is nothing special to do for now as VeraCrypt will continue to work" [105]; heise (9 April) said Microsoft was working on it and the developer confirmed his account was being restored [106]; two search summaries report reinstatement in mid-April (snippet only) [107]. 1.26.29 then shipped on 9 June with support for Microsoft UEFI CA 2023-signed bootloaders [94], which suggests signing resumed (inference; I did not inspect the installer's signature). **Rule for this project: use VeraCrypt only for the external drive, never for the Windows boot drive.**

**Design choice (decided, Fable advisor round 2).** File container, not whole-device: a standard user can mount it, an accidental format prompt is avoided (that prompt behaviour is (M), UNVERIFIED here), and the one visible large file is named with the code name. Encrypting the whole device also works but must be created by an admin [97]. Either way keep the password and any keyfile off the PC and out of the family chat.

**Drop-in wording for nudge 5 (inference).** "Tool: VeraCrypt 1.26.29 (free, from veracrypt.io). While signed in as the PC's administrator, install it and create an encrypted volume on the external drive. From the project account, mount it only while working and dismount it before you step away or switch users. Do not use it on the Windows boot drive. Before real material goes in, test mount, copy, dismount and unplug once."

### F2. Does Windows Home Device Encryption cover a second local user?

**What Microsoft says (and does not say).**
- The consumer page: Device Encryption "is available on a wider range of devices, including those running Windows Home"; it switches on automatically during first setup when you sign in with a Microsoft or work/school account, with the recovery key attached to that account; "If you're using a local account, Device Encryption isn't turned on automatically"; turning it on needs an administrator account. It does not mention multiple users or additional accounts [112].
- The IT page (updated 2026-10-08): BitLocker "provides encryption for entire volumes"; device encryption "encrypts only the OS drive and fixed drives, it doesn't encrypt external/USB drives" [110].
- Same page: after setup the OS and fixed drives are initialised with a clear key (the equivalent of suspended BitLocker). On a device that is not domain or Entra joined, "a Microsoft account with administrative privileges on the device is required. When the administrator uses a Microsoft account to sign in, the clear key is removed, a recovery key is uploaded to the online Microsoft account, and a TPM protector is created." And: "If a device uses only local accounts, then it remains unprotected even though the data is encrypted." [110]
- The OEM page agrees: protection is armed only after a Microsoft or Azure AD sign-in, and is not enabled with local accounts [111]. Since Windows 11 24H2 the Modern Standby/HSTI and DMA prerequisites are gone, so more PCs qualify [110][111].
- A 2024 Microsoft Q&A thread on Home with a local account was inconclusive; only a community volunteer and the asker replied, no Microsoft staff [113].

**Reading for this project (inference).**
1. The state belongs to the machine and its drives, not to a user. Once an administrator has signed in with a Microsoft account and it is armed, everything on C: (every user's profile) is protected at rest by the TPM-held key. A second local user signing in does not arm it and does not need to. If it was never armed (local-only accounts), the data is encrypted but the key is stored in the clear, so it is effectively unprotected against someone who pulls the drive.
2. It is **theft protection, not family-privacy protection.** After boot the volume is readable for every account; separation between users is only file permissions. Use a separate Windows account plus the VeraCrypt volume for secrecy.
3. It does **not** cover the external drive [110]. An internal secondary drive would be covered.
4. The recovery key goes to the Microsoft account of the administrator who signed in [110]. Whoever can sign in to that account can retrieve the key; if that account is shared or the mother's, note it. It would not open the VeraCrypt volume.
5. Check status (documented): run `msinfo32` and read "Device Encryption Support"; the Settings device-encryption page shows On only when encryption is complete [110]. A community poster ran `manage-bde -status` on Home and saw BitLocker on C: [113]; availability of that command on Home is UNVERIFIED.

Still UNVERIFIED: any per-user nuance Microsoft has not documented. If it matters, test by arming it, creating a second local user, and checking that profile's folder is unreadable from a boot USB (the maker does not need to do this for the project).

**F5b. Audio cassettes, microcassettes and answering-machine tapes.**
- *What they are.* Microcassettes run at about 2.4 and 1.2 cm/s (the slow speed doubles recording time) and were "commonly used in telephone answering machines and dictation machines" [126][127] (Wikipedia flags its page as needing citations). Whether the maker's family used tape or a chip-based digital answering machine is unknown; chip machines have no tape to capture.
- *Triage first.* NARA's audio condition table: sticky shed (shedding on the tape path, squeal) and soft binder (squeal, little shedding) are different faults; "Do not bake" for soft binder, oxide loss, vinegar syndrome; for mould, "Do not handle recording because mold and fungus can be serious health hazards. Seek professional advice" [123]. Rule: squealing, shedding, powdery growth or a musty smell means do not play it and ask a lab.
- *Capture chain.* A real cassette deck with a Dolby switch into an audio interface, recorded in Audacity as 24-bit uncompressed WAV, with gain set from the loudest passage, trailing silence trimmed, a log of every setting and a master that is never edited [124] (a 2012 archive guide, which uses 96 kHz/24-bit). A heritage workflow in the results uses 48 kHz/24-bit (snippet only) [130]. For cassette bandwidth I would use 48 kHz/24-bit WAV (inference): about 1.0 GB per hour stereo or 0.5 GB mono (my arithmetic: 48,000 x 3 bytes x 2 channels), against about 2.1 GB per hour at 96 kHz. Audacity is free and open source (M).
- *Time.* Capture is real time: a 60-minute tape takes 60 minutes plus handling (a library preservation lab says the same, snippet only) [131]. Twenty tapes is a 20-hour job.
- *Microcassette speed.* The deck needs a speed selector. If the tape was recorded at 1.2 cm/s and the deck plays only 2.4, fix it in one speed-change step in software, not a stack of pitch edits (an archivists' mailing-list post, snippet only) [132]. Which speed answering-machine tapes used is UNVERIFIED.
- *Lab option.* UK: Oxford Duplication Centre lists £26.00 per cassette up to 60 minutes (£18.00 at 100 or more), excluding 20% VAT, in a group named "Cassettes, Dictaphone, Mini & Micro Tapes"; it does not mention answering-machine tapes or turnaround [129]. US figures of about $19.95 to $27 per tape come from search snippets only (the Archival Works pricing page returned 404 when I opened it) [133]. Call a lab before relying on any of these; for a microcassette ask whether they accept answering-machine microcassettes.
- *Phone voicemail.* The iPhone Phone app has a Share button for voicemail messages, and saved messages stay until deleted "or until your carrier erases them, if they're not stored in iCloud" (snippet of an Apple page whose body was cut off when I fetched it; the exported file type is UNVERIFIED) [134]. Other phones: not researched.
- *Keep it.* Open formats, at least two copies kept apart, a yearly readability check and fresh copies every five years [125], matching the existing rule [70].
- *Ethics (inference).* A tape is a recording of people who never agreed to a film, and callers' voices on answering-machine tapes are third parties. Found voice (Decision 18 in the playbook): her own greeting or message is the strongest candidate and top drawer, but she has not agreed to its reuse, so it is listed in the T9 ledger for her post-reveal decision and never opens a hard chapter. ### G1. German Criminal Code section 201, re-verified

**What loaded.** The English translation (Bohlander, updated by Reusch) loaded with HTTP 200 and was text-extracted locally; its header says it includes the amendment by Article 2 (2) of the Act of 7 November 2024 (BGBl. 2024 I Nr. 351) [136]. The German original also loaded [135]. The two 503s in the question were transient.

**Subsection (1), English, verbatim [136]:** "Whoever, without being authorised to do so, 1. makes an audio recording of the privately spoken words of another or 2. uses or makes a recording thus produced available to a third party incurs a penalty of imprisonment for a term not exceeding three years or a fine."

**German original, verbatim [135]:** "Mit Freiheitsstrafe bis zu drei Jahren oder mit Geldstrafe wird bestraft, wer unbefugt 1. das nichtöffentlich gesprochene Wort eines anderen auf einen Tonträger aufnimmt oder 2. eine so hergestellte Aufnahme gebraucht oder einem Dritten zugänglich macht."

**Correction to round 1.** The F5a row paraphrased item 2 as "uses or makes available such a recording to a third party". The verbatim wording is "uses or makes a recording thus produced available to a third party". The substance is the same. Round 1 also did not mention the rest of the section, which matters for a public premiere:

- **(2) no. 1:** same penalty for whoever, without authorisation, "uses a listening device to intercept the privately spoken words of another which they are not intended to hear" [136].
- **(2) no. 2:** same penalty for whoever, without authorisation, "publicly communicates, verbatim or the essential content of, the privately spoken words of another which were recorded as per subsection (1) no. 1 or intercepted as per subsection (2) no. 1". Sentence 2: this act is a crime "only" if the public communication "is suited to interfering with the legitimate interests of another"; it "is not unlawful" if made "for the purpose of safeguarding overriding public interests" [136].
- **(3)** a higher penalty (up to five years) for public officials; **(4)** attempt is punishable; **(5)** the recording and listening devices used may be confiscated [136].
- **Section 205 (1):** offences under section 201 (1) and (2) "are prosecuted only upon request" (a complaint by the person affected). **Section 77b (1):** the request must be made within three months, running from when the entitled person learns of the offence and the offender [136].

**The "no participant exception" reading holds.** At the level of the text, nothing in (1) to (5) excepts a person taking part in the conversation (verified by reading the full text). "Of another" (eines anderen) names whose words are recorded: the other speaker. Support from outside the statute, all read today:

1. **German Wikipedia's worked example** has host B secretly record, on his phone, a conversation with visitor A; it concludes "B hat sich nach 201 Absatz 1 Nr. 1 StGB strafbar gemacht, da er das private Gespräch ohne Einwilligung aufgenommen hat" (B committed the offence because he recorded the private conversation without consent). A third person C who posts the file online commits (1) no. 2 [137]. Wikipedia is a secondary source.
2. **LAG Rheinland-Pfalz, judgment of 19 Nov 2021, 2 Sa 40/21** (reported by Haufe, 24 Mar 2022): a cashier secretly recorded on his smartphone a private talk with his supervisor, a conversation he took part in. The court held the offence under section 201 "objectively" met, but that he acted under an avoidable mistake about the law, and both dismissals failed [140]. This is a labour court applying the offence, not a criminal court.
3. **A criminal-defence law firm's page** says "Das Verbot gilt auch, wenn Sie selbst am Gespräch teilnehmen" (the ban also applies when you take part in the conversation yourself), that consent protects "only" if the speaker knew **before** the recording that it would be made, and that agreeing to the conversation is not enough [141]. Secondary; undated.
4. **The Federal Constitutional Court, BVerfGE 106, 28 (9 Oct 2002, 1 BvR 1611/96 and 805/98), section C.II.1.a, p. 40**, says the Basic Law protects against conversations being "heimlich aufgenommen und ohne Einwilligung des Sprechenden ... verwertet", and adds that the legal order shows how seriously it takes this because "die unbefugte Aufnahme des nicht öffentlich gesprochenen Wortes eines anderen auf einem Tonträger gemäß 201 Abs. 1 Nr. 1 StGB mit Strafe bedroht ist" [138]. The case itself was about listening in, not recording (below).

**Does case law treat a participant's own recording differently from a third party's?** What I found, with the confidence of each point:

- **In the statute (inference from the text).** A participant can fall only under (1) no. 1 (recording) and (1) no. 2 (use or passing on). A listener who is not meant to hear the words, using a listening device, falls under (2) no. 1. So the statute does separate them, by who the recorder is, but it exempts neither.
- **Constitutional level.** The Federal Constitutional Court treated both as interferences with the right to the spoken word, a part of the general personality right. In BVerfGE 34, 238 (31 Jan 1973, 2 BvR 454/71), the recording was made without the speaker's knowledge by a party to the conversation (a husband in the case) and passed to the police; the Court called the use of such a tape as evidence an interference with the right and required weighing [139]. In BVerfGE 106, 28 (2002) conversation partners had let third parties listen in through a listening-in device on a telephone; the Court held the right also protects against a conversation partner who, "ohne Kenntnis des anderen eine dritte Person als Zuhörer in das Gespräch mit einbezieht" (C.II.1.a, p. 40). On criminal liability it says (C.II.3.a.aa, p. 44): "Zwar kann die Strafbarkeit ein Anhaltspunkt dafür sein, dass ein rechtlich besonders geschütztes Verhalten betroffen ist. Der verfassungsrechtliche Schutz des allgemeinen Persönlichkeitsrechts richtet sich jedoch nicht danach, ob die Rechtsordnung einen zusätzlichen strafrechtlichen Schutz vorsieht." The same passage also refers to whether a listening-in device is a Abhörgerät under section 201 (2) no. 1 as a question the constitutional protection does not turn on (a tool's summary of p. 44; check the text before citing) [138]. The same decision lists secret taping to identify an anonymous caller who falsely used another's identity as an example of a case where an intrusion can be justified (C.II.4.a.bb, p. 50) [138].
- **Evidence law is where participants and third parties are handled differently**, and it is about admissibility, not about whether the recording is an offence. German Wikipedia lists BGH, 18 Feb 2003, XI ZR 165/02 (NJW 2003, 1727), quoting that use of the testimony as evidence in the civil case "nicht in Betracht" comes [137]; search summaries describe the test as a weighing of interests. I did not read the judgment. German Wikipedia also lists a Bavarian Supreme Court judgment (5 St RR 143/93, 1994) saying use of calls recorded by third parties is inadmissible [137]. Not needed for this project.
- **Criminal convictions of participants:** I found no criminal-court judgment convicting a person for secretly recording a conversation they took part in. Absence in a search is weak evidence. Prosecution needs a complaint within three months [136], so reported cases may be rare (inference).
- **Contrary claims exist.** Several consumer-advice pages say recording a conversation you take part in is not punishable. Those I could open were not German criminal-court sources, and one that appears to be hosted in Austria returned 403 (Austria's rule differs, (M), not checked). I treat the contrary claim as UNVERIFIED, likely a confusion with Austrian law or with the separate rule that listening in is not recording.
- **UNVERIFIED:** a law-firm page cites BGH 10 Nov 2022, 4 StR 91/22 for the idea that an ongoing unauthorised recording is a present unlawful attack for self-defence purposes; I could not find the judgment, so I do not rely on it [141]. German Wikipedia gives two different file numbers for one Hamm decision, so its case list needs checking before citation [137]. A dejure.org list of decisions on section 201 gave titles only, none clearly a participant's own recording [142].

**What it means for the project (inference).** The existing rule (the consent line spoken at the start, every time, in the contributor's own words, on the recording) is the right shape for a German speaker: consent must come before the recording [141] and, because the recording will be shown, should also name the showing. With consent the act is not "without being authorised"; without it, even a recording kept for private use is within (1) no. 1 and showing it publicly within (2) no. 2. Add the contributor's country to the consent sidecar (already in F5a). Which country's law applies to a call between the maker and someone in Germany: UNVERIFIED.
