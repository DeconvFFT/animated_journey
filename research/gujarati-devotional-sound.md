Table in M9; fixture `phone_speaker_haveli_instruments_2026-10-19.csv`. Pass/fail is a model, not a phone: the tone ladder replaces it.
4. **Haveli sangeet, in one paragraph.** The kirtan of Pushtimarg: Braj Bhasha padas by the eight Ashtachhap poets, sung as seva at the eight daily darshans in a dhrupad-and-dhamar-derived style, most of the repertoire in 14-beat dhamar and 7-beat rupak tala, on pakhawaj, tanpura, been, sarangi, harmonium, jhanj and manjira [1][3][4]. The tradition's own pairing of poet to darshan is a loose guide ("singers do not strictly follow this alignment") [1]. The four first-generation poets (disciples of Vallabhacharya) are Kumbhandas, Surdas, Paramanandadas, Krishnadas Adhikari; the four second-generation (disciples of Vitthalnath) are Nanddas, Chaturbhujdas, Govindswami, Chhitswami [2][5]. Their dates are not settled: every source differs, the group's founding is given as 1545 [3] and as 1565 [2], and modern scholars think it "was likely not called the Ashtachap at the time" [2]. All texts are 16th-century and out of every term.
5. **The darshan-to-raga table joins pushtimarg-world's T3; the two sources agree in full on three of eight rows and agree only in part or disagree on the other five.** A peer-reviewed paper of July 2026 lists the ragas per darshan [3]; the fan-portal and temple-blog lists in pushtimarg-world's T3a differ, in part or in whole, for Shringar, Gwal, Utthapan, Bhog and Sandhya [R2]. So the cue map takes a *set per darshan, with its source named*, never one raga; and no raga name ever goes on screen or in a credit (the sibling's rule). The seasonal assignment is thinner still: the sources name six seasons and a pool of 30-odd ragas but give no season-to-raga table (M1.4).
6. **The instruments need `ASK`, not a ruling.** The tradition's own list (Chaturbhujdas's *Shat Ritu Varta* as quoted in [3]) names 36 instruments including shehnai, sarangi, ravab and jhanjh; a 2021 field study names jhanj, harmonium, been, sarangi and pakhawaj, plus duf, upang and kinnari in the spring Hori season [4]. The harmonium is in the sanctum lists [1][4]. The flute is an open conflict: [1] says the bansuri is excluded from temple ensembles (a sentence footnoted to Gaston 1997, not read), while [3] quotes a 36-instrument list that names *Murali* among instruments of divine service; UNVERIFIED either way (M1.5 I-29). ## Findings by question

### M1. Haveli sangeet, the kirtan of Pushtimarg

**M1.1 What it is, and what it is called.**
- A devotional music offered as seva to Krishna in his child form in the havelis of the Vallabha sampradaya; "Haveli Sangeet" is "Hindu temple music" of Vaishnavism, centred on Nathdwara, originating in Govardhan in Braj (Wikipedia, tagged "better source needed" for two sentences) [1]. The Sangeet Galaxy paper (peer-reviewed, July 2026): "Music in this tradition is not intended for performance but is offered as seva" and the pada form is called *Vishnupada*, sung by *kirtaniyas* rather than the *kalawant* of dhrupad [3].
- Wikipedia's *Music of Gujarat* lists haveli sangeet as one classical tradition beside bhajan (classed by theme and by type such as prabhatiya and garba), garba and dandiya raas, padhar, dayro and the Charan and Barot oral forms (doha, sorathaa, chhand) [38].
- The sampradaya's liturgy "contains nearly ten thousand padas by thirty to forty poets including the aṣṭachāp" [10]. Gujarati lay devotees, particularly women, sing *dhol* songs that "are not part of the formal temple Braj liturgy" [10]; so the Gujarati household form and the Braj temple form are two repertoires (pushtimarg-world F0 says the same [R2]).
- **The name "haveli sangeet" is itself contested.** The paper says "Traditionally, the term haveli was not used for Pushtimargeey sangeet; it was first coined by Thakur Jaideva Singh in the 1960s" [3]. A Baroda exponent in the CCSD study says a Pushtimarg singer (named in the study) gave the name at All India Radio when asked for a programme title, and prefers "Pushti Bhakti Sangeet"; an Ahmedabad musicologist in the same study says it was named "in early 1950" and that in Kathiawar "mansions like temples are called Haveli" [4]. Three accounts, none documented. For a credit or a card the word is the family's, so ask (Q13); until then no card says "haveli sangeet" and every cue names its tradition value (`haveli_sangeet`), never "kirtan" alone.
- Not the same thing (watch-out 4): Gaudiya/ISKCON kirtan, Sikh kirtan (dharmic, out), Marathi abhanga, a Hindustani khayal. The CCSD study says haveli sangeet is "bhav pradhan" (expression-led) where classical music is "shastra pradhan", and that a dhrupad singer "will only touch one line each of Sthayi, antara, sanchari, abhog" where the kirtankar sings the whole pada [4]. A 2021 temple-linked voice also admits that "you can find a bit of khayal gayaki ang in Haveli sangeet nowadays" [4]. So even a "haveli" recording may be a blend; the credit names what the metadata says.

- **Formation.** The paper says Vitthalnath founded the group "in Vikram Samvat 1602, i.e., 1545" from "his four best disciples" and "his father's four best disciples" [3]; Wikipedia says it was "established in 1565 AD" and that the poets "lived as contemporaries" from "approximately 1549-1578" and that the earliest mention of the group is in Gokulnath's *Varta* literature around 1610, concluding that the group "was likely not called the Ashtachap at the time" [2]. The Pushti Marg site and the CCSD study agree on the four-and-four split [5][4]. (Wikipedia's Haveli Sangeet page also has a garbled line about "Vishundas" being "first in the group" and Chaturbhujdas replacing him; I do not repeat it [1].)
- **Text status.** All eight are 16th-century poets; every pada's Braj Bhasha text is far out of Indian term (life plus 60) and out of life-plus-70 terms. Two cautions: the *printed edition* a family reads may carry an editor's work, and Sur Sagar attributions are mixed [9]. A card quoting two lines is a text-only cue, and its edition is named in the rights log (hindu-frameworks T8 `text_invocation` row [R1]).

**M1.3 Forms.**
- **Pada** (a short Braj Bhasha hymn in sthayi, antara, sanchari, abhog) is the unit; **kirtan** is the act of singing it as seva; **dhamar** names both a tala (14 beats) and, in spring, a season of songs; **dhrupad** is the root style ("The essential component is dhrupad") [1]. Talas: dhamar (14) and rupak (7) "together about three-quarters of the temple repertoire"; charchari (10) and adi (8) in liturgy; classical dhrupad's chautal is 12 [1]. The 2026 paper adds Chautal, Dhamar, Sooltal, Tevra, Ektal, Jhaptal, "primarily rendered on the pakhawaj" [3]. A bol-style melodiousness ("Bol ang") distinguishes Vishnupada from the layakari of dhrupad [3].
- **Forms the household knows:** the **dhol** (Gujarati women's devotional song, outside the temple liturgy) [10]; **garbi** (Dayaram's lyric form, M2); **prabhatiya** (Narsinh Mehta's dawn songs, M2); **halardu** (lullaby, M3). These are Gujarati forms around the haveli tradition, not part of it.
- **Season.** The paper divides the year into six seasons (Basant, Grishm, Pavas, Sharad, Hemant, Shishir) and names festival occasions (Janmashtami, Radhashtami, Annakut, Holi, Sharad Purnima) [3]; the CCSD study says only that "four seasons have four different types of rendition of music" and that "ragas and lyrics also vary according to different hours of the day" [4]. **I found no source that maps a season to a raga set**; Basant and Malhar appear in the paper's pool of 30-odd names without a placement [3]. Dhamar as the spring form comes via pushtimarg-world (a search digest) [R2]. Season is therefore a `time_of_day_or_season` value with `raga_or_mode` left blank.

Rules for reading the table. **Two columns, always, and never merged:** `composition_status` (the text and the tune as works) and `recording_status` (a particular recording and its performers). **M2.1 Narsinh Mehta (15th century) and "Vaishnav Jan To".**
- *Poet.* Wikipedia: "no consensus among scholars on exact dates", infobox c. 1414 to 1488 (its text and categories disagree: "1481 deaths"; the sibling read 1480); born in Talaja, now in the Bhavnagar district; "the oldest available manuscript of his work is dated around 1612", his works "largely preserved orally"; the prabhatiya genre "is sung in the morning to evoke peacefulness and contentment"; the melodies he sang "cannot be confirmed or reconstructed" [20]. **A poet born in her own district, whose dawn songs are the Gujarati morning genre, is a "hers" fact worth a card only if the family sings him (Q1).** The Saurashtra note adds that two pages tie him to Gopnath Mahadev on the Gulf of Khambhat coast, where he "fasted and meditated by a secluded Shiva lingam" and Shiva then led him to watch Krishna's Rasaleela [R8]: Shiva and Krishna, two of her eight deities, in one story of one poet. That is a picture-and-card matter for that track; for sound it only says the poet is the family's to claim. The brief says "of Junagadh", which is how the Vaishnava Jana To page puts it [21], while the Narsinh Mehta page gives Talaja as his birthplace [20]; both can be true (born in one, associated with the other), and a card says "the 15th-century Gujarati poet-saint" or nothing.
- *"Vaishnava Jana To".* "Gandhi's favorite"; the earliest known manuscript of this poem is dated 1835, with an early-18th-century one reported by K. K. Shastri; printed 1885 in *Prāchīn Kāvya* (the page also dates the first print of Mehta's poems generally to 1852); a 19th-century claim that Dalpatram wrote it "was dismissed" [21]. So the *attribution to the 15th century* rests on tradition and later manuscripts; the text is public domain on any date. **The now-familiar tune is not the traditional morning tune: Narayan Khare, "musician at the Sabarmati Ashram, set it in the 1920s to a semi-classical tune in Mishra Khamaj"** [21]. I could not find Khare's death year on any page (SwarGanga "Not available"; Bharat Discovery gives no dates) [55]; the round-1 follow-up opened three more pages and still found none (Follow-up research, question 1). If he died in 1965 or earlier his tune is out of Indian term; later and it is not. **Status: text PD; tune `unknown` (UNVERIFIED), so a hum of the Khare tune is an S2-until-proved situation.** The sibling reached the same point [R1 N6].
- *Recordings.* M. S. Subbulakshmi's Carnatic concert performances; Asit Desai with Hema Desai and group, *Narasinha Mehta, Uttam Pado* on cassette then CD [21]; a mirror of an older Wikipedia list gives it as Navras Records, 1996, music direction "Aashit Desai", and *Narsinh, Ek Zhanki* (music director Kaumudi Munshi, N. K. Parikh Parivar, 1998) (SR) [43]; films *Narsinh Mehta* (1932, the first Gujarati talkie), *Narsi Bhagat* (1940, ends with the song), and it closes the titles of Attenborough's *Gandhi* [21][20]. **In 2018 the Ministry of External Affairs released a rendition built from artists in "over 124 countries", launched on 2 Oct 2018** (Government of India press pages and The Wire, SR) [22]: a government production with many performers; its reuse terms were not read.
- *Verdict.* Text card yes (edition named). Tune: only if the family's tune is placed. Every named recording is owned.

**M2.2 Dayaram (1777 to 1852 or 1853).** Born 16 Aug 1777 in Chanod (Chandod), died 1853 per Wikipedia and 1852 per Dwyer's SOAS thesis and book (SR); "initiated into Pushtimarg (Brahmasambandha) in A.D. 1803-04" by Gosvami Vallabhalalaji of the Vanamalij temple at Nathdwara; most works are *garbi*, "a lyric song"; *Rasikavallabha* (1838) "strongly" promotes Pushtimarg [23]. **Yes: a Pushtimarg poet whose garbis are both Gujarati and Vaishnava.** Text public domain. Tune: a label credit "Dayaram" on a Saregama garba (*Garbe Ramvane Gori Nisaryan Re Lol*, sung by Kaumudi Munshi) names the *poet* as "Composer" [34], which cannot be a tune credit for a man who died in the 1850s; so the tune is the label's arrangement and the status is `unknown` (treat as `PD_text_owned_tune`). An archive.org library lists "Dayaram krut Shyam ke dhol pad" as six tracks with no performer [44]. I did not verify any famous Dayaram garbi title.

**M2.3 Mirabai (16th century), and her Gujarati padas.** c. 1498 to c. 1546 or 1547; "just a few hundred are believed to be authentic by scholars"; "Only two poems attributed to her have been found in manuscripts dating from before the early eighteenth century"; the most extensive collections are 19th-century; the padas are described as "in the Rajasthani language" [24]. A lay site claims a Gujarati recension "preserved in Saurashtra" (SR, UNVERIFIED) [25]. So: text PD by age; **attribution of any given pada is the problem, and a card that says "Mirabai wrote" needs a scholarly edition or the family's own book.** She is a Krishna bhakta but not a Pushtimarg poet; whether the family sings her is Q1. Do not repeat the Akbar-and-Tansen legend (D-28).

**M2.4 Gujarati Pushtimarg kirtans.** Braj padas by the Ashtachhap (M1) and, in Gujarati homes, the lay *dhol* songs [10]. Text PD (16th century to 19th). Tune: a family's or a haveli's, oral; `unknown`. Recording: any sung recording is a living voice (D-05) and most carry a label's master.

**M2.5 Yamunashtakam and Madhurashtakam (Vallabhacharya, 1478 or 1479 to 1530 or 1531).**
- *Yamunashtakam.* The first of the sixteen Shodasha Granthas, "Eight stanzas to goddess Yamuna" on the list page [12]. Wikipedia glosses the hymn as "an esoteric hymn to the river goddess Yamunā which is recited daily by followers" [11]; that is an encyclopaedia's wording and not how her devotees name her. The maker's word is **Yamuna Maharani** (D-31), so any card, credit or note addresses her as the maker does (Yamuna Maharani, Shri Yamunaji) and never glosses her as "the river goddess" alone (O-49 as review 05 numbers it; no register row yet). Text PD. It may be recited or sung: Wikipedia says recited [11]; the advisor's review says it is sung in family and haveli tunes and on records in tunes that may belong to a named composer, a claim no page I read confirms or denies (Follow-up research, question 3). So **the tune is `unknown` until a specific rendering is placed**, and a sung recording is a living voice (D-05). Card only, and pushtimarg-world's recommendation stands: no Yamunashtakam card unless the maker has the family's printed copy [R2 F8].
- *Madhurashtakam.* "Adharaṃ madhuraṃ vadanaṃ madhuraṃ ..." eight verses, a 16th-century Sanskrit hymn "composed by the Hindu Bhakti saint Vallabha" with a legend of composition at midnight on Shravana Shukla Ekadashi [13]. **It is not among the sixteen**: the Shodasha Grantha list has Yamunashtakam first and no Madhurashtakam [12]; Wikipedia's Vallabhacharya page puts it among other texts attributed to him, some of which are "considered to be either lost or not actually composed by him" [11] (the page does not say which). So the attribution is traditional; say "attributed to Vallabhacharya". Recordings named without label or year: M. S. Subbulakshmi (classical) and K. J. Yesudas (semi-classical) [13]. The only e-text I opened is a volunteer file "to be used for personal study and research ... not to be copied or reposted ... for commercial purpose without permission" (latest update 19 Aug 2004) [14]: **type the card from the family's printed copy, not from that file**, as pushtimarg-world did for the Yamunashtakam [R2 T9].

**M2.6 "Jai Adhya Shakti" (Maa Ambe) and the Shiva aarti.**
- *Jai Adhya Shakti.* The Gujarati text on a panchang site names "શિવાનંદ સ્વામી" in the third verse from the end (read on [27] 2026-10-19; an earlier reading said "a verse before the last", which was wrong) and gives no author, date or place in any note [27]; the page warns that wording differs between printed versions [27]. Popular Gujarati sources and a 2017 social-media post say it was written by a Nagar poet, Shivanand Vamdev Pandya of Surat, in the 16th century (one summary gives c. 1601 at Mandwa on the Narmada), and one source says no dated manuscript has been traced (SR) [28]. **The aarti's own closing verses carry a date.** The fifth verse from the end carries two Samvat years (1657 and 1622 as the fetch tool returned them; one fetch said they are written as Gujarati number words, not digits; the tool would not quote the verse, so I have not read the wording myself) and names Reva, the Narmada [27]. So the "c. 1601 at Mandwa on the Narmada" claim is the text's own signature, not only a 2017 post: by the usual 56/57-year offset Samvat 1657 is c. 1600 to 1601 CE (our arithmetic), and what the second year, 1622, marks is not known to me. **Who Shivanand Swami was stays UNVERIFIED** (the Surat identification is SR [28]); the public-domain lead is stronger, not closed. Text status: public domain if the text's own date is genuine; wording varies between printed versions, so the edition the family reads is what counts, and the verse is to be confirmed against the family's printed aarti book (question 7). Do not print "written by" on a card. Tune: the common aarti tune; arrangements owned. I read no recording credit for it; treat every famous recording as owned.
- *"Om Jai Shiv Omkara".* The closing verse carries "a traditional composer signature naming Swami Shivanand" and names the subject as "Trigunswami"; the page states no date and no language of origin [26]. **Two aartis sharing one signature is a clue to a signature convention, not to a date.** `unknown`; do not call the text PD until a date is found.

- Garba is a circle dance round an image of the goddess or a lamp, part of Navratri (sibling N8) [R1]. The Saregama label's own compilation shows how "traditional" works: of 12 tracks, composer credit "Traditional" on three, "Dayaram" on one, **"Avinash Vyas" on three**, and named composers on five (Ninu Muzumdar, Madhubhai Patel, Ramesh Gupta, Kanti Ashok, Venibhai Purohit) [34]. **M2.9 Halardu (lullaby), the form most likely to be "the tune she sang".** Jhaverchand Meghani (born 28 Aug 1896 in Chotila, died 9 Mar 1947 in Botad) collected folk songs in *Radhiyali Raat* (parts 1 to 4, 1925 to 1942), *Chundadi* (1928 to 1929), *Rutugeeto* (1929), *Halarda* (1929) and others [36]; a Commons scan of *Halarda* says "first published in 1928" and carries the template "public domain in India because its term of copyright has expired" (a volunteer's reading) [36]; a lay summary says many of its lullabies "are hummed by many mothers and Grannies of Gujarat even today" [36]. India: he died in 1947, so 1948 + 60 puts the end of term at 31 Dec 2007; the US treats works published before 1931 as public domain in 2026; the UK and EU are life plus 70 (2018). The Library of Congress holds catalogue records for later commercial lullaby CDs, none online [37]. So **the text of a lullaby in *Halarda* is public domain in India, the US and the UK on my arithmetic; a 20th-century recording or modern arrangement of it is not.** The book contains lullabies he wrote as well as lullabies he collected, and I do not know which are which: T10 question 32.

**M2.10 What the tradition does with Narsinh, Dayaram and the Ashtachhap in the same house.** The CCSD study notes that popular musicians sing "Pyare pag vale" or "Ban Thhan kaha chale Laal" but "never sing the chhap of Nand Das, Sur Das or Paramananda Das" [4]: the signature line (the poet's name inside the last verse) is what a pada's attribution lives in. A cue that cuts a pada before its last line removes the poet's name; the April mix should not.

### M9. The phone speaker: which haveli instruments pass the 50% check, as PASS or FAIL

## Reverence notes, exclusions and ambiguous cases

- **Kirtan is seva, not atmosphere.** Do not use a pada as a bed or a mood; never cut it mid-line (the poet's name is in the last line [4]); never put it under a scene of her being cared for (the Mangla and Sandhya darshans are the parental moods; pushtimarg-world's VR-1 forbids seating her in the svarupa's or Yashoda's place [R2]). The darshan value in the sheet never maps to a life stage; Shayan is never "the present" (R2 T3 header).
- **Name the tradition on every cue.** Excluded as haveli sangeet: Gaudiya/ISKCON kirtan (the sibling's CC0 Antwerp 2013 Hare Krishna recording is out), Sikh kirtan and Gurbani (dharmic, out), Marathi abhanga, a Hindustani khayal, a Swaminarayan kirtan (another sampradaya), a Jain stavan (Palitana is near Bhavnagar; flagged, not used). "Kirtan" alone is not a value.
- **No raga claim.** A drone in a scale is not "Raga Bhairav". Where the sources agree only in part or disagree (five of eight darshans), the cue map carries the set and the source; a credit prints a raga only if the recording's metadata does.
- **Instruments:** the harmonium, sarangi, shehnai, ravab and duf carry flags and are `ASK`; the bansuri/murali is a source conflict ([1] excludes it from temple ensembles, [3] lists Murali; I-29, UNVERIFIED) and a stock Krishna-flute bed is out on Principle 2 and `ASK`, not on the exclusion claim; the tanpura's Mughal-era picture history is a SHARED-LIGHT flag; pakhawaj and jhanj are the unflagged haveli instruments (court use of the pakhawaj UNVERIFIED).
- **Surdas and Pushtimarg.** Modern scholars call his link to Vallabha and the sect "ahistorical" and attribute many Sur Sagar poems to later poets [9]; the sampradaya holds him an initiated disciple. A card says "a pada of Surdas" and nothing about his life.
- **Mirabai.** Attribution of any pada is contested; she is not a Pushtimarg poet; do not tell the Akbar-Tansen legend (D-28). **Narsinh Mehta, Dayaram and Mahaprabhuji** are tradition, not to be paraphrased in cards beyond the family's words. Yamunaji and Mahaprabhuji are named on any card with the honorific the maker uses (O-49 as review 05 numbers it; no register row yet), never as "the river goddess" or "the philosopher Vallabha".
- **The goddesses.** Jai Adhya Shakti, Harsiddhi and Ambe are Shakta registers; the film keeps separate frames and chapters for each deity's register (anyashraya, R2 PR18); a garba circle round a goddess's lamp is worship, not decoration, so a garba never scores a kitchen scene.
- **Shiva's shore.** The Koliyak shrine is Shiva's; a recording of the sea there is fine, a recording of the temple is a haveli-type permission question. Do not fold the Pandava story into the film (the Wikipedia sentence needs a citation).
- **"Shivanand Swami" is a signature in two aartis**, not a documented person; the Surat identification is a 2017 social-media post and popular sites (SR). Do not print "written by" on a card.
- **Santvani and other bhajan albums** (Gujarati Saurashtra bhajans of many saint-poets) draw on saint-poets of several panths; check poet by poet before any use (UNVERIFIED; I saw only album titles in a search result).
- **The 2018 MEA rendition of "Vaishnav Jan To"** features many performers in many traditions; not hers; flag if ever considered.
- **A garba that is really a film song** is an owned composition; a garba she dances to at Navratri is not a cue.
- **AI:** no AI voice for any devotional text in Gujarati, Braj or Sanskrit; no AI-made kirtan, aarti or lullaby; no synthetic "haveli" drone presented as hers (D-16, W-44).
