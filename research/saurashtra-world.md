## Summary (10–15 bullets)

1. **Her district has three Shaiva settings in a row, and one of them is a bridge to Krishna.** Takhteshwar on a hill in the city, Nishkalank Mahadev's five lingas in the sea at Koliyak, and Gopnath Mahadev on the sea cliffs. Two pages confirm the Narsinh Mehta link: at Gopnath he "fasted and meditated by a secluded Shiva lingam ... for seven days" and Shiva then "took him to Vrindavan" to watch Krishna's Rasaleela [27][26]. Shiva leading to Krishna's rasa is two of her eight deities in one place; the poet was born at Talaja, in her district [27].
2. **Her lineage and her sacred places meet at three sites.** Mahaprabhuji's 84 baithaks include Dwarka (Harikund on the Gomti, Mul Gomti, Gopi Talav, Bet Shankhoddhar), Prabhas (the Triveni ghat at Somnath's town), Junagadh, Madhavpur and Jamnagar; none is named in Bhavnagar district (Tagadi, "on the Ahmedabad-Botad Road", is unplaced: UNVERIFIED) [39]. A Dwarka or Somnath frame can therefore be *hers twice* (Shiva or Krishna, and Vallabha); this is a hers_if lead for pushtimarg-world, not a finding about her.
3. **Open imagery exists for most places and almost none for the sea at Koliyak.** Takhteshwar, Gopnath, Somnath, Dwarka, Ambaji (CC0 exterior), Harsiddhi at Koyla and at Ujjain each have Commons files with the licence read (CC BY-SA 3.0 or 4.0, GFDL, CC0, one CC BY 4.0); Commons holds a single Nishkalank file and none of Koliyak's coast. Almost all are CC BY-SA: a share-alike duty if traced (T1 and the imagery section).
4. **The political room has a one-line rule: the sea, the stone and the lamp; no date, no map, no history card, no crowd, no signage.** Somnath is inside a year-long national commemoration (11 Jan 2026 to 11 Jan 2027, reported by a secondary source), the 1990 Rath Yatra began there, and Dwarka's island had a demolition drive in January and February 2025 that went to the High Court [34][35][37]. Ayodhya (the place and the anniversary line) follows the same rule. Rama is Hindu and welcome under D-28; he is not on her list of eight, so nothing of Rama is a default; hers if she says it (O-63, T2).
5. **Home texture gives four strong object-clock candidates, all domestic:** the velan in her hand, the masala dabbo, the thepla tin that travelled, and the hichko if it travelled. The hichko is a Gujarati household staple under that name, with hindolo as a second name for the same swing [47][48]; the Pushtimarg Hindola is a seva swing, never an object clock (T3).
6. **Crafts: seven pass by origin, three are flagged by maker, and the sixteen exclusion rows hold five crafts practised today.** Pass (seven): saathiyo and rangoli, bead torans, Kathipa and Heer embroidery (Kathi landlord caste, Kanbi women), Ahir embroidery and Rabari embroidery (each named by community), carved wooden facades (an OK-note: checked building by building), Sanjhi (Braj Vaishnava; agent-buildable); Mata ni Pachedi is a further OK-note, with its six goddesses. Flagged by maker (three): bandhani (Khatri, Hindu and Muslim), gharchola (Hindu and Jain brides, makers vary), patola (Salvi families, religion not stated in the pages I read). Excluded (T4c, sixteen rows, X1 to X16), of which five are crafts practised today: Rogan, Ajrakh, the Mutwa-Jat-Memon-Node-Gotauvn embroidery group, Pithora (Adivasi; a FLAG row in T4a, counted here once) and "Kutch embroidery" as a blanket term (T4).
7. **The toran is the best Gujarati Hindu frame.** The door toran of mango-leaf pennants (Saurashtra's stylised leaves, CC0 Cooper Hewitt 1945-54-8) is the domestic form of the temple's stone toran (Vadnagar's Kirti Toran, 12th century, Chaulukya: semicircular arches, makara, Kartikeya and Ganesh) [50][45]. It can frame the module-break card (W-04). The cusped arch is flagged for the maker, not ruled out: it is "attested in Hindu temple architecture" yet the first multifoil arches were Umayyad, so its origin is ambiguous [45]; the default frame stays the plain semicircular toran.
8. **Fonts: Noto Sans Gujarati is the safe caption face; Rasa or Baloo Bhai 2 carry a card with Gujarati and IAST in one face.** Hind Vadodara cannot draw Gujarati digits (a Samvat 2083 line falls to FreeSerif), Farsan has no Om, Anek Gujarati resolves only as "Anek Gujarati Medium", Kumar One collapses in libass because its TrueType hinting program fails in FreeType (new: an unhinted copy renders), and Mukta Vaani draws every conjunct unfused whenever the language is tagged Gujarati (new: its `GUJ ` language record lacks the shaping features). Noto Sans Gujarati at FontSize 23 is 0.354 degrees at 35 cm on a 6.1-inch phone in landscape fit (target 0.35, floor 0.30) and 0.431 degrees at FontSize 28 (M, T6).
9. **Words: use whatever her children call her.** Wiktionary: મા has "mother" (informal) as its first sense, then "mum, mummy" (endearing), and "a suffix for goddesses" (used mainly for Amba and Shitala) as a further sense; બા is grandmother, sister and, dated, mom; મમ્મી is a loan from British English [21]. The word is the maker's (O-49, O-61); the only rule is a layout rule: a word for her never shares a frame with a goddess's name or epithet. Saal Mubarak is Persian sal plus Arabic mubarak and is also the Parsi greeting; Nutan Varshabhinandan is the Sanskrit alternative [20].
10. **Drik has a Gujarati calendar for Bhavnagar by geoname id: 1276032** (verified from page headers) [1][2]. The Gujarati year changes at Bestu Varas: the day page reads Gujarati Samvat 2082 Pingala on Mon 9 Nov and 2083 Kalayukta on Tue 10 Nov [4].
11. **The Diwali week has a three-way date disagreement.** Annakut and Govardhan Puja: Mon 9 Nov (Drik Bhavnagar) or Tue 10 Nov (Drik New Delhi, Shubh Panchang); Bestu Varas: Tue 10 (Drik) or Mon 9 (a Gujarati news calendar, the plain "day after Diwali"); Bhai Beej: Wed 11 or Tue 10 [1][3][5][6][7]. Only her household's wall panchang or her haveli's notice decides (O-50, sharpened).
12. **Uttarayan:** the Sun leaves Dhanu at 21:14 IST on Thu 14 Jan at Bhavnagar [4], so Drik prints Makar Sankranti and Uttarayana on Fri 15 Jan [8]; Gujarat flies kites on the 14th with Vasi Uttarayan on the 15th [19]; no 2027 notification yet [17]. The W-10 hard deadline (Fri 15 Jan) sits on this two-day festival: last send Tue 12 Jan, answers to Tue 19 Jan.
13. **Vinchudo (new): the premiere Saturday is a Vinchudo day.** Drik's Bhavnagar page prints "Vinchudo: Whole Day" on Sat 27 Feb and the period runs from 05:14 on 27 Feb to 16:27 on Mon 1 Mar [4][10]; other periods cover Bestu Varas to Bhai Beej (10 to 12 Nov), TD-L2c's afternoon (3 to 6 Jan) and G-DRAFT (30 Jan to 2 Feb). It is a Gujarati panchang caution on new beginnings, with no scriptural basis found [18]; whether her house heeds it for a gathering is a question only the maker can answer (O-57).
14. **Maha Shivaratri is Sat 6 Mar (Maha vad Chaudas) and Shiva is on her list:** the her-turn conversation (W-48) moves to Sun 7 Mar, not Mon 8 Mar (Somvati Amas) [2]. Sat 27 Feb is also the monthly Krishna Janmashtami (Saptami to 21:54, then Ashtami) [4].
15. **Deliverables are in "Templates and deliverables":** T1 places, T2 political-room rule, T3 home texture, T4 crafts matrix and exclusion list, T5 architecture, T6 fonts and caption size, T7 words, T8 the Bhavnagar festival table (54 rows, also as CSV), T9 the W-08, W-10, W-48, W-52 deltas, T10 the gathering-calendar deltas, T11 Gujarat tells for `cinematic-quality` T5, T12 the two saathiyo versions.

## Findings by question

### Q0. Six things the brief assumed that the sources did not support

So the maker is not surprised later:

1. **"Mata ni Pachedi for the Maa Ambe and Harsiddhi chapters."** The one page that names the goddesses painted on the cloth lists Khodiyar, Bahuchara (Becharaji), Vahanvati, Meladi, Visat and Dashama, and Kalika among the Vaghari's main deities; it names neither Ambaji nor Harsiddhi [55]. The cloth is a goddess *register* (a canopy and backdrop behind Mataji's image at Navratri and the Dashama vrat), not a pictorial source for her two goddesses. Khodiyar is on it, which ties it to Rajapara (a place, not her deity).
2. **"Rasa, Shrikhand, Farsan and Kumar One still need their OFL.txt read."** The sibling read them in its D-31 pass (all four carry SIL OFL 1.1, no Reserved Font Name). I re-read the four files independently and the SHA-256 values of all ten Gujarati font files and their `OFL.txt` match the sibling's table [66][R3]. What the sibling could not find, I found: why Kumar One collapses and why Mukta Vaani draws unfused conjuncts (T6).
3. **"Koliyak at low tide at dawn."** Gujarat Tourism gives the visiting window as "Depends upon the tides (Between 10 AM - 2 PM)" and says the sea is strongest on new-moon and full-moon days [22]; one digest says the temple is covered again after 7 pm. A dawn low tide happens on some days only. At the follow-up (F5) one third-party prediction for Bhavnagar city loaded and showed a low water within about 70 minutes of sunrise on four of the 29 days it covers, all near the quarter moons; no official table for Koliyak or Bhavnagar was found. Plan the shot from a published prediction read in the week of the shot, never "on the day".
4. **"Bestu Varas and Annakut fall on one day."** For Bhavnagar they do not, in the sources I read: three readings of the same tithi give two dates (T8, 9 to 11 Nov).
5. **"The Gujarat government's 2027 holiday list."** No official 2027 list has been published that I could find; the General Administration Department's site returned 503 and the third-party lists say the gazette is "expected in late 2026" [17]. Uttarayan is stated as a convention with its sources, not as a notified date. Re-checked 2026-10-19 (F6): still no official list, and the Department's site returned 503 again.
6. **"Palitana's skyline is the district's photograph."** Confirmed as Jain (Shvetambara), about 900 shrines, and flagged for the maker, never used [43]; the Talaja hill that carries Narsinh Mehta's birthplace also carries Buddhist caves and a Jain temple beside a Hindu Khodiyar shrine [42].

### Q1. Places as settings

The full table is T1. What it shows, question by question:

- **What each looks like at the hour the film would use it.** Takhteshwar: a marble temple on a hillock above the city with "splendid views of the city and out onto the Gulf of Cambay", open dawn to dusk, best visited November to February [24]. Nishkalank Mahadev: five lingas on a square platform about a kilometre out in the sea, each with a Nandi facing it, walked to barefoot at low tide [22]; the tide, not the clock, sets the hour. Gopnath: a "secluded seaside spot, an entry point to the Gulf of Khambhat" [26]. Khodiyar at Rajapara: on the bank of Lake Tataliya, about 15 km from Bhavnagar, with walkers for the dawn aarti on Sundays, Tuesdays and full-moon nights [30]. Somnath: Maru-Gurjara temple on the shore, completed May 1951 [33]. Dwarka: five storeys on 72 pillars, spire 78 m, a flag about 15 m long with a sun and a moon [31]. Ambaji: a marble temple at 480 m on the Arasur ridge; the primordial seat on Gabbar hill by 999 steps or a ropeway; **no idol, a Visa Yantra** [32]. Harsiddhi at Koyla: a foothill temple on Koyla hill near Miyani and Porbandar [28]. Ujjain: two lamp pillars with 726 lamp holders [29].
- **Deity and story in two lines** are in T1; the Gopnath row carries the one confirmed Narsinh Mehta association and Harsiddhi the legend with its conflicts (Krishna prayed to Amba before the Jarasandha fight, or a 13th-century merchant of Kutch, Jagadu, built the present temple after the goddess saved his ship; one source calls him a Jain merchant) [28].

The Nishkalank Bhadarvi fair (a new-moon night of Shravan or Bhadra, sources differ) and Ambaji's Bhadarvi Poonam fall in August and September, outside; Navratri at Ambaji and Ujjain is outside. Maha Shivaratri (Sat 6 Mar) is just after the window and will crowd Takhteshwar and Nishkalank (J).

### Q2. The political room

Which places carry a contemporary meaning in a mixed public room, with sources:

- **Somnath.** The present temple was completed in May 1951 [33]. The Ministry of Culture runs "Somnath Swabhiman Parv: 1000 Years of Resilience and Unwavering Faith" as a "nationwide year-long commemoration ... from January 11, 2026 to January 11, 2027" (Nicobar Times, a secondary report; the national broadcaster's page covers only the opening days and frames the event as "not a remembrance of destruction" but "a tribute to resilience, faith and civilisational self-respect") [34]. The 1990 Ram Rath Yatra began at Somnath on 25 September 1990 [35]. Historians read the 1026 raid and its memory differently (Thapar's *Somanatha: The Many Voices of a History*, 2004, digest only) [36]. The premiere falls about seven weeks after the commemoration year ends.
- **Dwarka.** In January and February 2025 a phased demolition drive on Bet Dwarka removed several hundred structures, and the Gujarat High Court dismissed the Muslim jamat's petitions; outlets describe it as encroachment removal or as selective targeting, and the pro-drive Organiser calls it reclaiming the island's sanctity (LiveLaw, The Wire and Organiser appear as digests; I opened none) [37]. Separately, the underwater structures off Dwarka and Bet Dwarka are documented and their dating and link to Krishna's Dvaraka are disputed (digests of 2007 reports and a Wikipedia bibliography) [38].
- **Ayodhya.** The Rath Yatra page records the aim of the campaign and the violence that followed [35]; Drik's Bhavnagar list prints "Ramalala Pratishtha Diwas" on Fri 22 Jan, a fixed Gregorian date [8]. The rule names Ayodhya (the place and the anniversary line), not the deity: Rama is Hindu and welcome under D-28 and is **not** on the maker's list of eight (D-31), so nothing of Rama is a default; hers if she says it (O-63).

**The rule (one line, written once at the top of T2):** *Somnath, Dwarka and anything of Ayodhya appear only as the sea, the temple's own stone and a lamp in her hands: no date of reconstruction, no history card, no map, no crowd, no signage, no flag except the temple's own cloth in motion, and never in a shot that needs a caption to explain it.* "Her devotion, not a position" is the process rule and never appears on screen. The Commons category for Somnath mixes devotional photographs with political-event files (a Prime Minister at a Trust meeting, commemoration chanting): choose by file, never by category [68].

### Q3. Home texture

The list is T3. Findings behind it:

- **The hichko.** "Swings are known as hichko or hindolo in Gujarat" and are "an integral part of Gujarati residences"; swings with back and armrests are "a common sight throughout the state" (a vernacular-furniture record from Devpar, Kutch) [47]; The Week calls it a "quintessential household staple", "hichko" or "jhula", and traces it to a plank, the patlo [48]. A digest said Saurashtra alone uses hindolo; the page I read does not say so (UNVERIFIED). Its **Hindola link**: the Pushtimarg Hindola utsav seats the deity on a swing in the haveli, in Shravan, for 32 days at Nathdwara (the pushti-marg.net list, digest), and Gujarati Vaishnav havelis keep it too (digest) [53]. The home hichko is domestic; the haveli's hindola is a seva article. Both are outside the window, so neither can be filmed in season this year.
- **Otla, chowk, chabutro, jharokha.** The otla is a "raised stone or timber plinth-cum-seat built into the house front", a semi-public meeting point; the chowk is "an internal courtyard, open to the sky"; the chabutro is "an ornamental free-standing tower built specifically to feed birds"; jharokha are "projecting carved timber balconies" [49]. None of the two pages I read describes a Saurashtra house type, the gokh or the paniyaru; those two are Gujarati usage from background knowledge (J) and are marked leads.
- **Toran, saathiyo, rangoli at the threshold.** A Saurashtra toran's pennants "represent mango leaves which are considered auspicious and welcoming" and the 19th-century Cooper Hewitt example's eight pennants are "stylized mango leaves", "symbols of love and fertility" [50][51]. The swastika is "commonly used before entrances or on doorways" and has "a particular association with Diwali" [54]. A Chopda Pujan page's first-page marks are Om, saathiyo, Shubh and Labh (digest) [14].
- **Kitchen.** The masala dabba was brass for centuries, five to seven compartments, an older version with a plate for dried chillies (retailer pages, digest). I found no reliable page for the velan, patlo, tavdi, thepla tin or paniyaru: they are leads (J), and the object-clock test is hers, not the item's.
- **Festival foods by day.** Diwali's faral: chakli, mathiya, sev, mohanthal, besan ladoo, gol papdi; mathiya from moth-bean flour; the Gujarati chakri from rice and chickpea flours (digest) [13]. Bestu Varas meal: undhiyu, ghughra, lapsi, moong dal pakoda, dahi vada [13]. Uttarayan: undhiyu (named from "undhu", upside down, cooked in earthen pots), chikki, jalebi [19]. Sharad Poonam: kheer or masala milk kept in moonlight is documented; **doodh-pauva** (rice flakes with milk and sugar) is attested for the night in one Gujarati-language source, Gujarati Wikipedia's article on Sharad Purnima, as a custom "in many places" (uncited, not tied to Bhavnagar or Pushtimarg) [70]; the pages that describe the moonlight custom name kheer only [15]; whether her house does it is the maker's (O-56). Bhavnagar is "known for its version of Ganthiya and for Jalebi" [41]. Annakut is the Pushtimarg mound (pushtimarg-world). Shivaratri faral is a lead, not read.
- **Uttarayan on the terrace.** Light paper-and-bamboo kites; lines "coated with glue and ground glass" that "become sharp enough to cut skin" [19]. The shouts are voices and stay out (D-05); the kite's flutter and the line's whir do not.
- **Navratri garba and the garbo.** "An earthen pot with holes on the sides, revealing a flame inside" is the lamp at the centre; a picture or statue of Amba may stand there instead [52]. the same page records UNESCO's recognition of garba in December 2023 [52]. The 2026 Navratri ended on Mon 19 Oct, so garba cannot be filmed in season until October 2027 (the sound track's stand-in problem).
- **Which are daily and could be the object clock (W-32, domestic only):** velan, masala dabbo, thepla tin, tavdi, a named steel or brass vessel, the hichko if it moved with her. **Which are a chapter's opening hands (W-33):** the hand on the hichko's chain, the velan on the patlo, threading or fixing the toran, drawing the saathiyo, spooling the manja, crimping a ghughra. **Never the object clock:** the garbo, the home mandir's cloth, a Thakorji article, a Tulsi planter in worship, the Hindola.

### Q4. Crafts by community

The matrix and exclusion list are T4. The findings that decide each row:

- **Mata ni Pachedi.** Practised by "a handful of Vaghari community chitaras"; the Vaghris "also call themselves as Devipujak"; about 300 years ago Chhipa printers, "during a period of economic deficiency", sold their wooden blocks to a Vaghri trader of Viramgam, whose family later moved to Ahmedabad; black from iron oxide and jaggery, red from tamarind-seed extract, washed in the Sabarmati; the cloth is a canopy and backdrop for Mataji at Navratri and the Dashama vrat [55]. The Chhipa printers' religion is not stated on the page (UNVERIFIED), so the origin is Hindu goddess cloth with one ambiguous predecessor: OK-note.
- **Pithora.** Rathwa (Panchmahal and Chhota Udaipur) and Bhil and Bhilala (Madhya Pradesh); a lekhar paints and a badwa "reads" it; the consecration "begins with a goat sacrifice"; GI tag 2021 (digest) [61]. Adivasi ritual art: flagged, out by default.
- **Kathi, Ahir and Rabari.** The Kathis are "a group of clans in peninsular Kathiawar", "originally sun-worshippers, but now also worship general Hindu deities and western Indian goddesses"; Swaminarayan and Satyanarayana movements have attracted followers [58]. "Women of the Kathi landlord caste were the first to practice embroidery in the Kathipa style" (Cooper Hewitt) [50]; Heer bharat (silk floss darning) is its core, Kanbi women the most prolific embroiderers, Mochi chain stitch absorbed (digest) [58]. The Ahir are "a Hindu community who believe they are descendants of Lord Krishna" (Dastkari Haat Samiti) and Ahir culture is "closely associated to the worship of Krishna and Shiva" [56][58]. The Rabari are "a large community of Hindu pastoralists" [56]. **"Kutch embroidery" as one term mixes communities:** the same sources name Mutwa ("about 20 Muslim clans commonly known as Banni Muslims"), Sindhi Memon ("the Muslim community in Kutch"), Node ("smaller Muslim communities of Kutch") and Gotauvn ("Muslim pastoral people of 11 villages") beside Jat, Meghwar and Soda Rajput work whose religion the pages do not state [56][57].
- **Bead torans.** "Bead torans are made by rural women of Saurashtra who embellish them with colorful glass beads" (a retailer listing, digest) and the toran hung above a doorway is "a sign of welcoming" [50]: pass by use and region; the maker community of a given piece is asked at the piece.
- **Patola.** "Salvi families of Patan"; only two or three families still weave it; GI in 2013 or 2014 (sources differ); a Jain text (the Kalpasutra) is cited for the technique; **the pages I read state no religion for the Salvis** (digests) [60]. Flagged by maker, and the question is not settled by my sources.
- **Bandhani and gharchola.** Work in Gujarat "exclusively carried out by the Khatri community of Kutchh and Saurashtra" (Wikipedia, religion not stated) [59]; a second page says "both Hindus and Muslims" (digest) [59]; Jamnagar is the Saurashtra centre; a gharchola is a red bandhani sari with a zari grid of 9, 12 or 52 squares, "worn by Hindu and Jain merchant communities of Gujarat at the time of marriage" and given by the mother-in-law; intricate tying is done in Kutch and dyeing in Jamnagar (digest) [59]. Use is Hindu; the **maker** of a given piece is the flag.
- **Rogan and Ajrakh.** Rogan survives in one Khatri family of Nirona, "over eight generations", "400-year-old" (the family's own page does not state religion; Wikipedia's reference list and other pages call it a Muslim family) [62]. Ajrakh: Khatri printers, the Khatri Muslim community "widely described as the main practitioners"; the name is "commonly traced to Azrak, which means blue in Arabic"; relocated to Ajrakhpur after the 2001 earthquake (digests) [63]. Out as practised today.
- **Sanjhi.** A stencil-cut paper and powder art of Mathura and Vrindavan, "16th and 17th centuries", linked to Krishna's leelas, with the Vallabhacharya sect and Vaishnava temples; temple Sanjhi is made "during the Pitru Paksha in Ashwin"; folk Sanjhi is "traditionally practiced by women"; cut with "small, delicate, custom-made scissors"; GI tag (year not read; a digest says 2023) [64]. Neither Gujarat nor Nathdwara is named on the page. At the follow-up (F4) the only Pushtimarg-specific page I could open, a 2021 personal blog, described Sanjhi at Nathdwara and said it "is done in other pustimarg havelis" without naming one, so whether a Gujarati haveli makes it stays UNVERIFIED (a question for pushtimarg-world) [75]. As SVG cut-work it is the most agent-buildable of all (T4).
- **Carved wooden facades.** Wood carving is associated with the Hindu Suthar community across Patan, Kadi, Ahmedabad and Nadiad (digest); the pol houses mix "Hindu and Jain figurines, Muslim abstract and geometrical motifs, as well as European, Persian, and Maratha influences" (digest) [65]. Bhavnagar's Darbargadh keeps carved windows and doors, an eastern wing with "a torana with elephants, Krishna with a cow, dvarapalas" and a western wing with Indo-Saracenic jaalis [44]. Each building is checked; none is assumed.
- **Mirror work as a blanket Gujarati style** is out; each piece names its community.

### Q5. Architecture

The element table is T5. Decisions: the **Chaulukya torana** passes as a Hindu temple gateway (Kirti Toran, Vadnagar, 12th century: "semi-circular arches", Kirtimukhas, Kartikeya flanked by Ganesh and Makara [45]), but the page does not discuss scalloped arches, so the sibling's E2 is unchanged by it. The Wikipedia page on the multifoil arch says "The cusped arch is attested in Hindu temple architecture" (Martand, 8th century, Kashmir; Pandrethan, 10th century) and also that "the first multifoil arches were developed by the Umayyads"; Modhera's cusped arches of 1026 to 1027 appear only in digests [45]. So the origin is ambiguous, and the maker's rule for an ambiguous origin is to flag it for the maker, not to rule it out: the default frame is the pillar-and-lintel toran with makara or kirtimukha ends and a plain semicircular arch, and a multifoil or cusped lintel is a FLAG until the maker decides (one question, T5: accept the Kashmir-and-Chaulukya argument, or keep the plain semicircular toran only?). **Stepwells:** Rani ki Vav (Patan; Queen Udayamati, in memory of Bhimadeva I, 11th century, ten avatars of Vishnu, UNESCO 2014; traditional date 1063, a later calculation) passes as Chaulukya and Hindu; Navghan Kuvo (Chudasama king Ra Navghan, 1025 to 1044, other scholars propose Kshatrapa or Maitraka dates) and Adi Kadi Vav (dated from the 10th to the 15th century) pass on dynasty and fail on date, so each needs one more source (digests) [46]. **Flagged:** the Gohil palaces (Darbargadh: western side Indo-Saracenic, jaalis and Roman-villa columns; Nilambag Palace of 1894 by William Emerson; the Barton Library of 1895 with Gothic arch windows; the Town Hall of 1932) [41][44]; **Gangajalia** (1893), a Hindu temple to Ganga Devi designed by Sir John Griffith with a chhatri (E4) [41]; **Ganga Deri**, a state-protected monument that one heritage-walk digest calls the "Taj Mahal of Gujarat" (UNVERIFIED: the walk's own page redirected to an unrelated domain and was not opened): out on that comparison alone (E5); **Bohra havelis** of Sidhpur and Kapadvanj (Dawoodi Bohra, Victorian, Baroque, Art Deco and Islamic motifs, digest): out [65].

### Q8. The festival year for Bhavnagar

Table T8 (54 rows; CSV in the fixtures). **Source and method.** Drik Panchang's Gujarati calendar page takes a geoname id: Bhavnagar is **1276032** (its page headers read "Bhavnagar, Gujarat, India", 21 45 46 N, 72 09 11 E) [1][2]; I read its 2026 and 2027 Gujarati calendars, the monthly festival pages for November, December, January and February, the Vinchudo pages and eight day pages [3][4][8][9][10][11]. The brief's "printed Gujarati panchang" was not available to me: the two Gujarati-language pages I read (Shubh Panchang, which names no city, and Gujju Samachar's calendar, "based on panchangs recognised for Gujarat") disagree with Drik on three Diwali-week dates [6][7]. Where it matters, her household's wall panchang and her haveli's notice win over any website (the same rule as [R2]). Drik's pages state no reuse licence and claim copyright in "All Images and data" (F2): the dates are restated as facts with their source and read date, and nothing is copied.

- **The Gujarati reckoning.** Gujarat is amanta: the month ends on Amavasya, so Aso vad Amas is Sun 8 Nov and Kartak sud Padvo follows; the same day is Kartika or Ashwina depending on the purnimanta or amanta label (Drik's day page prints both) [4]. Month names used in T8: આસો (Aso), કારતક (Kartak), માગશર (Magshar), પોષ (Posh), મહા (Maha); sud is the bright half, vad the dark half; tithi names in Gujarati script (એકમ or પડવો, બીજ, ચોથ, પાંચમ, છઠ, સાતમ, આઠમ, નોમ, દસમ, અગિયારસ, બારસ, તેરસ, ચૌદસ, પૂનમ, અમાસ) are from the Drik Gujarati list's transliteration and general knowledge and need a check against the printed panchang. **Vikram Samvat 2083** is printed from 19 March 2026 in the Chaitra reckoning (a digest of a Sandesh article) [69], but the Gujarati year changes at Bestu Varas: Drik's day page reads Gujarati Samvat 2082 Pingala on Mon 9 Nov and 2083 Kalayukta on Tue 10 Nov [4], and Shubh Panchang prints "ગુજરાતી સંવત: ૨૦૮૩" on 10 Nov [6].
- **The Diwali-week disagreement (T8, 9 to 11 Nov).** Pratipada runs from 12:31 on Mon 9 Nov to 14:00 on Tue 10 Nov at Bhavnagar [4]. Drik Bhavnagar prints Govardhan Puja and Annakut on Mon 9 and Bestu Varas on Tue 10; Drik New Delhi prints Annakut on Tue 10 from the same tithi times (Delhi sunset 17:31 against Bhavnagar's 18:01); Gujju Samachar prints Bestu Varas on Mon 9, "the day after Diwali", and Bhai Beej on Tue 10; Shubh Panchang prints the new year and Annakutotsav on Tue 10 [1][3][5][6][7]. ### T2. The political room: one rule, one card rule, per-place handling

**The rule (written once):** *Somnath, Dwarka and anything of Ayodhya appear only as the sea, the temple's own stone and a lamp in her hands; no date of reconstruction, no history card, no map, no crowd, no signage, no flag except the temple's own cloth in motion, and never in a shot that needs a caption to explain it.* "Her devotion, not a position" lives in the shot brief's `motif_check` and in the maker's decision sheet; it is never on screen.

**The card rule.** A card about any of the three names the place and nothing else (or says nothing); it carries no year, no verb of conquest or recovery, no number of years, no "ancient", no "rebuilt". If the card needs a sentence, the place is dropped.

| Place or image | Allowed | Out |
|---|---|---|
| Somnath | The shore at sunset; the temple at dawn or moonset from a CC BY-SA 4.0 file chosen singly; a lamp | Any file of a ceremony, a Prime Minister, a commemoration banner or chanting event (the Commons category holds such files); the 1951 date; the Kartak Poonam crowd |
| Dwarka | The Gomti ghat steps at dusk; the temple's cloth flag in motion at a distance | Bet Dwarka; bulldozers, demolition, or "submerged city" dive footage or maps; a held close-up of the flag (a composition rule for every Dwarka shot, not a ruling on the sun and moon, which pass on origin: P06) |
| Ayodhya | Nothing: the place and the anniversary line stay out (the rule above) | The "Ramalala Pratishtha Diwas" line on Drik's list (Fri 22 Jan); the saffron flag (hindu-visual E25) |
| Rama | Hindu and welcome under D-28; not on her list of eight (D-31), so nothing of Rama is a default; hers if she says it (O-63) | Nothing is out because of the deity; the Ayodhya items above stay out |
| Any of them | One lamp lit before the frame, no figure (P1 default) | A deity figure moving; a crowd; a politician; a map |

What the sources say: the swastika is "commonly used before entrances or on doorways of homes or temples" and has "a particular association with Diwali", drawn in rangoli or made of lamps outside Hindu houses [54]; at Chopda Pujan the first page of the account book is marked with the swastika, Om, Shubh and Labh in kumkum or sandalwood paste (digest) [14].
