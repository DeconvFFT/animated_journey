## Summary

1. **Free question sets exist but are small tools, not scripts.** StoryCorps' Great Questions page has no licence text and says "We encourage you to use the ones you like and to come up with your own" [1]; StoryCorps itself says a ~40-minute session cannot cover a life and advises 5-10 questions per session [2]. The genuinely texture-producing items are the sensory, ritual, object and "what did it look/smell/sound like" ones (StoryCorps' own tip 8 is "What did your kitchen smell like when you were growing up?") [3]. The work/achievement items are résumé questions and are cut or transformed in Deliverable 1.
2. **McAdams' Life Story Interview is borrowable as structure, not as script.** Its skeleton is: 2-7 titled life chapters; key scenes (peak, nadir, turning point, earliest memory, childhood, adolescent, adult, one more); a life challenge; positive and negative influences; positive and negative futures; a life theme [6]. The official Northwestern URL returned 404 on 2026-10-18, so I worked from a public Northwestern summary [6] and a published adaptation [5]. "Redemption" is a finding about how many people narrate, with a mixed evidence base on well-being [7]; it must not become a template we force a life into.
3. **Prompt types, ranked by evidence.** Strongest lab evidence: smell (odour-cued memories are older, vivid, emotional, but rare) [41]; music from about ages 15-30 (about 70% of "bump" songs evoked a memory vs 44% of others in one older-adult study) [46]. Moderate: photo elicitation (argument is neurological and comes to me second-hand) [45], go-along walks [44]. Practitioner consensus without experiments: open-ended "tell me about", silence, "tell me more", asking for sensory detail, objects and photos as prompts [3][10][11][36]. Ordinary-day methods have good provenance (Mass-Observation day diaries, life-history calendars) but no head-to-head test against other prompts [50][66].
4. **The middle decades are the hardest to recall (inference from a cultural life-script study).** People share a "life script" with few anchor events after about age 35 and those are mostly negative [47]. Expect the subject's 35-55 era to be thin in memory and rich in documents, relatives' observations and routine. Use the Tuesday walkthrough, a life-history calendar and records there.
5. **Surprise mode has a 70-year precedent with documented failure modes.** "This Is Your Life" built a life from spouse, relatives and friends under secrecy: code names, small circles, feasibility reports weighing "points for and against" each subject, memos shredded [28][29]. It still had leaks (a grandson, a note with a phone number), a walk-out (Danny Blanchflower, 1961), a subject who suspected an affair because his wife was being secretive, relatives with second thoughts, and over 80% of proposed subjects rejected at the production conference [28][29][30][31].
6. **Half the literature does not apply in surprise mode.** Without the subject: no LSI, no subject photo/object elicitation, no go-along, no narrator consent or narrator review before recording, no ethical-will or dignity-therapy style prompts. What still applies: relative interviews, documents, objects, recorded voice already in existence, triangulation, the inventory, and a post-reveal veto window (inference).
7. **Choosing which milestones to dramatise.** Biographers sample rather than exhaust (Strachey: "a brevity which excludes everything that is redundant and nothing that is significant") [48]; Woolf separated vivid "moments of being" from the "nondescript cotton wool" of ordinary days [49]; editors protect emotional truth first (Murch's Rule of Six weights emotion 51%) [56]; Pixar whittled 30-40 minutes of material to about 4.5 minutes and cut a slapstick beat that tested badly [14][13]. I propose four treatments: dramatise / glimpse / mention / hold.
8. **Up "Married Life" at beat level (from a student's timecoded table [17]).** About 4:05 from wedding flash to fade. The audience gets 77 seconds of accumulation before the first blow. The first blow lasts about 10 seconds, then Carl immediately brings the Adventure book. Three jar-breaking setbacks take about 8 seconds. The longest quiet beat (24 s, Carl at the window, then the painting) is a thought, not an event. Ellie's death is never shown; the cut goes from the hospital to the church steps. The kiss appears three times, the hill twice, "watching her" repeatedly, and the same music theme changes tempo and timbre [17][14][16].
9. **Second exemplar: StoryCorps' Danny & Annie.** Two rituals set up in the 2004 interview (a proposal call every April 22, answered yes 25 times; a daily handwritten "romantic weather report") are inverted in the 2006 final interview (Annie reads his Valentine's letter; she will walk out behind the casket alone). After Danny died, Annie read one listener condolence letter each day in place of his daily note [20][21][22]. Hard chapter told by the people living it, in their words, with their consent, and with texture first.
10. **Hard chapters: use a gate, not a vibe.** Brooker's "chest of drawers" (top: freely shared; middle: shared inside trust; bottom: family secrets, trauma, not for public work) is the clearest published sorting tool [34]. Add: pre-talk about the pain of remembering [33], subject controls order and pace, breaks, review before publication, "don't leave them worse off than when you met them" [54], and a no-push rule from life-story work with older people [35][36].
11. **Recording for a non-technical maker.** A phone is enough if: quiet soft room, phone on a stand or folded cloth 6-12 inches from each mouth (5-6 inches if single speaker), Do Not Disturb, iPhone Voice Memos set to Lossless or Android at least 128 kbps, a spoken slate at the start, two copies straight away, and a time-coded log [10][37][38]. Transcribe locally with Whisper (MIT licence) so private audio does not go to a third party [39][40]. Do not use the StoryCorps app/archive for private material: their terms say private interviews can still be retained and accessed by StoryCorps, partners and the Library of Congress [4].
12. **What goes wrong in family tributes (thin evidence).** I found no systematic survey. The recurring items in vendor and practitioner guidance are: too long, only photos and no stories, same effect on every photo, skipping family review ("hard feelings"), uneven coverage of family members, unlicensed music [70][71]. From oral-history and memoir sources: no consent trail, tiring elderly narrators, equipment failure, disputes about "whose story it is" [10][11][55][34]. Surprise adds leaks and suspicion [28][30].
13. **Photo asymmetry (few childhood photos, thousands recent) is a design input, not a defect.** Family photo archives skew toward nostalgic, celebratory moments and away from sorrow and routine [58]. Cover early years with places, objects, voices and maps; sample recent photos by theme, not volume; clearly mark any reconstruction (charter principle 4). Animated documentaries (Flee, the StoryCorps shorts) show that research-grounded reconstruction works [24][26].
14. **The decision that gates everything is surprise vs together.** My recommended default is a hybrid ("consent-light": the subject knows a project exists and agrees to a few story sessions; the form and content stay secret), with a full-surprise runbook ready if the maker chooses it. The reasoning is in "What this means for Life Journey".

### Q2. How do oral historians get a 50+ subject past the "official version" to sensory detail? Which prompt types have evidence or consensus?

Method consensus (all primary practitioner guides):
- Ask open-ended questions and probe for detail ("Describe...", "Tell me more about that", "How often...?"); ask one question at a time; use "the silent question. Keep quiet and wait"; ask for definitions of the obvious [10].
- Ask for the specific, not the general. ISJL's example: "What do you remember most about the customers who visited?" invites more than "Who shopped at the store?"; repeat the narrator's last statement to prompt more [36].
- Do not rush silence: "Silence is okay"; the Baylor manual says silence "is an integral, important part" [10][36].
- Give the narrator room for a long answer; nod instead of "uh-huh" because audible sounds contaminate the recording [11][12][10].
- Studs Terkel's famous simplest prompt was "And what happened then?"; he reportedly never used a prepared list (secondhand reports, snippet only) [60].
- Alessandro Portelli's central claim is that oral history "tells us less about events than about their meaning", so "errors" are data (as summarised in search results; I did not open the essay) [64]. For us: do not rush to correct the "official version"; log it, then look for the scene underneath.

My working technique for the "official version" (inference, built from the above): treat habitual narration ("we always...") as texture to be explored and episodic narration ("one time...") as scenes to be captured, and ask for both: "What was a normal X like?" then "Tell me about one time it wasn't normal." Return a second time to the same period through a different door (object, map, song) rather than repeating the question.

Prompt types, evidence and use:

| Prompt | Evidence / consensus | Notes for a 50+ subject and 70-80s relatives |
|---|---|---|
| **Smell** | Lab evidence: odour-cued autobiographical memories are limbic, old (mostly first decade), vivid, emotional, and rare (fewer memories than word or picture cues); naming the odour helps recall [41]. | Ask "what did it smell like?" in words rather than hoping a sniff works. Real smell kits are optional and can surprise someone emotionally (inference). |
| **Music / songs** | Music-evoked memories cluster in the reminiscence bump (about ages 15-30); in a study with older adults about 70% of bump-period songs evoked a memory against 44% of non-bump songs [46]. | Use 20-30 second clips from about ages 12-30; ask "where were you?" Songs the subject has played to death since may carry later memories (inference). |
| **Photos** | Photo elicitation is an established social-science method; Harper's argument is that visual processing is evolutionarily older than verbal, so photos surface different material (I saw this second-hand, not Harper's own text) [45]. Veterans History Project guidance: showing photos and documents can jog memory (snippet only) [12]. | Pick 6, not 60. Ask what is outside the frame, who took it, what happened just before and after. Warning: family photos over-represent celebration and nostalgia and under-represent sorrow and routine [58]. |
| **Objects** | Practitioner consensus in life-story work: use photos, videos, music and objects as icebreakers [35]; VHP archives scans of objects and documents with logs [11]. | Ask each relative to bring one object and say where it lived. |
| **Place / go-along** | Kusenbach (2003) argues go-alongs access meanings of place that sit-down interviews miss: environmental perception, spatial practices, biographies, social architecture, social realms [44]. | Walking with 70-80s relatives may not be feasible: do the house tour at their kitchen table, or use a sketched map. |
| **Maps** | Sketch-map elicitation is used in oral-history projects (Carleton University; artist Marlene Creates' memory maps), with the interviewer drawing their own first (snippet only) [65]. | No controlled evidence found. Cheap and good for the route-to-school, house layout, and where-everyone-sat questions. |
| **Ordinary-day walkthrough** | Mass-Observation asked volunteers to record "everything they did" from waking to sleeping on 12 May 1937 and monthly through 1937; over 500 people took part in the 12th-day series, and it is still an annual call (snippet only) [50]. Perec's call to question "what happens every day and repeats every day" is the literary parallel (snippet only) [51]. | No experiment shows it beats other prompts. It is the best fit for the texture inventory, so I recommend it as the core prompt for the 35-55 era. |
| **"I remember" lists** | Brainard's 1970 book and Perec's homage use a fixed opener and short fragments; Kenneth Koch used it in teaching (snippet only) [52]. | Fast, low-pressure, good for warming up elderly narrators and for building a texture list in 10 minutes. |
| **Sensory roadmaps** | An Oral History Review author argues five-senses prompts surface more evocative stories than interviews centred on major events (snippet only) [66]; Maynooth's method uses five senses prompts, aimed at participants who faced discrimination or trauma (snippet only) [66]. | Practitioner claim, not an experiment. |
| **Lie down, eyes closed, present tense** | Flee's director says the technique comes from his radio interviewing, to bring the subject back to a specific situation (NPR via snippet) [27]; the film took roughly 20 interviews over 3-4 years [26]. | Single practitioner. Only with a subject who wants it; not for a surprise project. Not for painful material unless the subject has led there. |

Date anchoring (separate from prompting): the life history calendar, a grid of years by domains (where lived, work, relationships, children), improved recall of dates in longitudinal research (snippet only) [67]. Landmark public events and age anchors serve the same purpose (inference).

Two cautions: (a) the memory "bump" means the subject will be vivid for ages 10-30 and thin for 35-55 [46][47]; (b) interviewing a couple or siblings together can raise recall for shared memories but outcomes vary with how the pair talks (snippet only) [59]. Practical rule (inference): interview relatives separately first for facts and contradictions, then together for banter and texture.

### Q3. If the project is a surprise, what is the proven method and what goes wrong?

**Proven method: "This Is Your Life"** (US television 1952; UK 1955) [30][31]. Its production practice is the best-documented example of assembling a life from other people without the subject knowing:
- **Choose with a written case.** Each researcher wrote a "feasibility report" (a "feeza") listing points for and against a subject plus a possible "spotlist" of guests [28]. More than 80% of suggested names were rejected, mostly for filming difficulties or missing key figures from the subject's past [29].
- **Start at the centre.** Researchers first phoned a close contact (spouse, parent, agent, business partner) and asked for "the strictest confidence" [28]. Family and friends were "usually very good at keeping their mouths shut" and the secret leaked "only 4 times" in Ralph Edwards' recollection [31].
- **Keep the circle small, and give things code names.** Codenames were deliberately illogical; "Not even the managing director... or the cameramen and technicians are let into the secret"; memos were shredded once memorised [28][29].
- **Cover stories.** For instance a researcher posing as collecting information on another person until the last minute [29]. Cover stories created their own risks (see below).
- **Use insiders to unlock others.** Subjects' spouses or friends often did the set-up (Ronnie Barker's wife; John Le Mesurier for Hattie Jacques) [30].

**Failure modes, all documented:**

| Failure | Example | Lesson (inference where marked) |
|---|---|---|
| Leak through a family member | Bernard Braden's six-year-old grandson revealed it at lunch; programme shelved for over a decade [28] | Anyone who may be around the subject informally is a leak path. Brief children and elderly relatives with one line, or leave them out of the circle until late. |
| Leak through a physical clue | Ronnie Barker found a note with a phone number and the name "Brian"; the researcher answering said "This Is Your Life office" [28]. Derek Nimmo: anonymous note slipped under the dressing-room door [28]. | Never write the project name where the subject may see it; use a neutral label and one shared folder (inference). |
| Secrecy damages the relationship | Barker became upset by his wife's secrecy and suspected an affair; programme cancelled, he said he never wanted to be featured [30] | Decide in advance what the secret-keeper will say. A suspicious partner is a risk to the subject, not only to the plan (inference). |
| The subject refuses | Danny Blanchflower, live, 6 Feb 1961: "Oh no it's not", walked away; the BBC substituted another episode; he later said he never regretted it [30] | The subject owns the right to say no, and a surprise has to have a plan for that outcome. |
| Pre-agreed opt-out | Maureen Lipman and her husband had agreed neither would be a subject; both appeared as guests on others' shows [30] | Ask an insider early: "Would they love this or hate this? Have they ever said?" |
| Relatives with second thoughts or objections | Nigel Havers' parents; Peter Davison's then-wife objected to a finale and it was dropped [28][30] | Treat relatives' comfort as a real veto on their own appearance. |
| Contacting a relative without checking first | A 1996 Sunday Mirror report said a planned Arthur Mullard edition was pulled after researchers contacted his eldest son; the same report made serious abuse allegations against Mullard (reported claims, not verified) [30] | Do not cold-contact relatives who may have a difficult history with the subject without checking with a trusted insider first (inference). |

**Other failure modes for our setting (inference):**
- *Wrong facts.* Memory is unreliable; family legends harden. Keep a confidence column in the inventory and a list of "10 things only the subject can confirm" for a post-reveal corrections round.
- *Subject dislikes being the object.* Some people dislike being the centre of attention or having private history known to relatives; the mitigations are the pre-agreed opt-out question, a pre-reveal check with their closest confidant, and a post-reveal veto window before any wider sharing.
- *Sensitive material surfaced by well-meaning relatives.* Do not ask relatives to disclose bottom-drawer material (Brooker) [34]; log it as "do not use".

**Which methods fail without the subject:** LSI chapters and key scenes; subject-led photo/object/map/song elicitation; go-along; narrator consent and the OHA expectation of narrator approval before release [8]; dignity-therapy or ethical-will prompts; a living-subject member check [53].
**What still works:** every relative interview (Deliverable 1, R banks); documents, objects, places visited by the maker; the inventory template; found voice (old voicemails, home video, letters, tapes: recorded audio that already exists) (inference); triangulation with a confidence field; a proxy member-check by one or two people who know the subject well; and a deliberate "reveal as consent point" (see Surprise-mode runbook).

### Q4. How do biographers and documentary editors choose which of 30+ milestones to dramatise vs mention, find the through-line, and check it is true?

What the sources say:
- **Sample, don't exhaust.** Strachey's preface: the biographer "will row out over that great ocean of material, and lower down into it, here and there, a little bucket"; and "a becoming brevity — a brevity which excludes everything that is redundant and nothing that is significant — that, surely, is the first duty of the biographer" [48].
- **Two kinds of day.** Woolf distinguished "moments of being" from "non-being", the "nondescript cotton wool" of routine; she never defines "being" precisely, so critics supply examples [49]. For us: a *scene* is a moment of being; *texture* is the cotton wool made visible as pattern.
- **The subject's own chosen scenes.** McAdams' protocol asks the subject to pick the scenes (peak, nadir, turning point...) [6]; what they choose is itself evidence of the through-line. Compare it with the scenes relatives pick; divergence is information.
- **Emotion first, in cutting.** Murch's Rule of Six: emotion, story, rhythm, eye-trace, 2D plane, 3D space, with emotion weighted 51%; "If you have to give up something, don't ever give up emotion before story" (as reported by two secondary sites; the percentages are Murch's judgement, not a measurement) [56].
- **Spine and point of attack.** Documentary Storytelling's fifth edition stresses a simple throughline that need not be a hero's journey, and a "point of attack" (entry point) [57].
- **Testing and whittling.** Docter: 30-40 minutes of material cut to about 4.5 minutes; the team removed dialogue ("the less we had the more emotional it felt"); an early slapstick "punching game" drew silence in tests and was replaced; the miscarriage beat was cut, then restored when the film "felt less emotional" without it [14][13].

A decision procedure for 30+ milestones (inference, assembled from the above; test it on the vertical slice):

| Test | Question | If yes |
|---|---|---|
| Turn | Did this change what the person did, believed, or who was around them? | Candidate to dramatise |
| Motif | Does it touch the through-line (a recurring object, place, phrase, tension)? | Dramatise or glimpse |
| Scene | Is there a concrete scene (place, people, an object, one sensory detail) we can render? | Needed for "dramatise"; otherwise glimpse or mention |
| Voice | Do we have the subject's or a relative's own words or sound for it? | Strongly favours dramatise |
| Dignity | Is it middle- or bottom-drawer? Has the right person said yes? | Gate (see Q7) |
| Variety | Does the piece already have a scene like this? | If yes, glimpse or merge |

Treatments:
- **Dramatise:** a scene of 45-120 seconds with a place, people, an object, sound.
- **Glimpse:** 3-10 seconds: one image or sound, a line of voice.
- **Mention:** a label, a date on a map, a line in a list (the "mention layer" can carry 15-25 milestones cheaply).
- **Hold:** consciously left out for now (not forgotten).

**Finding the through-line** (inference): look for (1) things the person *keeps doing* (verbs and objects recurring across interviews and decades), (2) a standing tension (what they wanted vs what was in the way), (3) the same trait named unprompted by two or more independent relatives with different scenes, (4) the subject's own chapter titles [5][6]. Write 3 candidate through-lines, each with 3 verbatim quotes and timestamps.

**Check it is true to the person, not imposed** (inference, using member-checking logic [53]):
1. *Own-words test:* the through-line words appear in the subject's or relatives' transcripts, not only in ours.
2. *Independence test:* at least two independent witnesses, different scenes.
3. *Counter-example test:* find the scene that contradicts it; if there is none, we have not looked.
4. *Would-they-say-it test:* in collaborative mode, ask the subject; in surprise mode, ask the closest insider; Birt et al. describe "synthesized member checking": return a synthesis to participants to confirm it rings true [53].
5. *Not-a-redemption-arc test:* McAdams' "redemption" is a pattern many people narrate and which correlates with better well-being in his studies; the literature also notes inconsistent effects, and his own framing is a narrative-identity finding, not an editing recipe [7]. The subject's life may not run from suffering to gain, and cultural fit is UNVERIFIED (the book's title, *The Redemptive Self: Stories Americans Live By*, signals scope; I did not retrieve the book).

### Q5. What chapter structures work for whole lives in under 30 minutes? How to handle few childhood photos vs thousands of recent ones?

Structures with real precedent:

| Structure | Precedent | Fit for ~25 minutes |
|---|---|---|
| Subject-titled chapters (2-7) | McAdams LSI: "Picture your life as if it were a book... between two and seven" chapters, title each [5][6] | 5-7 chapters of 3-5 min. Chapter titles come from the subject. Avoids the date-timeline feel if each opens on a texture beat, not a year. |
| Constrained selection | *Desert Island Discs*: eight records, one book, one luxury per castaway (format since 1942; snippet only) [68] | A "eight songs/objects" frame forces prioritisation and carries voice. Running time of the show UNVERIFIED. |
| Short wordless compression | *Up*, about 4 minutes, a marriage from wedding to widowhood [17] | One chapter or the "heart" sequence, not the whole. |
| Audio-first assembly | *Flee*: editing moved to the front; Rasmussen built a "master assembly" from sound and storyboards, like a radio documentary [26] | Best match for a learner whose strongest asset is the voice of the subject and relatives. |
| Two-part ritual structure | StoryCorps' Danny & Annie: Part I (rituals, courtship), Part II (illness and goodbye) [23][20] | A model for the "texture first, hard chapter second" order. |
| Frame plus episodes | *Dick Johnson Is Dead*: staged fictional deaths interleaved with real events (snippet only) [69] | Only if the subject co-designs it. |
| Thematic rather than chronological | Life-story work with older people: the book "does not need to follow a timeline" and can be built around themes (snippet only) [35] | Useful for a "rooms of the house" structure (see Skeleton C). |
| Photo-light tribute | Funeral-industry guidance: about 25-30 photos, under about 10 minutes (vendor, snippet only) [70][71] | A floor for how little is needed; our piece is longer, so quality per image matters more. |

Three candidate skeletons for a ≤30 minute piece (inference; the maker can test them on one chapter):
- **Skeleton A: Chapters in the subject's words.** 5-7 chapters; each begins with a texture beat (a sound or a smell described by the subject) and ends on a turn; dates appear only in the mention layer.
- **Skeleton B: One Tuesday, many years.** The same ordinary morning at 5-6 ages, built by repetition with variation (the Up method); 3-4 dramatised interruptions (the turning points) break the pattern. Not a timeline because the unit is the day, not the year.
- **Skeleton C: Rooms of a house.** Chapters are rooms (kitchen = family; garage = cars and work; garden = rest). Time is a second axis shown by objects ageing. Game-friendly (charter: mechanics must mean something).

Avoiding the timeline feel (inference): dates in the mention layer; chapter lengths follow density of meaning, not years (Up spent 31% of its length before the first blow [17]); let chapters overlap; recur one object, one room and one song; use a present-day frame (the subject now) and let the past arrive as memory (Docter described Married Life as intended to seem like a memory, per commentary reported by Wozny [17]).

**Few childhood photos vs thousands recent** (techniques; (inference) unless sourced):
1. *Let the asymmetry be true.* West's reading of Kodak culture is that snapshots train us to record nostalgic moments and leave out sorrow [58]; the archive is a biased witness. Early years should be carried by *non-photographic* evidence: places (visited, photographed now), objects, maps drawn by the subject or relatives, recipes, handwriting, voices telling it.
2. *Audio over absence.* Relatives' voices over a still room, a doorway, a hand. The Rauch brothers' shorts show the model: visit storytellers, gather family photos, photograph the landmarks, design characters and layouts from that research, "there is some imagining we have to do along the way" [24].
3. *Mark reconstructions.* Flee mixes animation with real archive "reminding people that this is a true story" (via snippet) [26]. Our charter requires reconstructions to be visibly reconstructions.
4. *Sample by theme for recent years.* Strachey's bucket, not the trawl net [48]. Pick 5-8 images per chapter by test (turn, motif, scene), not by volume.
5. *Turn density into meaning.* If the world gets visually richer as the life gets fuller, use image density deliberately: sparse and quiet early, fuller later, with the "thousands" appearing as texture (a camera-roll wall) once, not as a slideshow.
6. *Prefer frame-ready ordinary photos.* Ask relatives for photographs that are *not* celebrations (a car, a kitchen, a corner shop) because that is where texture lives [58].

### Q7. How do practitioners handle hard chapters with dignity in a celebratory piece?

**Inclusion criteria** (assembled; sources noted):
1. *Whose say.* The subject decides what enters; where they cannot (surprise), a trusted proxy decides and the subject gets a veto after the reveal (inference). OHA: interviewers "must respect the rights of interviewees to refuse to discuss certain subjects, to restrict access to the interview" [8].
2. *Load-bearing.* Does the piece become false or flat without it? Pixar restored the miscarriage beat because the film "felt less emotional" without it [14][13]. If it is not load-bearing, hold it.
3. *Drawer test.* Brooker's chest of drawers: top = freely shared (what they did for a living, family members, preferences); middle = shared within a trusting relationship (feelings, significant relationships, more personal memories); bottom = private to the person or a few others, "family secrets, private or traumatic memories", not for public work [34]. Bottom-drawer content stays out unless the subject themselves invites it in.
4. *Third parties.* Other people appear in hard chapters (an ex, an estranged sibling, a person who died). Barnwell's cases (Knausgaard, Kureishi, Sedaris, Law) show relatives can feel exposed and can dispute the account; sharing a draft with family and honouring explicit requests to be left out are the practices she records [55].
5. *"Don't leave them worse off than when you met them"* (Aufderheide, reporting documentary-maker consensus) [54].

**Consent steps** (assembled):
1. Pre-talk: tell the person what you will ask about and that remembering can hurt; let them ask questions first [33] (Ochberg, via Poynter). For trauma-adjacent topics, open with an invitation ("what would you like to tell me") rather than "how did you feel" [32].
2. Give choices: where to sit, which images are used, who is present, breaks, and that they may stop; follow any explicit request to stop recording [32].
3. Offer review before use: Dart: "Offer to let them review the story before publication" and "Don't rush to publish" [32]; OHA: "when possible providing the narrator an opportunity to approve" [8].
4. Re-confirm at each stage: after the interview, at rough cut, at lock. (inference)
5. Do not promise protections you cannot guarantee; promise only what you will do [8][10].

**Techniques that keep dignity in a celebratory piece:**
- *Brevity.* Up gives the loss about 10 seconds, then moves [17]. The eulogy practitioners say: name the hard facts in one or two neutral lines without blame, then spend the time on who the person was (secondary advice from a funeral-planning blog and similar; snippet only) [72].
- *Stillness.* Up's longest beat is a face at a window, no action [17]. ISJL: don't rush silence [36].
- *The subject's own words.* Danny & Annie: Danny and Annie narrate their own illness and funeral plan; Annie reads his letter [20][21].
- *Object instead of event.* The jar on the shelf, the chair [17][19].
- *Style as shield.* Flee used animation to protect Amin's anonymity and to let him tell what he had never told; he said at first "I can't talk about this yet" and opened over many sessions [26].
- *Humour with care.* Kirsten Johnson wanted *Dick Johnson Is Dead* to be funny and kept questioning whether her father, who has dementia, could meaningfully consent as the disease progressed (snippet only) [69].
- *Write a private version* of what cannot be said publicly (a therapist's suggestion reported in the eulogy search results; snippet only) [72].

**Care ethics borrowed from reminiscence and life-story work:** no one should be pushed to tell their life story; life story work can raise sensitive issues; take breaks, move on if upset; the person may share things that must not be recorded; exclude sensitive information from the record but not necessarily from the conversation [34][35]. In a 2015 case study, a nurse listened to private disclosures but decided with supervision not to put them in the written book [34].

### Q8. What recording setup gives usable audio for a non-technical maker (voice will be used in the piece)?

Setup (sources: [10], [11], [37], [38]; my edits are marked):
- **Room.** Small, soft furnishings (rugs, curtains, sofa, pillows); "closets can be excellent recording studios" [37]. Turn off the fridge if you can, stop clocks, close windows; Baylor lists "chiming clocks, humming refrigerators, clattering dishes" as hazards [10]. Avoid big bare rooms [37]. Do not seat anyone with a window behind them if you are also filming [11].
- **Device.** A smartphone is workable; an external clip or lavalier microphone is the biggest single upgrade (several guides; snippet only) [73]. Model recommendations: UNVERIFIED. If you can, record on two devices (a second phone) because repositories and professionals advise redundancy [10][38].
- **Placement.** Phone on a table or stand, not in the hand; keep your hand off the mic; for two speakers place it as close to both as possible; without an external mic, 5-6 inches from one speaker's mouth [37]. For a seated two-person interview at a table, a phone on a folded towel midway, mic end pointed between you, is the practical compromise (inference; Baylor: "Place the microphone... between you and the narrator. Test... play back" [10]).
- **Phone settings.** Do Not Disturb or airplane mode; plugged in or charged; iPhone: Settings > Voice Memos > Audio Quality > Lossless; Android: a bitrate of 128 kbps or higher [37].
- **Format.** Uncompressed PCM WAV (16-bit 44.1 kHz or better) is the oral-history norm [10]; one gigabyte holds about 3 h 13 min of mono 16-bit 44.1 kHz WAV [10]; higher-quality 24-bit 96 kHz is common among archives for oral history with music, with 48 kHz/24-bit cited as suitable for speech-only (university guidelines in search results) [74]. For this project, a lossless phone recording is enough (inference).
- **Slate.** Start every session by saying names, full date, place ("narrator's home" rather than a street address), and subject of the session [8][10].
- **Pacing the recording.** Split into recordings of an hour or less [10]. Interviews average 60-90 minutes [10]; VHP volunteers report 40-80 minutes [11]; for dementia-affected narrators, 30-45 minutes [35].
- **Copies and names.** Copy off the phone as soon as possible; keep at least two copies, ideally in different places; Baylor suggests at least four places [10][38]; use consistent file names, e.g. `2026-11-08_relative-AuntM_session01.wav` (inference). LoC: check readability yearly [38].
- **Log.** Index by time and topic at least every five minutes while it is fresh [10]. Write down correct spellings of names after the interview [10].
- **Transcription.** Whisper (MIT licence): `pip install -U openai-whisper`, then `whisper session.wav --model turbo`; needs ffmpeg; the `turbo` model needs about 6 GB VRAM and is about 8x faster than `large` on an A100 per the README [39]. `whisper.cpp` (MIT) runs CPU-only and on Apple Silicon and takes 16-bit WAV; convert with `ffmpeg -i in.mp3 -ar 16000 -ac 1 -c:a pcm_s16le out.wav` [40]. Running locally keeps private audio off third-party servers (inference). Speed on the maker's machine, and accuracy on elderly voices and accents: UNVERIFIED; test on one recording first.
- **Do not upload to the StoryCorps app/archive** if privacy matters: their Terms (last modified 2023-09-14) grant StoryCorps a perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide licence to hosted user content and say private interviews "may still be retained by StoryCorps and accessed by StoryCorps and its partners", with the Library of Congress named as an example retainer [4].
- **Video and B-roll.** VHP: camera no more than six feet away, face at or slightly above centre, interviewer beside the camera, speak up for hard-of-hearing narrators [11]. If the camera distracts the narrator, move it out of the line of sight or switch to audio (storii) [35]. Record "room tone" (30 seconds of silence) and short clips of the table, hands, door, kitchen for later (inference).

### Q9. What goes wrong most often in family tribute projects, and how do professionals prevent it?

Evidence quality: I found no study or professional survey. The list below is from vendor guides (marked), oral-history manuals, memoir-ethics writing and the This Is Your Life record. The ranking is my judgement.

| # | Failure | Source | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Asking only for photos, not stories | FuneralOne (vendor): "Asking only for photos instead of stories" is a listed mistake [70] | Gather the story first (interviews), then pick images to illustrate it. |
| 2 | Too long; same effect on every photo; long captions | FuneralOne (vendor), Hutchison Funeral Home (snippet only) [70][71] | Hard time budget; vary treatment (Recipe 5: treatment tiers). |
| 3 | Skipping family review, "hard feelings"; uneven coverage of family members | FuneralOne: "Check fairness with family... Skipping family review can cause hard feelings" [70]; Benjamin Law let family read the manuscript [55] | Review round with a small group; keep a "who appears how much" tally. |
| 4 | Repetitive contributions ("Hope you have a great day" x20) | VidDay (vendor, snippet only) [75] | Ask contributors for different things: a scene, a saying, an object. |
| 5 | Disputed or wrong facts; family myth taken as fact | Barnwell: relatives "remember the same events differently" [55]; Baylor: challenge accounts without questioning memory or honesty [10] | Confidence field; two-witness rule; corrections round. |
| 6 | Whose story is it: exposure of others; withdrawn consent later | Barnwell; Brooker's drawers [55][34] | Consent column; named-person check; withdrawal date. |
| 7 | Narrator tired or over-pushed; interview ran long | Baylor (60-90 min average; concentration diminishes) [10]; family-history guides note emotional memories "can be exhausting... You may have to end a session before you're ready" (snippet only) [76] | 45-60 minute sessions; stop signals. |
| 8 | Pre-interview uses up the story before recording | VHP: "be careful that they don't tell you their whole story before you turn the camera on" [11] | Keep the pre-talk to logistics and consent. |
| 9 | Technical: noise, no backup, wrong format | Baylor, UDayton, LoC [10][37][38] | Test, two devices, two copies. |
| 10 | Music licensing | FuneralOne says "confirm you have the right licenses" (vendor) [70]. Content ID/takedown behaviour: UNVERIFIED | Private screening only unless licensed; check before any upload. |
| 11 | Leaks and suspicion in surprise mode | Big Red Book record [28][29][30] | Small circle, neutral project name, scripted answers. |
| 12 | The tribute becomes an obituary or CV | Charter principle 3 and the résumé cut in Deliverable 1 | Texture quota in the inventory. |
| 13 | Practical: gathering too late | A common warning in family-history guides: people assume that once elders are gone there are no options, and miss neighbours, classmates, friends (snippet only) [76] | Interview the oldest relatives first (inference). |

### Exemplar 1: Pixar's *Up*, "Married Life" (2009), beat-level

- **What it is.** The wordless opening marriage montage, wedding to widowhood, scored by Michael Giacchino. About 4:05 by the timecodes in [17] (The Ringer: "about four and a half minutes" [14]).
- **Why it works.** Every shot is a setup for something later (Docter, via The Ringer) [14]; the sequence was meant to feel like a memory [17]; dialogue and sound effects were stripped away because "the less we had the more emotional it felt" [14].
- **How it was made.** Overwritten (30-40 minutes of material); Ronnie del Carmen suggested letting Giacchino's piece carry the montage; early slapstick punching game drew silence from test audiences and was replaced; miscarriage beat cut then restored [14][13]. Giacchino wrote two piano pieces that did not feel right and found the melody on the third attempt; he decided the tone should be quiet, gentle and reverent, imagining what he would say to Carl after Ellie's death [16]. It was his first assignment on the film; Docter asked for something like a grandmother's music box [13].

**Transferable rules (with the evidence for each)**
1. *Earn the first blow.* Wozny: "We only have 1 minute and 17 seconds to care about these characters before the first incident occurs"; the build-up is what makes the later events land [17]. Rule of thumb for a 25-minute piece (inference): do not deliver the first hard beat before about a quarter to a third of the runtime.
2. *Cut to the blow from the highest point of hope, and use the same transition twice.* Bright baby-room painting, pan, grey clinic; ticket purchase, hill, pan, hospital [17]. The viewer learns the pan means loss.
3. *Recovery by action, with the founding object.* Within seconds of blow #1 he brings her childhood adventure book [13][17]. The same book returns when she hands it to him at the end [13].
4. *Repetition with variation.* Kiss three times (mouth; cheek in a mirror; forehead, with the composition flipped to the left third) [17]; the hill twice, with the roles reversed and the light at dusk [17]; the window shot of him looking from dark into her light [17]; house work marks each story change ("Every time they make a change to their house, it indicates a change in the story") [17].
5. *Recurring object that changes meaning.* The jar goes from dream to emergency fund to forgotten; the balloon from childhood to work to hospital to church; the book from her to him [17][13].
6. *Compress years by repeating one action while the actors age.* The tie sequence "gradually changing each time showing passage of time" [17].
7. *The withheld beat.* The death is not shown; the film cuts from the hospital to the church steps [17]. The miscarriage is implied by the clinic room and her crying [13][17]. The viewer knows he bought the tickets, which she never learns (inference).
8. *Silence as the peak.* The longest beat is a face at a window and no dialogue or sound effects [17][14].
9. *One theme, many tempos.* "Dropping in tempo when upsetting or unexpected incidents occur" [17]; "the less we had the more emotional it felt" [14].
10. *Colour is a person.* Per the director's commentary reported by Wozny, Ellie is magenta; the funeral is full of it, and it drains from the frame after [17]. Chairs: two different shapes for two personalities (design commentary, snippet only) [19]. The same lateral camera moves are reused later in the film to link back to the marriage (cinematographer Lin, in The Ringer) [14]. A search snippet also paraphrased Lin as saying the camera "doesn't really start moving until after they get married"; I did not see that sentence in the text I opened, so treat it as UNVERIFIED.
11. *Test and cut.* Silence from an audience told Pixar a beat did not work [13]. Docter says it was hard to judge edits: "Some days it would be super emotional, and other days we wouldn't feel anything at all" [14]. Plan fresh-eyed viewing sessions.

**What we can reuse.** Plan one object, one place, one gesture and one song as recurring motifs for [NAME]; give each three appearances (young, middle, now) that change a little each time; decide which beat is withheld; keep a stopwatch beat sheet. Link: [14] The Ringer; [17] Wozny.

### Exemplar 4: *Flee* (Jonas Poher Rasmussen, 2021): hard chapter, audio-first, style as protection

### Exemplar 6: Texture methods with literary pedigree

### Recipe 1 (Deliverable 1): Question banks by life stage, two versions

**How to use**
- Choose 5-10 questions for a 40-45 minute session, drawn from 2-3 stages; the rest are reserve [2]. Start with a warm-up; end with a reflective question [3][5].
- `[S]` = ask the subject directly (collaborative mode). `[R]` = ask a relative about the subject (works in both modes; in surprise mode the relative is briefed about the secret first).
- In `[R]`, "you" is the relative and "they" is [NAME]. The relatives are narrators of their own lives too (they were adults then), so let them answer about their own day, prices, jobs and fears.
- Codes at the end of each line are the texture categories the inventory uses: R routine/ritual · S sense (smell, sound, touch, taste, light) · O object · P people · W words/sayings/jokes · L place · M money/work · B body/health · X scene (a one-time event).
- Tone rules for 50+ subjects and 70-80s relatives (sources: [10][11][32][35][36]; some inference): one question at a time; short sentences; anchor to a concrete image ("the first home you remember") rather than abstract ("what was your childhood like"); wait; do not correct; avoid asking "why" (it can read as blame; inference); no more than 45-60 minutes, with a break; follow tears per Deliverable 3.
- Do not rush: answer by the narrator's habit ("we always...") counts as texture; follow with "tell me about one time it wasn't like that".
- Gate: Stage 8 is opt-in and run only after the boundaries conversation in Stage 0 (or the proxy equivalent in surprise mode).

**Prompt kits (bring these; they work across stages)**
- *Tuesday walkthrough* (best for stages 5-7): "It's a Tuesday in [year]. Start from the alarm, or whatever woke you. What does your foot touch first? What do you hear? What's in your hand? What do you eat? What do you wear? How do you get out of the door, and what's the first thing you notice outside? Walk me through to lights-out." Follow-ups: "What was different on a Friday?" "What always went wrong?" For relatives: "Walk me through a normal Sunday visit: parking, door, who answers, where you sit, what's cooked, how it ends."
- *Memory map:* paper and pen; the interviewer draws their own childhood street first, then asks the narrator to draw theirs and label "where something memorable happened" [65]; photograph the drawing with the audio running.
- *Photo set (6, not 60):* "What's outside the frame? Who took it? What happened just before and after? What did that room smell like? Who's missing?" Include 2 non-celebration photos [58].
- *Song set:* 8-10 clips (20-30 seconds) from ages 12-30 plus 2-3 from the last decade [46]; ask "where were you?" and "who was there?"
- *Object:* ask each relative to bring one object and tell where it lived and who touched it.
- *"I remember" list:* ten fragments in five minutes, no explanation [52].
- *Smell (optional):* ask "what did it smell like?" first; real smell items only if the narrator is game [41].
- *House inventory:* the kitchen table (who sat where, what lived on it), the fridge door, the hall table, the glovebox, the junk drawer, the shed.
- *Era hooks (adapt to the subject's country and decade):* the family car, the phone (where it hung, how long the cord), the TV (who held the dial), the radio station, the first music they owned and how they played it, brands on the shelves, what a school lunch cost.

### Recipe 2 (Deliverable 2): Milestone-plus-texture inventory template

**Purpose.** One table with two row types: `milestone` (a one-off event) and `texture` (a repeated pattern: routine, saying, smell, car, pet, the kitchen table). The template forces texture by (a) a mandatory `sensory_detail`, (b) a `recurrence` field, and (c) a quota (below). Columns required by the brief are first-class: date, place, people, objects, sensory detail, source, confidence, consent status.

**CSV (copy into any spreadsheet app; one header row)**

```csv
id,type,date,date_precision,age,place,people,what_happened_or_repeats,objects,sensory_detail,recurrence,source,confidence,consent_status,drawer,treatment,motif_tags,who_can_verify,notes
M-001,milestone,1978-06,month,~8,[town],"[NAME];[parent]",moved house across town,"blue suitcase; cardboard boxes","smell of fresh paint in the new hallway",once,"R: [relative] session 2 @00:14:30; photo IMG_0231 (date on back)",B,asked-ok,top,dramatise,"home;leaving",[sibling],"ILLUSTRATIVE ROW - not real data"
T-001,texture,1983-1985,range,~13-15,[kitchen],"[NAME];[parent]","Sunday breakfast: eggs and the radio sports results","yellow radio; chipped enamel mug","radio static between stations; burnt toast",weekly,"S: [NAME] session 1 @00:21:10",C,asked-ok,top,glimpse,"kitchen;radio",[sibling],"ILLUSTRATIVE ROW - sibling remembers a different radio"
T-002,texture,1994-1999,range,~28-33,[car],"[NAME]","the drive to work with the same cassette on repeat","green hatchback; glovebox of tapes","gear-stick rattle at 40 mph",daily,"R: [PARTNER] session 4 @00:08:05",C,not-asked (surprise hold),top,mention,"cars;music",[PARTNER],"ILLUSTRATIVE ROW"
M-014,milestone,2003,year,~38,[hospital],"[NAME];[PARTNER]",[withheld],,,once,"R: not solicited",D,bottom-drawer (do not use),bottom,hold,,,"ILLUSTRATIVE ROW - shows how a held row is logged without detail"
```

**Column rules**
- `type`: `milestone` or `texture`.
- `date` + `date_precision`: `exact | month | year | range | decade | relative` (relative = "the summer after the move"). Prefer ranges and anchors (age, house, landmark public event) over guessed exact dates; a life-history calendar (years by domains: where lived, work, relationships, children) cross-references and helps dates [67].
- `people`: use names or roles; one cell per name separated by `;`.
- `sensory_detail`: exactly one concrete, specific detail (a smell, sound, texture, taste, light). Mandatory. If none is known the row cannot be marked `dramatise`.
- `recurrence` (texture): `daily | weekly | monthly | seasonal | annual | once`.
- `source`: who/what and where: `S` subject, `R` relative, `D` document, `O` object, `P` photo, `V` existing audio/video; add session id and timestamp (`@hh:mm:ss`) or file name. No source, no row.
- `confidence` (informed by genealogical evidence analysis: original vs derivative, primary vs secondary information [79]; and oral-history practice that "errors" are data [64]):
  - **A**: a dated original record (certificate, dated letter, dated photo) and at least one person agrees.
  - **B**: two or more independent people agree, no record.
  - **C**: one first-hand memory.
  - **D**: second-hand, family legend, or conflicting accounts. Keep conflicting versions as separate rows linked in `notes`; do not average them.
- `consent_status`: `asked-ok | asked-ok-with-limits | not-asked (surprise hold) | declined | third-party-needs-check | bottom-drawer (do not use)`.
- `drawer`: `top | middle | bottom` [34].
- `treatment`: `dramatise | glimpse | mention | hold`.
- `motif_tags`: free tags that build the through-line (objects, places, phrases).
- `who_can_verify`: person, not a source.

**Texture quota (inference)**: for each life stage, at least 6 texture rows from at least 4 categories (morning routine; meals; transport; sound and music; home layout; sayings and jokes; money; pets; clothes; the workplace; the weekly rhythm; the annual ritual), plus at least one texture row per decade for each of: a place, an object, a saying, a smell or sound. Count milestone and texture rows separately; target more texture rows than milestone rows.

**AI handling rules for the inventory (inference, from charter principle 4):** an AI may propose a row only from a transcript line or a document, and must put the quote and timestamp in `source`; it must not invent a sensory detail or a date; unknown cells stay empty; any conflicts become two rows; the human confirms `confidence` and `consent_status`.

### Recipe 3 (Deliverable 3): One-page interview protocol (v0.1)

**BEFORE (a week out)**
1. Pick one stage (Deliverable 1) and 5-10 questions; choose 1-3 prompts (object, photo, song, map) [2]. Reserve the rest.
2. Pre-talk (not recorded; do not let them tell the whole story yet) [11]: why you are doing it; who will hear it; that AI tools will be used to transcribe and tidy audio and may be used to restore or animate real photos but will not invent events or voices (charter); they can skip any question, stop, restrict a passage, review it before use, and withdraw it until a lock date [8]. Promise only what you will do [10]. Hearing and comfort: ask where they want to sit; speak up for hard-of-hearing narrators [11].
3. Consent: spoken consent on the recording plus a short written release, signed at the end if possible (many oral historians prefer signing after the interview so they can address anything sensitive) [8].
4. Length: 45-60 minutes with a break (Baylor average 60-90; frail or memory-affected narrators 30-45) [10][35]; book a two-hour slot with time for tea [11].

**SET-UP (10 minutes)**
5. Quiet, soft room; fridge and clocks off; phone on Do Not Disturb; lossless setting; plugged in [10][37]. Phone on a stand or folded cloth, between you, 6-12 inches from the nearest mouth, 5-6 inches if one speaker, hands off the mic [37]. Second device if possible.
6. Test: speak, play back, adjust [10].
7. Slate on tape: names, full date, place ("narrator's home"), session number, topic [8][10].

**DURING**
8. Open with an easy present-tense or object question. Chronological order at the start, then wander [11].
9. One question at a time. Then wait. Count to ten in silence [10]. Nod; no "uh-huh" [11][12].
10. Follow up: "Tell me more", "And then what happened?", "What did it look/smell/sound like?", repeat their last words [3][36][60].
11. Ask for the scene, not the summary: "Tell me about one time."
12. Do not correct. Note contradictions to check later [10].
13. Jot unusual words and names to spell afterwards [10]. Watch for fatigue: fidgeting, repeating yourself, flat answers; wrap up [35].

**IF SOMEONE CRIES OR GOES QUIET** (assembled from [32][36][11][35]; ordering is my edit)
14. Stay. Say nothing for a few seconds. Offer a tissue.
15. Say something plain ("That's a lot."), not "How do you feel?" [32].
16. Offer three doors: stay with it, take a break, or move on [32]. Do not change the subject for them [36]; follow their lead if they ask to move on [11].
17. Keep recording unless they ask you to stop [32]; mark the time. Afterwards ask whether that passage should be kept, restricted or removed [8].
18. If they seem to be far away or overwhelmed, stop [32]. Finish with an easy present-tense question (what's for dinner?) (inference).

**CLOSE**
19. Ask a reflective question ("What was it like to talk about this today?") [5]; thank them.
20. Check the recorder is still running until they have left the room; say goodbye off-tape (inference).

**AFTER (within 24 hours)**
21. Copy to two places; name files `YYYY-MM-DD_person_session##.wav`; never edit the original [10][38].
22. Make a time-coded index (every 5 minutes or less) and a list of spellings [10]. Run Whisper locally for a transcript [39][40].
23. Send a thank-you; give a date for when they will hear or read it [10][32].
24. Populate inventory rows with source and timestamps. Put anything hard in `drawer` and `consent_status`.
25. Review: Did they get tired? What did we miss? Next session's stage and prompts.

### Recipe 4: Surprise-mode runbook

| Gate | Actions |
|---|---|
| 0 Suitability | Write a one-page "points for and against" (the "feeza") [28]. Ask the closest insider: would they love it or hate it? Have they ever said they hate surprises, speeches, being photographed? Record yes/no/unsure. If "hate", stop or switch to collaborative [30]. |
| 1 Circle | Keep the circle as small as the work allows. Neutral project name; one shared folder; no group chats; no notes with the project name on paper the subject might find [28][29]. Brief everyone, including the spouse, with one scripted line for if they are asked. Brief young children only if essential. |
| 2 Sources | Relative interviews (R banks); documents; objects; existing audio and video of the subject; photos. Track who knows. |
| 3 Hard material | Bottom-drawer material is not solicited, not used [34]. Named third parties are checked with the right person. Any relative with a difficult history with the subject: ask an insider before approaching (cf. the reported Mullard case) [30]. |
| 4 Facts | Inventory with confidence. Triangulate. Prepare a list of ten items only the subject can confirm. |
| 5 Reveal | Show it first privately to the closest insider. Reveal to the subject in a setting where they can say "stop". Give a veto window: nothing is shared beyond the room until they say so; they can ask for cuts [8][32] (inference). |
| 6 Contingency | If it leaks, tell the subject and convert to collaborative. If they refuse, stop [30]. |

Script for relatives (honest, no false pretext): "I'm making a piece about [NAME]'s life as a gift. It is a secret from [NAME] until [date]. I'd love 45 minutes of your memories; you can skip anything, and nothing you say will be used without your OK. Please don't mention it to [NAME] or anyone who might." Avoid inventing a cover story that requires the subject to do something on camera: the Blanchflower approach used a false pretext and it failed live [30].

### Recipe 5: Treatment tiers and beat-sheet worksheet

**Scoring (per milestone)**: Turn / Motif / Scene / Voice / Dignity / Variety (Q4). Mark `dramatise`, `glimpse`, `mention`, `hold`.

**Starting time budget for a ~25 minute piece (our heuristic, to be tested on the vertical slice; not from a source):**
- Cold open or present-day frame: 60 s
- 5-6 chapters averaging ~3 min: each one dramatised scene (60-120 s) + 2-3 glimpses (5-10 s each) + one texture sequence (30-60 s) + a chapter card (5-8 s)
- 3 "Tuesday" interludes of ~40 s (repetition with variation)
- First hard beat not before ~25-30% of the runtime (see Up) [17]
- Present-day close: 90 s
- Thanks: 60 s

**Beat sheet** (adapted from Wozny's Appendix 1 [17]): columns `time | description | emotion | recurring image | what is withheld`. Fill one for the final cut and one for each reference piece (Up, Danny & Annie) while you watch with a stopwatch, then draw the mood graph (x = time, y = mood) [17]. A good rhythm check: does the piece contain one long quiet beat, three or more repeats of a motif, and one withheld beat?

### Recipe 6: Through-line workshop (three sessions, ~1 hour each; AI can assemble the packets)
1. *Harvest*: from transcripts, list verbs and objects that recur; list the subject's own chapter titles and key scenes [5][6]; list traits named by two or more relatives.
2. *Three candidates*: write three through-lines, each in one sentence, each with three timestamped quotes and one counter-example.
3. *Test*: apply the five checks in Q4; pick one; name what it excludes.
4. *Check with a person who loves them* (the subject if collaborative; the closest insider if not): "Does this sound like them? What's missing?" [53].

## Techniques

Named techniques, with where to find the how-to in this file.

1. **Silent question** (Baylor): ask, then wait; "Keep quiet and wait" [10]. Recipe 3 step 9.
2. **Definition ask** (Baylor): "I know what ___ is, but future generations may not. Please tell me what a ___ was and how it was used." [10]. Good for objects, brands, routines.
3. **Echo / mirror last words** (ISJL): repeat the narrator's last statement; do not change the subject after an emotional statement [36].
4. **"And what happened then?"** (Terkel, reported): the simplest forward prompt (snippet only) [60].
5. **Specific over general** (ISJL): "What do you remember most about the customers?" beats "Who shopped?" [36].
6. **Sensory ask** (StoryCorps tip 8; Werner-Thomas sensory roadmaps): "What did your kitchen smell like?" [3][66].
7. **Habitual then episodic** (inference): "What was a normal X like?" then "Tell me about one time it wasn't."
8. **Tuesday walkthrough** (Mass-Observation day diary method adapted) [50]. Kit in Recipe 1.
9. **Memory map** (sketch-map elicitation; interviewer draws first) [65]. Kit in Recipe 1.
10. **Photo elicitation** with six photos, "outside the frame" questions [45][58].
11. **Song set from the reminiscence bump** (ages ~12-30) [46].
12. **Go-along / house tour** (Kusenbach) [44], adapted to a kitchen-table tour for frail narrators (inference).
13. **Eyes-closed present-tense recall** (Rasmussen via NPR snippet) [27]. Use only if the subject chooses.
14. **"I remember" list** (Brainard, Perec) [52].
15. **Life-history calendar** for date anchoring (years by domains) [67].
16. **Chest of drawers** (Brooker, in McKeown et al.): sort material into top, middle, bottom drawers [34].

## Pitfalls and ethics

1. **Treating the LSI as a script.** It is a research instrument; the "redemption sequence" is how many people narrate, with an inconsistent link to well-being in the literature, not a template [7]. Let the subject's chapters stand even if they do not resolve.
2. **Therapy framing vs entertainment craft.** Reminiscence and life-review work has care ethics: do not push on painful memory; life story work "should not be assumed" wanted and can be emotional (focus groups, snippet only) [35][34]; the Cochrane review for dementia reports small, inconsistent effects (snippet only) [43]. Borrow prompts, respect limits. We are making a gift, not providing therapy; if distress is serious, stop and get appropriate help (inference).
3. **Consent.** Informed consent before recording, narrator right to refuse and to restrict, narrator approval before release where possible, and honesty about what you cannot guarantee [8]. Some oral historians sign legal releases after the interview to handle sensitive material [8]. The OHA also asks users of interviews to avoid "manipulations of the narrator's words" and to retain "the integrity of the narrator's perspective" [8]. In this project: the narrator's words are quoted exactly; no AI-edited speech.
4. **AI use on real memories.** The charter forbids invented events, fake people, or words in anyone's mouth. Add to consent: tell each narrator that AI will transcribe and may help tidy and restyle real photos; no voice cloning or synthetic voice unless that person explicitly agrees (inference, aligned to charter principle 4). Keep full recordings and transcripts local where possible; if a cloud AI is used for transcripts, narrators should know (inference).
5. **Third parties.** Family memoir cases show people can feel exposed and dispute accounts; practices: share drafts with family, honour explicit requests to be left out, keep the account as a single point of view [55]. Named third parties in hard chapters need a check.
6. **Bottom-drawer material.** Do not solicit it; if volunteered, log and do not use [34]. McKeown's case study: the nurse listened but did not put it in the book [34].
7. **Hard chapters as exploitation.** Do not treat tears or rage as material: The Dart tip sheet's section on handling distress (my paraphrase) says to expect anger, rage or tears and not to treat them as material to exploit, and notes that filming vulnerable moments can make the memory harder to process [32]. Ask permission before using any emotional moment.
8. **Surprise ethics.** The subject does not choose to be the object. Mitigate with an insider check, a veto window, and the right to stop [30]. Cover stories can fail publicly [30]. Contacting a relative without checking first can expose conflict (the reported Mullard case) [30].
9. **Leaks.** Documented and predictable: children, notes, shared devices [28]. Expect them; plan the response.
10. **Memory is not a record.** Different relatives remember the same event differently [55]; narrators leave things out [11]; Portelli: errors are meaningful [64]. Do not fact-check in the interview in a way that questions the narrator's honesty [10]; check afterwards.
11. **Bias in the photo archive.** Celebration over routine and sorrow [58].
12. **Over-long, over-tired sessions** [10][35][76].
13. **Platform terms.** StoryCorps' app/archive terms give StoryCorps a perpetual licence and retention rights even for "private" interviews (terms last modified 2023-09-14) [4]. Do not upload private family interviews there.
14. **Music rights.** Unresolved: private viewing vs any public posting; confirm licences (vendor advice) [70]; platform takedown behaviour UNVERIFIED.
15. **Do not impersonate or fabricate** records or reviews (artifact policy) and keep personal material private until the maker says otherwise (charter).
16. **Over-fitting to Pixar.** *Up* is fiction built by a large team over years with test screenings [14]; use its rules, not its scale.

### Q1. Windows hardware: which Whisper, and how long for 10 minutes

**What is known (sources).**
- OpenAI Whisper models: `turbo` is 809M parameters, about 6 GB VRAM, about 8x the speed of `large` (measured on an A100 with English speech; "real-world speed may vary with language, speaking speed, and hardware"). `small` is 244M, about 2 GB VRAM; `medium` 769M, about 5 GB; `large` 1550M, about 10 GB. `turbo` "is not trained for translation tasks"; use `medium` or `large` to translate [39]. Install is Python plus PyTorch plus ffmpeg; on Windows the README gives `choco install ffmpeg` or `scoop install ffmpeg` [39].
- whisper.cpp memory (README table): tiny ~273 MB, base ~388 MB, small ~852 MB, medium ~2.1 GB, large ~3.9 GB. Windows is supported with MSVC and MinGW. Backends: NVIDIA CUDA, Vulkan (cross-vendor), OpenVINO (Intel), AMD ROCm/HIP, and an AMD Ryzen AI NPU path on Windows 11. It lists `large-v3-turbo` as a downloadable model but gives no memory figure for it [40]. RAM is therefore not the constraint on an 8 GB machine; speed is.
- Whether prebuilt Windows binaries exist is UNVERIFIED. The README fetch did not mention any, and the releases page showed v1.9.5 as "Latest" (06 Oct, year not shown) with "Assets 2" but did not list file names [83]. The maker should open that release page and look; otherwise the route is a cmake build, which is the hardest step for a learner.
- faster-whisper (a third option, CTranslate2-based): on a GPU it needs cuBLAS for CUDA 12 and cuDNN 9, and the pip route for those libraries is Linux-only; on Windows the README points to a separate archive of NVIDIA libraries that must be extracted and added to `PATH` [80]. That makes it the fiddliest Windows install of the three (inference from [80]).

### Q2. The family's language(s)

**Evidence on how much language matters.**
- The Whisper README says "performance varies widely depending on the language" and points to per-language error charts for `large-v3` and `large-v2` on Common Voice 15 and FLEURS [39].
- Table 13 of the Whisper paper gives `large-v2` word error rates on FLEURS (read speech) [82]. I read the table from the PDF text; column order was recovered by me, so spot-check any number you rely on. Percent WER, lower is better:

| Group | Languages (WER %) |
|---|---|
| Under 6 | Spanish 3.0, Italian 4.0, English 4.2, Portuguese 4.3, German 4.5, Polish 5.4, Russian 5.6, Japanese 5.3 (character-level, see note) |
| 6 to 15 | Dutch 6.7, Catalan 7.3, French 8.3, Turkish 8.4, Swedish 8.5, Ukrainian 8.6, Finnish 9.7, Vietnamese 10.3, Thai 11.5 (character-level), Greek 12.5, Czech 13.3, Croatian 13.4, Tagalog 13.8, Korean 14.3, Romanian 14.4, Chinese 14.7 (character-level) |
| 15 to 40 | Arabic 16.0, Tamil 17.5, Hindi 21.5, Urdu 22.6, Hebrew 27.1, Persian 32.9, Afrikaans 36.7, Swahili 39.3 |
| About 90 or worse (unusable) | Bengali 104.1, Punjabi 102.4, Gujarati 102.7, Telugu 99.0, Yoruba 94.8, Hausa 88.9 |

  The paper spaces every character for Chinese, Japanese, Thai, Lao and Burmese, "effectively measuring the character error rate instead", so those numbers are not comparable with the rest [82].
- Why: the paper reports a squared correlation of 0.83 between log WER and log training data per language, with WER roughly halving for every 16x more training data [82]. A language with little training data is a risk even when the model "supports" it.
- What this does not cover: FLEURS is read speech. The paper's tables say nothing about older voices, conversational family talk, regional dialects, or mixing two languages in one sentence. I found no study on any of these this round: UNVERIFIED. (From my own prior knowledge, not checked this round: Whisper decides the language once per 30-second window, so mid-sentence switching may be handled badly. UNVERIFIED.)

**Rule for Exercise 3.** Run it on a real clip, in the language the oldest relatives actually speak at home, with the speaker who is hardest to hear. Set the language explicitly rather than trusting auto-detect (inference; check `whisper --help`). Check ten random lines against the audio and count wrong words. If the language is in the "15 to 40" band or worse, or the family code-switches, budget a human correction pass for every transcript and do not let an agent quote from an unchecked transcript (consistent with the inventory rule "no source, no row"). If the maker wants English subtitles from non-English speech, do not use `turbo`; transcribe first, then translate the corrected text and have a family member check it [39].

### Q7. Music licensing: private screening versus public

Belongs to the proposed `sound-and-voice` track. **Proposed owner: the maker decides, that track's researcher answers, and the answer is due by Saturday 2026-11-14 (end of week 4).** Jurisdiction is not stated in the charter; the law text below is US. Anywhere else: UNVERIFIED.

**What the US statute says (primary text):**
- "Publicly" means (1) at a place open to the public, or at any place where "a substantial number of persons outside of a normal circle of a family and its social acquaintances is gathered", or (2) transmitting or communicating a performance "to the public, by means of any device or process", whether received in the same place or separate places, at the same time or different times [95]. My reading, not legal advice: a showing in a home for family and friends is probably not public; a hired hall or restaurant room with guests beyond that circle, a stream or link that reaches people outside it, or a posting online probably is.
- Section 106 gives the owner separate exclusive rights to reproduce, make derivative works, distribute, and perform or display publicly [96]. Audience size does not remove the reproduction right. In practice a song under a video needs permission for the composition (a synchronization licence) and for the recording (a master use licence); performing-rights organisations (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC) license public performance, and a licence from one does not cover another's catalogue [98]. [98] is Wikipedia (tertiary; it cites a 2016 Congressional Research Service report I did not open).
- Section 110(4) exempts some non-commercial performances of a "nondramatic literary or musical work" with no admission charge and no payment to performers or organisers [97]. Whether playing back a video with a recorded soundtrack falls inside it: UNVERIFIED. Do not rely on it.
- Practical enforcement risk of a family-only file that is never uploaded is probably low, but I found no source that says so: UNVERIFIED.

**Platform rule found:** YouTube's Audio Library page says only music and sound effects from the Audio Library are known to YouTube to be copyright-safe; tracks from it "won't be claimed by a rights holder"; Creative Commons tracks in it must be credited in the video description; and YouTube "is not responsible for issues that arise from" "royalty-free" music from other sources, advising a lawyer for questions [99]. Whether a private or unlisted upload is still scanned: UNVERIFIED.

**Options that keep a family piece clean (my synthesis; confirm the terms of anything you pick):**
1. Original music made for the piece by the maker, a relative or a commissioned musician, with a short written agreement saying where it may be played. *Danny & Annie* credits original music as its own role [92].
2. A licensed library with the licence read and saved per track (the YouTube Audio Library is one, with its stated terms [99]).
3. Her songs where they belong: in the room, not in the file. The story question set already collects her songs as memory cues [46]; collecting them as story is separate from putting the recordings in the film. How a venue's own music licence applies to a video played there: UNVERIFIED; ask the venue.
4. Keep a cue sheet from day one so the answer for any screening is one filter: `cue_id | where in piece | title | artist | composer | source/licence | licence text saved (y/n) | allowed: home / hall / online | date checked`.

**Decisions the maker supplies by 2026-11-14:** country; where it will be shown (home, rented room, streamed to relatives abroad, posted); whether copies will be given to family; whether her favourite songs are wanted in the file or only in the room.
