# Surprise Ops: Running a Surprise Tribute for Your Mother

*Draft v1, 2026-10-18. Scribe draft from `research/surprise-ops.md` (researcher: Sonnet, 2026-10-18) and `CHARTER.md`. Not legal advice. Citation numbers match the research notes. Anything marked TODO(research) is still open.*

## Why this matters for our life journey

Most of the material for the film is not in her house. It is with the people who shared her life: her parents, her brothers and sisters, old friends, a former colleague, a neighbour. It is also in albums, letters, slides and tapes that other people keep. To collect it, we have to ask those people. Asking people carries a risk that she hears about it.

So the secrecy is part of the craft, and the charter sets its limits. Principle 4 says real memories stay real: nothing is invented, and no words go into anyone's mouth. Principle 5 says dignity comes first, even in a surprise. She cannot approve the film in advance, so anything she might not want shown stays out of the shared version until she has had her say.

The deadline sets the dates. The reveal is Saturday 27 February 2027. Week 1 started on Sunday 18 October 2026. Week 15 starts on Sunday 24 January 2027, and by then all material should be back in hand. That leaves the last weeks for editing and rehearsal.

This playbook is a learning path. Do the six exercises before you ask a real relative for anything. Then follow the steps in order. The ArtCraft apps are not covered in these notes; the craft-visual and craft-motion tracks cover them. TODO(research): decide whether any ArtCraft app has a place in covert scanning or the reveal.

## Core principles

1. **Omit, but never lie.** You may leave things out. You may not say anything untrue, and you may not record her in secret. A research-ethics rule on deception draws the same line: withholding details is different from misleading people [13]. This is our reading of that rule, not a legal test.
2. **Make every pretext real.** When you ask someone for help with a cover task, you actually do it, and she gets the result. You label the photos and give her a copy. You write the family page. A pretext that is not real is a lie with a delay (our inference).
3. **Apply the no-lie floor.** If she sincerely asks whether something is being made about her, you do not deny it. You can say yes and keep the details until the date. The "If she asks" lines under the truthful pretext cards show the words.
4. **Ask her directly only in truthful windows.** There are three. You can label and date old photos with her. You can hold a real family-history conversation that also covers her parents and grandparents. You can hold an announced family-stories recording at a gathering, where everyone, her included, can see the recorder [9][10][11]. Everything else comes from other people and from objects.
5. **Get a spoken yes, at the time.** Read the consent card aloud at the start of every recording, and get a clear yes on the file. The research notes state the rule as "never any recording of her". Two planned moments do record her with her knowledge: a family-stories evening, and filming the room at the reveal. Settle the exact rule before the first interview (Decision 10).
6. **The reveal is her first consent point, and she can change anything after.** Give her the review options: delete, restrict, redact, add clarifications, correct mistakes, or keep material closed until a date she picks [8]. Her decisions override ours.
7. **Default to HOLD.** If we are unsure about a hard chapter, it stays out for now. You can add something later. You cannot un-show it (our inference).
8. **Honest and warm, not raw.** Describe a hard person by trait, not by one incident. Follow each honest sentence with something kind [56]. Hint rather than detail. Do not invent a sunny ending. Use no synthetic voices [63]. Show no animated faces of the dead unless the closest family says yes explicitly [64].

**Philosophy note.** Philosophers disagree on whether a lie to hide a surprise is acceptable. A search summary of Derek Parfit's 2002 Tanner lecture reports that he treats such a lie as innocent when the person would consent if asked [76]. We could not open the lecture (404), so we do not rely on it. A Leiden doctoral thesis separates surprise, as a reaction to the unexpected, from whether it later turns out good or bad [75]. The practical test is plain: would she consent? Only an insider can answer that (Step 1).

## How to do it, step by step

Work in order. Each step ends with a gate. Do not start a step until the gate before it is passed.

### Step 1. Establish the facts that decide everything (week 1)

1. Ask one trusted insider, not her, whether she likes surprises, speeches, or being filmed. Reports of people who dislike surprises are opinion and anecdote, not research [81].
2. Write down your country and region, and those of her and each key contributor. Recording law, holidays, labs and phone services depend on them. UK, EU and cross-border recording law is not checked (TODO(research)).
3. Decide whether 27 February 2027 is her birthday, a named milestone, or a date chosen for another reason. The date also clashes with calendars. One projection puts Ramadan starting about 8 February 2027 [72], and Lunar New Year falls on Saturday 6 February [73]. Ask the proxy and an elder about any clash.

Gate: a one-page note of what she would and would not want, with the insider named as the source for each line.

### Step 2. Set the rings and the two keys (week 1)

1. Ring 0 is you. Ring 1 is one co-keeper, who helps by looking (not by asking her) at what is on the shelves, plus two proxies for hard chapters. Ring 2 is the contributors, each told only their part. Ring 3 is the people in the room, told two to three days before.
2. Score proxy candidates 0 to 2 on: knows her well, discreet, no stake in the chapters concerned, will say no to you, trusted by her, and available in weeks 12 to 18. Pick the top two. A partner may be one key, but not the only one. In one review of 16 studies, surrogates matched patients' treatment preferences about 68% of the time [60]. Those were medical hypotheticals, so one proxy is not an oracle.
3. Decide the partner's role: co-keeper, proxy, both, or neither. The notes call a partner the strongest leak path and the likeliest proxy, drawing on story.md Q3. Decide this first.

Gate: names written down, and the partner's role decided.

### Step 3. Set up a neutral name and a project email (week 1)

1. Pick a neutral code name. The notes use "Lantern" as an example. Use the code name in file names, subject lines and contacts. Do not use her name on paper, or in a file name that might sync to a shared device.
2. Create a project email that only you read. Every lab, shop, upload link and notification goes there. Dropbox file requests email the requester every time someone uploads [34].

Gate: nothing visible on a family device pairs her name with the project.

### Step 4. Make devices and accounts safe (week 1, then monthly)

Run the Mum-test: open each shared device and account the way she would, and look for project words. The checklist is in Templates. The main fixes:

1. **Scanning phone.** Turn off the shared photo library, and set the camera to save to personal [18]. Turn off partner sharing [19]. Check the scanner app's backup settings before the first scan [21][22].
2. **Notifications.** Turn off message previews on shared screens. iOS previews can be set per app to "When Unlocked" or "Never" [33 S] (TODO(research): check on your own phone). Signal has name-only and no-content notification options, but its page was not opened (TODO(research)) [80 S].
3. **Family calendars.** Joining an Apple family or setting up a Google family creates a shared Family calendar automatically [31][32]. Keep project dates on paper, or in your project account only.
4. **Smart speakers.** When a child asked for recent orders, an Alexa+ device read a parent's recent Amazon orders aloud, including birthday gifts [29]. Turn off personal results on shared devices [30]. Turn off item titles in shopping notifications, and disable voice purchasing [29].
5. **Your Windows PC.** Use a separate Windows account for the project. Keep project files on an external drive, in a folder that OneDrive does not back up. Check that Manage backup is off for Desktop, Documents and Pictures [25]. Set "Hide content when notifications are on lock screen" per app, and use Do Not Disturb [24]. Windows Recall exists only on Copilot+ PCs and is opt-in per user [23].
6. **Encryption.** BitLocker is for Pro, Enterprise and Education editions, not Home [26]. Free encryption for Windows Home is TODO(research).

Gate: the Mum-test finds no project word on any shared screen, and each fix is written down.

### Step 5. Choose one truthful side-project and agree one line (weeks 1 to 2)

1. Choose one cover task you will really finish. The options are Cards A to E in Templates. The notes list photo labelling first.
2. Agree one line with your co-keeper. Everyone in Ring 1 says the same truthful thing if asked.
3. Write the no-lie floor at the top of your card: you may leave things out; you may not lie; you may not record her; you stop when she says stop.

Gate: the side-project is on your paper plan, with a date you will keep.

### Step 6. Collect stories from people (weeks 2 to 12)

1. Make sure the relative wants to be recorded before you start. A reluctant relative derails the project [12].
2. Send the contributor brief personally, never in a group chat she is in. Use Version A or B from Templates. It says what you want, by when, the secret rules, and what happens to their words. Do not ask older relatives to use a website they do not already use [37].
3. Interview one person at a time, in a quiet place. Start with the oldest relatives and the co-keeper. Interview siblings separately before any group evening, because their versions differ [9]. If her parents are alive, interview each one separately, at their table, with props. Frailty and hearing matter [40][66].
4. Ask for scenes, not summaries. Broad prompts like "tell me about your childhood" get names and dates. Props such as albums, letters and photos draw out scenes, and so do open follow-ups [9]. Send three prompts in advance, not all of them [11]. StoryCorps groups its question lists by relationship, and says to use the ones you like [5].
5. Keep it to 20 to 30 minutes. The notes cite StoryCorps for six to eight questions in about 40 minutes [1]. The 20 to 30 minute shape is our inference for a favour.
6. Let the person choose how to record. A voice memo on their own phone is the default. The Oral History Association says audio-only is often the least stressful option, and calls a phone-based recording "likely the most secure remote option" [6]. Video calls are a last resort [7]. On an iPhone, Voice Memos can share a recording as one .m4a file [17].
7. Ask the "only you" question once, with an exit (Templates).
8. Log each person's consent, the drawer each piece came from (top, middle or bottom, after the notes' use of Brooker's drawers), and their withdrawal date.

Gate: each contributor has a clear yes, knows the secret rules, and knows how to withdraw.

### Step 7. Borrow and digitise without tipping her off (weeks 2 to 13)

1. Choose items she will not miss. Ask the co-keeper to look, not ask her. Items in weekly use are photographed in place or left alone.
2. Pick a window when she is reliably out, with at least twice as long as you need. Tell the co-keeper where you are.
3. Photograph as found: the shelf or drawer, each album's cover and spine, and every spread before any page moves. This set is your order record and your first scan.
4. Do not mark originals. Keep loose items in a labelled envelope outside the album, and keep a paper list.
5. Log each item: time out, where it went, time back, and who handled it.
6. Return the items in reverse order, and compare them with the as-found photographs. Return them the same day by default.
7. Magnetic album pages: the adhesive can weld to prints and tear them on removal [55]. The notes assume the same for sticky albums, with no source (TODO(research)). So photograph each spread in place first, and remove only prints that lift easily.
8. Originals of legal papers, passports and certificates never leave the house. Photograph them flat, in window light, with no flash.
9. Time: according to one vendor, a phone takes about 15 seconds per print once you are in rhythm, and a flatbed takes 30 to 60 seconds plus handling [52]. A sheet-fed scanner is another option: one Epson model is rated at about one photo per second at 300 dpi, takes loose prints only, and publishes no 600 dpi speed [54]. Our estimate for six albums of 20 spreads with four prints each is 3 to 5 hours of phone work (inference). Time your own first album (Exercise 2). Scan settings and file naming are in `playbooks/ai-imagery.md` and are not restated here.
10. A library may offer self-service digitising. One example takes VHS, MiniDV, 8 mm film, photos and slides in three-hour blocks, but Video8, Hi8 and Digital8 were unavailable [49]. Your location is unknown, so treat it only as an example.

Gate: every item has a log line, and everything is back or logged as out.

### Step 8. Send tapes and film to labs, with dates in writing (order by week 2)

1. Send one pilot tape by Friday 30 October 2026, to a lab quoting about two weeks or less. The pilot tests the whole loop before any batch goes out (research notes, Table C).
2. Use the receive-by table in Templates. The notes say the Friday is the date the lab must have the item. One table heading says "send-by", and a column shows items leaving her house about two days earlier. Confirm the meaning with each lab (TODO(research)).
3. Ask every lab in writing for: turnaround in weeks, holiday closures from November to January, handling of mould and sticky shed, return shipping, and what happens to film or tapes after completion. Pro8mm holds film for 30 days after completion, then recycles it unless storage is bought [46].
4. Compare quotes. Most price pages are competitor-authored or undated, so treat them as rough. Examples: Legacy Digital at $19.95 on special or $28.50 regular per two-hour tape [44]; Perfect Image Camera at $28.99 for VHS and $22.99 for MiniDV [45]; Capture at $19.99, $29.99 and $39.99 for SD, FHD and 4K [43]. Capture ranks itself first, so weigh its ranking with care [43].
5. Do not mail anything she would miss. A mail-in lab may keep an item away from her home for 4 to 10 weeks.
6. No lab in the notes published a 2026 holiday cut-off. TODO(research): ask each lab in writing.

Gate: each lab's written dates and terms are in the project email, and the receive-by date is checked against them.

### Step 9. Decide the hard chapters with two keys (weeks 12 to 17)

1. Fill one decision sheet for each candidate chapter (Templates). Keep the facts, the sources, and how sure you are of each.
2. Brief each key on paper. Cover the purpose, confidentiality, the standard, the asymmetry ("we can add later, we cannot un-show"), what they will see (drafts of hard chapters only), what they decide, a time limit of about 45 minutes, and that she decides at the reveal and after.
3. Ask the standard question: "What would she choose, and what have you seen or heard her say or do that tells you?" This is substituted judgment, which is the medical-ethics standard for someone who cannot decide [61]. Ask this instead of "what would be best for her".
4. Each key decides OUT, PRIVATE, SHARED-LIGHT or HOLD, and gives a reason.
5. Apply the rules. If either key says she would not want it in the shared cut, it stays out. If the keys disagree on PRIVATE, it is HOLD. A key who is part of the story steps back on that item. You may not overrule a key towards inclusion.
6. Check that nobody is left worse off than when you met them. This principle comes from a search summary of a study of 45 documentary filmmakers [62].
7. Keep private showings private. In a 2023 Hong Kong documentary dispute, a subject said she never agreed to public release [82]. A private showing does not stand in for consent to a wider release.

Gate: each candidate chapter has a decision, a reason and a date.

### Step 10. Build the reveal (weeks 16 to 19)

1. Make two cuts. The shared cut runs about 6 to 10 minutes, with a hard cap of 12. The private cut runs 20 to 30 minutes. Tribute videos in practitioner guidance cluster at 3 to 8 minutes [67][68]. Those are funeral-industry sources, and the notes found no study of reveal length. Our figures are inference.
2. Burn the captions in. Mix speech forward, with no loud music sting. Test with an older relative in the room, because about one in three US adults aged 65 to 74 has hearing loss [66].
3. Choose the room: a living room for four to eight people, a family dinner for 10 to 20, or a party for 30 or more. Each has trade-offs (Decision 12).
4. Plan the quiet word, 10 to 20 minutes before the film, in a separate room (script in Templates). Offer her the choices: where to sit, who sits beside her, whether to watch with the room or alone first, a stop signal, and whether the room may be filmed, with no close-ups unless she says yes.
5. Film the room and the screen, not her. Give her the footage, and let her delete any of it [42].
6. Rehearse on the real playback chain on Monday 22 February. Hold the room brief on Wednesday 24 February. Do a cold start from a powered-off laptop on Thursday 25 February, and again on Friday 26 February.
7. Prepare the fallback ladder: the laptop; a second laptop with the same file; a USB stick straight to the TV; a phone by cable or cast; a three-minute audio or still version; and, last, a postponed private evening, said plainly. Keep two USB sticks in different pockets.

Gate: a rehearsal with one trusted person who is not a proxy, and two cold starts passed.

### Step 11. The reveal (Saturday 27 February 2027)

1. Four hours before the start, set up: test the sound, turn captions on, turn on Do Not Disturb, hide notifications, and close mail and chat [24].
2. Guests arrive by a route she will not see. Phones are silent. Older relatives sit near the speakers with a clear sight line.
3. A distractor brings her by a plan that is true. If she asks who will be there, do not lie.
4. The quiet word happens 20 minutes before the film. Then she chooses her seat and her companion.
5. Open with a welcome of 30 to 60 seconds: "This is a gift from all of us. There is nothing to say and nothing to do." Then play the shared cut.
6. At the end, hold silence for 20 seconds. Bring the lights up slowly. Do not cue applause. Say: "No speech. There's food."
7. Give a pause of 10 to 15 minutes, with food, soft music and her companion beside her. No toasts, no questions about the film, and she may step out.
8. Toasts only if she signals she is ready. Contributors speak only if they agreed in advance.
9. Later, in a quiet corner, hand her the private cut and the invitation card: "This is yours. No rush."
10. The next day, send a short text. Ask nothing about the film.

Gate: she is not asked to speak, perform gratitude, or say what she thought.

### Step 12. After the reveal (from 28 February 2027)

1. Days 0 to 1: send a short thank-you. Ask nothing about the film.
2. Days 2 to 5: send a card, the keepsake and the invitation: "This is version 1. It is yours to correct and to add to."
3. About day 7 (Saturday 6 March 2027): hold a recorded "her turn" conversation of about 45 minutes, in a place and at a time she chooses. Record only with her yes. Use five to eight prompts from Templates.
4. Keep a corrections ledger: what the film says, what she says, the action, who did it, and the status.
5. Day 30 (Monday 29 March 2027): close the consent ledger. Every row is kept, changed, removed, or closed until a date, in her words.
6. About eight weeks after the reveal (Saturday 24 April 2027): deliver "her version" as version 2, if she wants one. Keep the family version unchanged as a record.
7. Make two copies on different media, and refresh them at least every five years [70]. Write "next copies due 2032-02" on each drive label.
8. Use static QR codes, not subscription ones [71]. Treat a private web link as a convenience copy, not the archive. Of the pages that existed in 2013, 38% were gone by October 2023 [69].
9. Contributors receive the shared cut only after she agrees, with a thank-you note.

### If you slip (any time)

1. Stop. Do not cover it with a lie.
2. Within 10 minutes, decide what she saw or heard, and whether it points at the project or only at "something".
3. Tell your co-keeper within an hour.
4. Choose one path: carry on, if the truthful line is enough; a partial reveal ("There's a gift in progress; here's what you can know"); convert to collaborative, where she picks the chapters she sees; or, if she objects, stop, ask the proxy, and later ask her. This is the notes' slip protocol (T8), which draws on story.md Recipe 4.
5. Fix the cause: the setting, the device, or the person. Log what happened and what was fixed.
6. If a contributor slipped, thank them without blame, and ask exactly what they said. If a child blurted, stay calm and use the partial-reveal lines.

## Learn-by-doing exercises

Do these in order, before any real relative is asked for anything. Times are our estimates, not sourced figures.

### Exercise 1. Mum-test one device of your own (45 to 60 minutes)

- **Goal:** find every place a project word could show up on a shared screen, before there is anything to hide.
- **Steps:** (1) Pick a device that others share with you, such as your own tablet, a speaker you own, or a shared TV. (2) Use a dummy code name, such as "Lantern test". (3) Search for it on the device and in its apps. (4) Ask any speaker "what's on my calendar" and "show recent orders". (5) Write each finding and its fix on one sheet. (6) Apply one fix, then test again.
- **Time estimate:** 45 to 60 minutes.
- **What good looks like:** a sheet with a finding and a fix for each device, and a second test that finds nothing. Use your own or a practice device, not hers. The real Mum-test on her home devices happens in Step 4, with the co-keeper's help.

### Exercise 2. Time one album spread in place (1 to 2 hours)

- **Goal:** measure your own rate. No source in the notes gives a rate for photographing album pages in place, so the estimate is inference.
- **Steps:** (1) Use an album you own. (2) Set the phone on a stand near a window, no flash, at the highest quality setting. (3) Time the set-up, the cover and spine, then each spread. (4) Note what slows you: glare, curled pages, sticky corners. (5) Multiply to estimate 20 spreads. (6) Repeat with a second album and compare.
- **Time estimate:** 1 to 2 hours.
- **What good looks like:** a table of times with an estimate for a whole album, labelled as your own measurement. No figure copied from a vendor page.

### Exercise 3. Run a practice "only you" interview (about 1 hour with review)

- **Goal:** practise the card, the set-up, the spoken consent, and the "only you" question.
- **Steps:** (1) Ask a practice partner who knows it is a practice, and who agrees to be recorded. (2) Send a three-prompt card a day before. (3) Set up: Do Not Disturb on, phone on a folded towel, 6 to 12 inches from the mouth. Say a test line and play it back. (4) Read the consent card aloud on the file and get a spoken yes. (5) Run the 20 to 30 minute shape: slate, warm-up, two to four prompts, the "only you" question, and the closer. (6) Within 24 hours, copy the file to two places, rename it `YYYY-MM-DD_[relation]-[initials]_kit01`, and write a time-coded index.
- **Time estimate:** about 1 hour, including review.
- **What good looks like:** a clean file with a spoken yes at the start; at least one answer with a place, a sequence and a sensory detail; no steering; and an exit offered in the closer.

### Exercise 4. Get lab dates in writing (1 hour of work, plus waiting)

- **Goal:** learn to get the dates, terms and costs in writing, and check our receive-by table against them.
- **Steps:** (1) From the project email, write to two labs, one fast and one slow. (2) Ask for turnaround in weeks, holiday closures (November to January), mould and sticky-shed policy, return shipping, what happens after completion, and the total price with shipping and tax. (3) Save each reply in the project email. (4) Recompute the receive-by date for each lab from the formula in Templates.
- **Time estimate:** 1 hour of work, plus waiting for replies.
- **What good looks like:** two written replies with dates, costs and terms, and a receive-by date that matches the table or differs for a stated reason.

### Exercise 5. Cold-start a reveal test (about 1 hour)

- **Goal:** prove the playback chain works from a powered-off laptop, with captions, sound and notifications set.
- **Steps:** (1) Use a three-minute test file, not her film. (2) Power the laptop off. (3) Connect the screen cable, turn on captions, turn on Do Not Disturb, and hide notifications on the lock screen [24]. (4) Start the file from cold, and time the first frame. (5) Check the sound at room level with a listener who is hard of hearing, if possible [66]. (6) Walk the fallback ladder: second laptop, USB stick, audio-only version.
- **Time estimate:** about 1 hour.
- **What good looks like:** the first frame appears without fiddling, captions are readable, the sound is clear to the listener, and the fallback order is written on a card.

### Exercise 6. Write a decision sheet for a made-up chapter (45 minutes)

- **Goal:** practise the two-key rules on an invented case, never on her life.
- **Steps:** (1) Invent a chapter about a fictional family, and label it FICTIONAL. (2) Fill every field of the decision sheet in Templates, including the four questions. (3) Write Key 1 and Key 2 decisions separately, as two different invented people might. (4) Let the outcome follow the rules: a "no" on the shared cut means OUT, and a disagreement on PRIVATE means HOLD. (5) Write the treatment as a trait, not an incident.
- **Time estimate:** 45 minutes.
- **What good looks like:** no blank field, a disagreement produces HOLD, and the treatment line is honest and warm.

### Consent card (read aloud on the recording)

> "[MAKER] is making a private film and keepsake about [NAME]'s life as a surprise gift, for [date]. May I use what you say, and your recorded voice, in two places: a film that family will watch together, and a longer private version given to [NAME]? You can skip any question, ask me to stop, and ask me afterwards to cut anything. I will keep the recording private, on storage I control, and will not share it more widely without asking you again. I may use software, including AI tools [that run on my own computer / in a private online workspace: edit to match what really happens], to type it up and tidy the sound, but I will not change what you said or make a synthetic copy of your voice. Is that all right with you?"

Do not read this card until the bracketed line is true. Decision 9 is about exactly this. The card follows the Oral History Association's four points: consent before recording, goals and risks, how the material may be used, and a chance to review [8].

One-line version for a text or voice message: "May I use your recorded voice in a private film and keepsake for [NAME], shown to family and given to her, with you able to skip questions, stop, or have anything taken out afterwards?"

### Interview kit shell (per relationship)

- **Before (24 to 48 hours):** ask personally, and confirm they want to take part [12]. Send a card with three prompts only. Say how long: "about 20 to 30 minutes; we can stop whenever you like".
- **Set-up:** quiet, soft room; fridge and clock off; Do Not Disturb on; phone charged; phone on a folded towel, 6 to 12 inches from the nearest mouth; test line played back.
- **Shape:** 0:00 to 0:03 slate and consent; 0:03 to 0:08 warm-up ("Where are you sitting?" or "How did you hear I was doing this?"); 0:08 to 0:22 two to four prompts, with follow-ups such as "Tell me more", "What did it smell like?", "Then what?", and silence; 0:22 to 0:27 the "only you" question; 0:27 to 0:30 the closer.
- **Closer:** "Is there anything I should have asked?" "Is there anything you have said that you would rather I left out?" "Would [NAME] be happy for the whole family to hear any of it?" Keep recording until they leave the room, then say goodbye off tape [notes T1.0].
- **Do not ask:** "What do you love most about her?", "Was she a good mother?", "Why did she...?", or anything about money, health, romance, conflict or faith unless they bring it up. If they do, log it as `drawer: middle/bottom` and stop.
- **Children:** under 14, a guardian present, one question, a drawing is fine. From 14 to 17, guardian consent and a written release, with the same card in plain words.

### Sample prompts by relationship

The full sets are in the notes (T1). This wording is the researcher's, not a published script, so test it.

- **Her parents:** "Take me into the first home you brought [NAME] to. Start at the front door: what do you see, smell, hear?" "What was she always doing with her hands at six or seven?"
- **Siblings:** "Describe the room you shared, or the wall between you: what could you hear through it?" "What were the rules of your games, and how did she bend them?"
- **Partner:** "Walk me through an ordinary weekday morning: who is up first, what is the first thing she touches, what does she say?" "What does she hum, sing or whistle?"
- **Oldest friends:** "Where were you the first time you met? What was she wearing, what were you both doing?" "Which song, show or phrase from those years would send you both into laughter?"

### The "only you" line

- Ask once, plainly: "You were the only person in the room for at least one story about [NAME]. Which one is yours?"
- If they cannot think of one, ladder, do not repeat: "When were you alone with her?" Then "Where?" Then "What happened next?"
- Sort it: "Would she tell this one herself, to a room?" "Is there a version you would be happy for the whole family to hear?"
- Always give an exit: "If this one is better kept between us, say so, and I will keep it out."

### Truthful pretext cards

Rule: you may omit. You may not state anything untrue. You may not record her. You stop if she says stop.

- **Card A, photo labelling.** Say: "The old photos are fading and nobody has written on them. Will you help me put names and years on them? I'll keep the labels and give you a copy." Do: actually label the photos, keep them, give her a copy, and write your own notes afterwards. Disclose at the reveal that the labels fed the film.
- **Card B, "tell me about Grandma".** Say: "I'd like to write a page about Grandma and Grandpa before we lose the details. What do you remember about them?" Do: write the page, give her a copy, and ask about the other ancestors too.
- **Card C, recipes or a playlist.** Say: "I'd like to write down how you make [dish]." Or: "I'm making a playlist for the [holiday dinner]. What was on in the kitchen when you were 20?" Do: make the recipe card or the playlist, and share it.
- **Card D, my own memory project.** Say: "I'm trying to write down my own early memories. Do you remember what the street looked like?" Do: write your own memories down too.
- **Card E, the family-stories evening.** Say, at the table: "Can I record a few family stories tonight? Everyone just tells their favourite memory of [Grandpa / this house]." Do: put the recorder where everyone can see it, ask each person's yes, and say it is for the family's stories and a family project. Do not say "only an archive" if it will feed the film.

Test for any card, from the notes (inference), all six must hold: real (you do the thing, and she gets the result); accurate as far as it goes; stoppable; not used for heavy material without the proxy and her later OK; disclosed afterwards; and no recording of her without her knowledge.

**If she asks "are you making something about me?"** Use these, in order: (1) "Yes, something for you, and I'd love to keep the details until [date]. If you'd rather know more, tell me, and I'll tell you." (2) "I'm working on a family project, and I've promised people to keep it quiet until it's done." Never say "no", and never invent a different project. If she objects, stop and take it to the proxy.

**If a contributor is asked:** "[MAKER] asked me for a few memories for a family project, and I promised to keep it between us until it is finished."

### Holiday rules (weeks 10 and 11, and Thanksgiving week if it applies)

1. The room is not a studio. Do not extract, interview or scan in the middle of a meal. Listen, notice who is in the room, and note who might tell stories later.
2. Quiet one-to-ones with the oldest relatives are the exception: a walk or a cup of tea, ten minutes, only if natural, and only with the consent card.
3. If you want a group-stories moment, announce it (Card E). The recorder is visible, the invitation is clear, and she takes part knowing it is on [9].
4. Mute project people on any device the family shares. No project talk in a chat she is in.
5. No new smart speaker, tablet or shared account as a gift. Each one is a leak path.
6. Shared albums are fine. Shared libraries are not [18][19].
7. If she asks what you are up to, use Card E's line or the no-lie floor.
8. Do not take anything out of her house in these two weeks, unless it is a short, planned, logged borrow.

### Gate for a former partner

Default: do not approach. Ask the proxy whether she is on good terms with the ex, whether she would welcome their contribution, and whether a child in common should decide. If any answer is no or unsure, stop. If all are yes, use three or four neutral prompts, never about the end of the relationship, and include the material only in the private cut unless the proxy and her own history say otherwise [78].

### Estranged relatives

Ask the proxy first. Never contact a person cold. Choose one of four options: (A) a neutral past image, only if she would tell that story herself; (B) exclude; (C) a private contribution for her private cut only, with the proxy as the go-between; (D) in the room, only if she has said she would welcome it. Default to B or C. If an estranged person contacts you, say thank you, promise nothing, and take it to the proxy. The guest list is the honoree's or host's call; an etiquette column judged an unauthorised invitation to be a breach [74].

### Cultural and religious questions (questions, not rules)

1. Is 27 February her birthday, or a milestone with its own name or rites in her tradition? Does her community celebrate birthdays at all?
2. Does the date clash with a fast, a mourning period, a festival or an observance (Ramadan, Lent, a death anniversary)? Would the meal, timing or alcohol need to change?
3. Who is the head of the family, and should that person hear first, as a courtesy?
4. Who speaks first, sits first or is served first? Is it right for a child (the maker) to host, or to present to elders?
5. Are there norms about being photographed or filmed, about who may see her image, or about dress on screen?
6. May images, voices or names of the dead be shown? Are there rituals or warnings expected first? Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people may be distressed by images, voices and names of the dead, which can conflict with cultural prohibitions [65].
7. Which language or languages will the room speak? Do you need subtitles in more than one?
8. Which music is acceptable in her tradition, and are there times when music is not played?
9. Are divorce, remarriage, illness, miscarriage or debt taboo, shameful, or a source of pride when told well?
10. What is the right way to thank, and is a public speech expected or unwelcome?

None of these is a sourced rule for any culture. Do not apply any of them as an assumption. Ask the proxy and an elder.

## Decisions you'll need to make

Each decision lists the options, then our current lean. A lean is the scribe's reading of the notes, or our inference where marked. The maker decides.

1. **Country and region** (you, her, each key contributor). Options: none needed yet. Lean: find out in week 1. Recording law, holidays, labs and services depend on it. UK, EU and cross-border law is unverified (TODO(research)). Recording law outside the US is not checked here.
2. **Does she live with you?** Options: yes, or no. Lean: unknown. If yes, scan away from home (a friend's, your workplace, a library), or while she is out on a fixed routine. Scan on a phone with sharing off, never on a shared PC unless it has its own account. One commenter scanned their mother's photos at dawn while she was at her class, then made a book [41, snippet only].
3. **Who organises the family accounts** (Apple Family, Google Family, Amazon, streaming, phone). Options: you, her, or another family member. Lean: find out in week 1. The organiser may see some purchases and bills [83 S][84 S]. Ship to a work address, a friend, or a locker.
4. **Is Feb 27 her birthday?** Options: a birthday; a named milestone; a date chosen for other reasons. Lean: unknown. Use "Happy [birthday]" in the run sheet only once it is confirmed.
5. **Does she love or hate surprises?** Options: proceed as planned; a two-step reveal (private first, then the room); a private gift only. Lean: ask the insider first (Step 1). If she dislikes surprises, use the two-step reveal or the private gift. Dislike of surprises is anecdote [81].
6. **The rings: co-keeper and two proxy keys.** Options: one co-keeper; the partner as co-keeper, proxy, both or neither. Lean (scribe's reading): one co-keeper and two proxies. Keep the co-keeper and proxy roles separate if the relationship is fragile [notes, Pitfalls].
7. **Which truthful side-project?** Options: Card A (photo labelling), B (family page), C (recipes or playlist), D (your own memory project), E (announced family-stories evening). Lean (scribe's reading): Card A first, because it is real, bounded and gives you a reason to handle the photos. Card E is the only one where she is recorded knowingly, so keep it for a gathering where you can announce the recorder.
8. **Shared cut and private cut length.** Options: anything from 3 to 12 minutes for the shared cut. Lean (inference): shared 6 to 10 minutes with a hard cap of 12; private 20 to 30 minutes. Test it on a trusted person first (Exercise 5 and Step 10).
9. **Where contributor audio lives, and which services touch it.** Options: local storage only, with transcription on your own machine; a private cloud workspace; any third-party service. Lean (inference from the notes): keep raw audio on storage you control, transcribe locally, and edit the consent card to match what really happens. The notes name story.md Q8 for the local transcription set-up; the tool is not named here (TODO(research)). Settle this before the first interview. The charter says nothing personal goes to a third-party service without your explicit say.
10. **The recording rule.** Options: (a) the strict rule, "never any recording of her", which would drop the family-stories evening and filming the room at the reveal; (b) "record her only with her knowledge, a spoken yes on the spot, and an announced recorder", which keeps both planned moments. Lean (inference): (b), with the reveal room filmed only if she says yes in the quiet word, wide shot only, and footage handed to her to delete. The notes' summary and their own plan conflict here. You must pick one and write it in the consent card.
11. **Default for hard chapters.** Options: HOLD (out until she decides after the reveal); SHARED-LIGHT by default. Lean: HOLD. Holding costs contributors their moment, and that cost is real. Choosing HOLD is the cheaper error, since you can add later (the notes' reasoning, inference). The notes raise this as an open question for the advisor.
12. **Reveal format and room.** Options: a living room for four to eight; a family dinner for 10 to 20; a party for 30 or more; a two-step reveal; a private gift. Lean: the notes give no lean. The run sheet assumes a living room or a family dinner (inference). A party has the highest social load and is hardest to protect the hard chapters in.
13. **Filming the reveal.** Options: no filming; a wide shot of the room only; close-ups with her yes. Lean: wide shot only, and close-ups only with her yes. Give her the footage and let her delete any of it [42].
14. **The archive.** Options: family drives only; a copy placed in a local library or historical society, her call [85]; a StoryCorps-style recording, her choice only. Lean: avoid StoryCorps Connect by default. Its archive can be published, a Library of Congress copy persists whatever the privacy setting, and the session cannot be paused [1][2][3][4].

16. **Culture and faith checks.** Options: ask the proxy and an elder; a calendar check; a move of the viewing date. Lean: ask the proxy and an elder before week 16. Move the viewing if she needs it moved. The build deadline can stay.
17. **Who may speak first, host or present.** Options: none set; the eldest; the maker; a relative. Lean: none set. Ask the cultural questions above before deciding.

## Pitfalls

- **Smart speakers read orders aloud.** A child asked, and a recent order was read out [29]. Fix: turn off personal results and item titles on shared devices, and disable voice purchasing.
- **Family calendars appear by themselves.** Joining a family plan creates a shared calendar that every member can see [31][32]. Fix: keep project dates off it.
- **Shared libraries share everything.** Every participant can add, edit and delete; contributed items move out of your library; the camera can share automatically [18]. Fix: keep the scanning phone out of any shared library.
- **Partner sharing shares photos as they are backed up.** The partner can save them to their own account, and they can appear in Memories and search [19]. A report says the "other apps" option has been off by default since November 2024 [20 S]. Fix: check the setting, or use a project-only account.
- **Scanner apps back up without asking.** PhotoScan uploads depend on backup settings [21]. Photomyne's membership backs up everything to its cloud, and shares albums of more than five photos as a link [22] (vendor's claims). Fix: check backup settings before the first scan.
- **OneDrive backs up Desktop, Documents and Pictures** once switched on [25]. Fix: keep project files on an external drive, outside those folders.
- **Dropbox emails the requester on every upload** [34]. Fix: use the project email as the only address.
- **Notification previews show names and text** on shared screens [33 S]. Fix: set previews off for any shared device.
- **Mail previews.** The USPS Informed Delivery service shows letter images and package status to the account holder [77 S]. Fix: check who holds the account, and send project mail to an address she does not see.
- **Mail-in absences get noticed.** A mail-in lab may keep an item away for 4 to 10 weeks (the notes' estimate from lab turnarounds). Fix: send only what she will not miss. Film may be recycled after 30 days unless storage is bought [46].
- **Group chats leak.** A message can land in the wrong thread, and previews show on shared screens [35][36]. Fix: no project talk in any chat she is in.
- **Too many helpers.** One successful surprise video needed at least ten helpers [38]. Fix: tell each person only their part, and keep a small inner ring.
- **Hidden objects get found.** A ring hidden in the oven was found while cleaning [36]. Fix: choose hiding places by asking who cleans or looks there.
- **Proxies are wrong sometimes.** In one review, surrogates matched preferences about 68% of the time [60]. Fix: two keys, default HOLD, and her veto after the reveal.
- **Synthetic voices and animated faces of the dead.** A 2021 documentary used an AI voice of a dead person without clear consent, and the director disclosed it only later [63]. Fix: real audio, objects and places. No synthetic voices, and no animated faces of the dead without the closest family's yes [64].
- **Anecdote used as best practice.** The first-hand material is blogs, columns and a Substack. Reddit was not reviewed (TODO(research)). Fix: label every claim as anecdote or practice, and test the method on one trusted person first.
- **Long or crowded reveals.** Hearing loss is common among older viewers [66]. Fix: keep the shared cut under 12 minutes, burn in captions, and seat older guests near the speakers.
- **Technical failure on the day.** Fix: two cold starts, two USB sticks, and the fallback ladder.
- **Vendor bias.** Capture ranks its own service first [43]. Fix: get quotes from several labs, in writing.
- **Holiday overload.** Fix: listen at holidays, and extract nothing mid-meal (holiday rules).
- **Staging a reconciliation.** Fix: never. The proxy decides on any estranged person's place.
- **Unverified law.** The US reporters' guide is a baseline, and it says it is not legal advice [14]. Apple's call recording is consent-by-design, with a repeating notice, and is available only in some regions [15]. Third-party call recorders may record silently [15]; this project does not use them. Fix: ask a lawyer where it matters.
- **Remote recording tools.** Many video platforms save only MP3, and each re-save of a compressed file adds loss [6][7]. Audio is about 1 GB per hour, against 10 to 30 GB for video [7]. Fix: have the contributor record audio on their own phone, and keep the original file.
- **Automatic calendar events.** In a 2012 forum thread, a Siri-created calendar event for a surprise party was emailed to the mother, who was not on the invitee list (snippet only) [79]. Fix: check what any assistant creates, and who it emails.
- **Naming a death.** Funeral and suicide-loss guidance says the family decides how a death is described, and warns against an open platform where anyone may speak [57][91] (snippets only). Fix: never name a cause of death without the closest family's say.
- **Web links rot.** 38% of pages from 2013 were gone by 2023 [69]. Fix: the link is a convenience copy only.
- **Private memories can surface.** In dementia-care research, people sometimes recalled private memories they did not mean for any written product [59]. Fix: ask before anything goes into the film, and let people stop.
