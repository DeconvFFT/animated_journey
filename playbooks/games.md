# Games that tell life stories: mechanics that carry meaning

Track: games · Draft v0.2 · 2026-10-18 · For the maker (a motivated learner)
Built from research/games.md and CHARTER.md. Numbers in brackets, like [1], point to the Sources list at the end. Anything marked TODO(research) is still open.

## Why this matters for our life journey

Charter Principle 2 says every interactive or animated device has to say something true about the life. Games have spent years finding small actions that do this. In *Unpacking*, a player places one life's belongings into successive homes. In *Florence*, a conversation is a puzzle that gets easier as two people relax. In *A Memoir Blue*, the trophies are moved aside to reveal photographs. Each one uses a single small verb to say something large [1][3][5][30].

Most game mechanics are built for quick hands, points and fail states. Our viewers are of mixed ages, and the subject is over 50 and may not play games at all. So we borrow the ideas that carry meaning and leave the gamer parts behind [39][41].

Minecraft sits in the middle. Some people will recognise it, and real memorials have been built in it. But its rules limit public showing and selling, and its default look reads as generic. That is why our default is original voxel assets, with Minecraft as a reference [42][49].

The biggest risk is decoration: a points bar, or a copy of a famous game's look with none of her facts behind it. *Unpacking*'s designer Wren Brier says a clone leaves "this sort of hollow experience" [3].

## Core principles (8)

1. **Swap the subject.** Change the person's facts to another family's. If the device still works unchanged, it is decoration and fails Principle 2. A device that passes could only be this person's: a real object, a real room, a real move. A 10-year-old's zoo for his late father has an octopus aquarium because his father fished. Nobody else's zoo would look like that [1][3][59].
2. **One verb, one meaning.** Pick one small action (place, pack, lift aside, wait for a chime) and tie it to a real event. If you cannot finish this sentence, cut the device: "In this chapter the viewer ___, which means ___ about her life" [1][5].
3. **No points, no fail, no timer.** Milestones are not achievements. Remove scores, badges, progress percentages, game over and timed input. If a pop-up could read "Achievement unlocked: Married," delete it [1][37][38].
4. **Four input rules.** Every interaction must (a) work with one click, (b) show the next step, (c) work if nobody touches it, and (d) let the host pause. The pause chime from *Before Your Eyes* is the model for (b) to (d) [14][39].
5. **Friction tracks the relationship.** Make a moment harder when the relationship is tense and easier when it is settled. This is *Florence*'s core trick [5][6].
6. **Real things, few of them.** One object that survives every move is worth more than fifty props. Photograph the real thing, keep the set small, and log every object. In *Edith Finch*, one bedroom's decor was reportedly revised late, once the team saw that the room told its owner's story without narration. TODO(research): verify this detail against developer interviews [12][13].
7. **Reconstructions look like reconstructions.** Rebuilt scenes use a visibly different medium from documents. Hard chapters follow the family's say (Principles 4 and 5). *Edith Finch* leaves the player unsure how accurate each account is, and we must make our reconstructions honest in the same way [11][13].
8. **Design for a room.** Assume a host, a shared screen and mixed ages. Pause-and-talk beats solo skill. Viewers of *Unpacking* on Twitch narrate their own lives as they watch [3][61]. Surprise mode: collect contributions only from the 'in on it' list kept by `surprise-ops`, one person at a time, never via a group chat; the wall can keep growing after the reveal.

## How to do it, step by step

We are in the LEARN phase, so every step is done on paper, in a document or in a free desktop tool. No code yet.

1. Read CHARTER.md, then research/games.md. The notes' summary (item 15) gives a starting menu of seven devices: a trophies-aside reveal, unpacking across three homes, a map that fills in [52], a first-meeting conversation piece [5][6], one real room as a shrine [12], a pause chime [14], and one warm voxel diorama [64][65].
2. Get the story facts for one chapter from the story track: objects, rooms, places, sayings and turning points. Mark which ones the family has confirmed. Do not design a device around a fact nobody has confirmed. Real locations work best when the story needs them. *Dordogne*'s art director picked places this way [32].
3. Apply subtractive design. For each chapter, list what it does not need, and cut anything that does not serve the core idea [1].
4. Choose one verb per chapter and write the Principle 2 sentence.
5. Run the swap test on every device. Rewrite any device that works unchanged for any family.
6. Build the object ledger in a spreadsheet. Columns: item, source photo, room or chapter, first and last appearance, recurring (yes or no), story, who confirmed it, OK to show. *Unpacking*'s team tracked items this way [3].
7. Score each device for access (1 to 5), gimmick risk (low, medium or high) and build effort (S, M or L). These scales are our own judgment, not research findings. Anything in the slice needs an access score of 4 or 5.
8. Choose a vertical slice: one chapter and no more than three devices. The charter asks for one great chapter first (Principle 6).
9. Paper-prototype each device with index cards or a storyboard. Test with people the maker has agreed to involve. Ask what they felt, not whether they understood. The notes call this "playtest for emotion" [11].
10. Set the visual rules: a palette for each era drawn from real photo stock, a small voxel palette, and one warm key light with a softer cool fill [25][64][65][66].
11. Before any asset leaves the desk, run the terms check (Exercise 7) and settle the visual source (Decision 1).
12. Hand the approved device list to the format track (how it is delivered) and the toolchain track (which tools).

## Learn-by-doing exercises

Times are our own estimates.

### Exercise 1: Swap-the-subject audit (about 1.5 hours)

**Goal:** tell a meaningful device from decoration.

**Steps:**
1. Pick five patterns from the notes' pattern library, for example unpacking into homes, the trophies-aside reveal, a map that fills in, the friction budget, and the pause chime.
2. For each one, write a single sentence: "This device says ___."
3. In your head, swap in a different family's facts. Mark each device pass or fail.
4. Rewrite each failure so the sentence names one specific object, room or event.

**Good looks like:** every sentence names something another family would not have. If all five pass with no edits, look harder.

### Exercise 2: Object ledger on your own home (about 2 hours)

**Goal:** learn the ledger on material that is yours, before touching hers.

**Steps:**
1. Photograph 20 objects in one room of your own home.
2. Fill in every ledger column for each object.
3. Choose one object as "the survivor," the thing you would carry through every move. Write one sentence on why.

**Good looks like:** every row has a story sentence and a "who confirmed" entry. No invented stories. The survivor has a clear reason.

### Exercise 3: Friction storyboard on paper (about 1 hour)

**Goal:** feel how difficulty can carry emotion, as *Florence* does [5].

**Steps:**
1. Draw six frames of two people talking: first meeting, growing comfort, a small disagreement, an argument, a repair, a settled ending. Stick figures are fine.
2. Under each frame, note how many conversation pieces there are and how sharp they are.
3. Show the frames without captions to one person. Ask them which stage each frame is.

**Good looks like:** the difficulty curve matches the mood, and the reader can name each stage without the captions.

### Exercise 4: Trophy reveal in two frames (about 3 hours)

**Goal:** build the most transferable device from *A Memoir Blue* [29][30] in a free tool.

**Steps:**
1. Open **VectorCraft** (installed, early alpha). If it cannot do two layers and a PNG export, use **Inkscape** as the proven fallback, per the charter rule that nothing critical depends on one alpha app.
2. Make two layers: a shelf of trophies in front, and a photograph behind. Use a placeholder photo, not any photo of her.
3. Export two still frames: "before" (trophies in front) and "after" (trophies moved aside, photo visible).
4. Write one line on how a viewer would know the trophies can move. Ask one person whether they knew what to do without being told.

**Good looks like:** the "after" frame changes how the whole shelf reads. Making it interactive is a later question for the toolchain track.

### Exercise 5: Chime pacing test (about 1 hour)

**Goal:** learn the pause-chime idea from *Before Your Eyes* [14] without a webcam.

**Steps:**
1. Choose a 20-second silent sequence: three slides or a short clip you own. Do not use family footage.
2. Mark three moments where a soft chime says "you may pause here."
3. Play it for one person who is not the subject. Ask when they felt able to stop, and whether they waited for the chime.

**Good looks like:** the viewer knows when to act or wait without any words of instruction. TODO(research): the notes give no timing values. Choose the intervals by testing.

### Exercise 6: Voxel readability (about 2 hours, MagicaVoxel)

**Goal:** learn what makes a blocky object read as warm rather than generic [64][65].

**Steps:**
1. Before using it, read the licence on the MagicaVoxel website. It says "Free to use for any project", asks for credit, and disallows selling or distributing the software in other packages [76]. The Windows download has no licence file [78].
2. Model one object from your own home, such as a mug or a kettle, about 16 voxels across.
3. Use 6 to 8 colours. Paint the shading into the colours. Keep one voxel size.
4. Shrink the object to thumbnail size and check the silhouette. Remove stair-step diagonals.

**Good looks like:** recognisable at thumbnail size, few colours, no default Minecraft textures.

### Exercise 7: Minecraft terms check (about 1 hour)

**Goal:** know what is allowed before you choose a visual source.

**Steps:**
1. Read the Minecraft Usage Guidelines [42] and the End User Licence Agreement [43].
2. Copy the clauses about sharing, public display, selling and endorsement into a one-page note. Write down the date you read them. Save the exact clauses as a dated plain-text excerpt at research/sources/minecraft-usage-guidelines-YYYY-MM-DD.md (short quotes, URL, date), so the terms check never depends on the page being up. Do the same once for the Eurogamer interview [3] (it carries the Bed Song, 14,000-foley, spreadsheet and "hollow" claims) at research/sources/eurogamer-unpacking-YYYY-MM-DD.md.
3. List the questions a lawyer would need to answer, such as whether a private family screening in a hired room counts as public display.

**Good looks like:** each clause has a quote or close paraphrase, a date and a source. The questions are written down. Mojang says it will not advise on a specific project, so the questions go to a lawyer [42]. The usage page has no visible date, and no dated version has been found. A third-party "April 19, 2026" date [71] has been withdrawn by its source, so do not cite it [83]. Re-read the page on the day of any showing.

### Exercise 8: Watch Brier, then Gaynor (about 2 hours)

**Goal:** hear two designers argue that interaction carries meaning. Brier's talk is closer to our problem, so it comes first.

**Steps:**
1. Watch Wren Brier's GDC 2023 talk, "'Unpacking' Zen: Designing a Game Without Fail States or Scores" [2]. GDC Vault tags it "free content".
2. Watch Steve Gaynor's GDC 2014 talk, "Why is Gone Home a game?" The notes say the video is free [36].
3. Write three notes: one device, why it works, and one thing you would cut.
4. Check each note against the eight principles.

**Good looks like:** three notes, each tied to a principle. TODO(research): Dallas's 2018 talk [10] has not been watched.

### Exercise 9: Ten-minute click test (about 1 hour, plus finding a tester)

**Goal:** find out whether a click-only device works for an older hand.

**Steps:**
1. Find a consenting older adult who is not the subject. Do not test with her, because the surprise would be at risk. TODO(research): the notes suggest a real 70-year-old on the actual hardware. Decide the tester with the surprise-ops track [41].
2. Give the tester one click-only prototype, such as the chime or the trophy reveal, on the hardware they would use.
3. Watch for ten minutes without helping. Count wrong-direction moves, missed double clicks, time spent unsure, and how often they ask what to do.

**Good looks like:** they reach the end without help and without visible stress. Older adults in one study felt stressed by time limits [41]. TODO(research): no direct study of trackpad drag or click-and-hold for older adults was found.

## Decisions you'll need to make (options and our current lean)

**1. Visual source.** Options: Minecraft's look and footage; original voxel or vector assets; a mix. *Our lean:* original assets in MagicaVoxel or Blockbench [49][50][51], with Minecraft as a reference only.

**2. Minecraft edition and computer.** Options: Java; Bedrock; no Minecraft at all. Replay Mod is a Java Edition mod (Fabric for 1.14+, Forge for 1.12.2 and below) and Flashback is a Fabric client mod; neither page mentions Bedrock [44][46]. Mineways exports Java worlds to OBJ and USD for Blender [48]. *Our lean:* no decision yet. The maker's computer is Windows (charter Known facts). TODO(research): Which Minecraft edition, if any, does the maker own? (Java is needed for Replay Mod, Flashback and Mineways.)

**3. Showing it.** Options: a private family showing at home; a hired venue; online. *Our lean:* private, free, no advertising, no Minecraft logo, original assets. A hired venue is a grey area under Mojang's wording [42]. TODO(research): ask a lawyer, or choose original assets.

**4. Input.** Options: click once; drag; keyboard; webcam. *Our lean:* click once only, with auto-advance [14][41].

**5. Pacing.** Options: timed auto-advance; advance on click; chime-gated pauses. *Our lean:* advance on click, with a chime marking pause moments and a host pause. Text advances on action, not on a timer [40].

**6. Reconstruction style.** Options: one look for everything; a different medium for reconstructions. *Our lean:* a different medium, with a visible label [13].

**7. Number of personal styles.** Options: one look; one style per key person. *Our lean:* at most three to five. The team behind *Edith Finch* built and dropped many prototypes to land 13 stories [10][11].

**8. Locked chapter.** Options: none; a locked door that the family decides about. *Our lean:* only if the trusted proxy agrees (Principle 5). The Minecraft Vault is the model: each player opens it once, with its own key [55].

**9. Format.** The format track decides. *Our lean from this track only:* a click-through scroll story with a few voxel dioramas, because it needs no gamer hands. TODO(research): confirm with the format track.

**10. Redstone or working machinery.** *Our lean:* skip, unless she truly worked with circuits or clockwork. TODO(research): redstone was not researched.

**11. Trophies moved aside.** *Our lean:* yes, as one device near the end. It turns the milestone list into a question [30].

## Pitfalls

- **Points and milestone scoreboards.** A life is not a leaderboard [37][38].
- **Progress bars and completion percentages.** They imply she is unfinished.
- **Stock vocabulary.** Circle-of-life day and night, generic biomes, treasure chests, hearts and hunger bars. Day and night in Minecraft run on a 20-minute cycle [53].
- **Enemies as illness, divorce or money.** This is a dignity risk (Principle 5). It is our judgment, in line with Ryan Green's view that mechanics should be metaphor, not spectacle [18].
- **Fail states and timers.** Older adults disliked time limits [41]. *Gris* has no fail state and was designed that way for accessibility [26].
- **Drag, swipe, double-tap, long-press, blinking, webcams.** Older participants in one study slid in the wrong direction, forgot to lift their fingers and failed double taps [41]. Dragging terrain is the core of *Old Man's Journey* [19], so convert it to animation.
- **Elegy by default.** She is alive. *Passage*, *Spiritfarer* and *Edith Finch* are about death. Borrow their devices, not their mood [23][27][41].
- **Invented events, people or quotes.** This breaks Principle 4 [11][13]. No synthetic, cloned or text-to-speech voice of any real person, living or dead, even with consent (charter review §1.2). Where she cannot be recorded, real words for surprise mode are existing home video or voice notes; her own letters and cards; or a saying that two relatives independently confirm, shown *attributed* ("as her sister remembers it"), never as a quotation in her voice.
- **Cloning famous looks.** Copying *Florence*'s couch colours or *Unpacking*'s boxes without her facts [3].
- **Minecraft UI cosplay.** Hotbars, hearts and achievement pop-ups look cheap and fall under Mojang's brand rules [42].
- **Solo assumptions.** Design for a host and a room [3][61].
- **Second-hand memorial stories.** The Gas Bandit tribute is reported by Salon and Everplans, and the original post was not found. Do not repeat it as fact [57][58].
- **Calibration and comfort.** Reviews of *Before Your Eyes* mention calibration trouble [69]. Reports of eye discomfort are unverified.
- **A broken link.** The hideandseek.net copy of Robertson's essay now redirects to an unrelated gambling site. Use the Kotaku republication [37].
- **Surprise leaks.** Test on a machine she does not use. Screen recordings must not sync to a cloud photo library, and test screenshots must not go into family group chats (charter, known facts).
- **Add-on privacy.** The Flashback Settings add-on is listed as PolyForm Noncommercial, with telemetry on by default [47]. Read its settings before installing it on any computer that holds family material (confirmed on Modrinth 2026-10-18: "Anonymous usage statistics are enabled by default").
- **A creator's reputation.** Wikipedia reports public allegations against Florence's director and an apology, but this was not checked against primary reporting [9]. If we credit *Florence* publicly, decide knowingly.
- **Our numbers.** Ratings, time estimates and build effort are judgment. Check them before quoting.
