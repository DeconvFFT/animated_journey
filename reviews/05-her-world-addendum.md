## 1. The film, in her idiom

**The concept, in one paragraph.** The film is a day in her house that holds her whole life. It opens in the dark on the first act of a Pushtimarg morning, Yamunaji's: water poured into brass before anyone is awake, one true sound above 400 Hz that carries on her phone as well as in the hall, then first light at the Mangla hour (a single flame against blue-black kitchen glass, 05:30 in her town's winter), no rhythm, no card, no name, which to a Vaishnav in the room is Mangla itself and to everyone else is a kitchen before dawn; both are true. The wrapper's camera is her evening seat: the slow pendulum of the hichko, the one piece of Gujarati furniture that is both daily and hers by use, from which the kitchen doorway swings into and out of frame and whose chain is a sound every Gujarati in the room knows before they see the swing. Between them sit the kept-by modules (W-04): one place, one object, one device, one turn each, every chapter opened on her hands doing a making or a tending (the stitch, the flower string, the sathiyo line, the dough, the velan), the one domestic object that followed her through every home as the only transition and the thing that stands under the screen (W-32), the kitchen as the only built set with its hour of light keyed to the eight darshans and never named (W-34), and each chapter card dated the way her house would say it (Gujarati script, Gujarati digits, "Maha sud Pancham") with the Gregorian date in the caption track. The look is her prints' own palette per era; the primary drawn idiom is the floor-art line of her region if her hand draws it; the frame register is the pichwai manner in season (never a niche, never a svarupa), which for the Vasant weeks that hold the premiere means white malmal and cream with gulal only as a puff over hands and table; the goddesses keep their own register, Shiva is the linga, the sea and the lamp; no deity is drawn, generated or photographed (P1). The sound is objects, rooms, the sea's surf and the maker's hum of the tune she sang, closed-mouth in the hall where a room can carry it and on an open vowel on her phone where only that survives; sung devotion waits for April. The ending returns to the hichko at the hour she actually sits in it, the open chapter card in native Gujarati ("her story, in her words"), the object set down by the maker's hands, the hum, and then the one card that is sampradaya text and ordinary speech at once, "જય શ્રી કૃષ્ણ", alone on the frame, in her script, at the phone floor: on her phone it is the screen saying what she says when she picks up; in the public room it is the one line a Vaishnav will answer aloud, which is the sound the reveal wants and nobody can script. Then the sound of the room the audience sits in, black, credits. Every one of these choices is keyed to a question only the maker can answer (section 8); if the answer is no, that piece falls away and nothing else changes.

**Alternative A: the kitchen clock (the fully domestic version).** If O-46, Q3 and Q7 say the house keeps little or no ghar seva (no Yamunajal, no Mangla at home, a picture only), the wrapper loses its Pushtimarg morning and keeps its truth: the first sound is a spoon on a steel vati or the cooker's whistle (both PASS on the phone model), the first light the kitchen window at the 05:30 hour, the kitchen's eight hours across the modules in non-darshan order are the whole clock, and the ending is the thepla tin or the masala dabbo closed by the maker's hands on the table. The darshan table still sets the light and is never seen. Cost: nothing new; it is the plan as written in W-34 and W-36 with the Gujarati kitchen's own sounds (Template F S-B1 to S-B5 of the sound playbook). Risk: it is the version a stranger might read as "a Gujarati kitchen" rather than *hers*; the object clock and the cards carry the specificity.

**Alternative B: the threshold year.** If O-40/Q8 says her hands draw the sathiyo and the maker chooses SW1 (O-64 renumbered, section 6), the modules are opened not on the kitchen but on the threshold at the festivals she keeps: the Diwali sathiyo and Lakshmi's rice-flour footprints, the Annakut mound of her own dishes as a still life, the Vasant kalash on an eight-petal lotus on a sathiya for the premiere season, the toran changed by hands above. The floor-art line is then decisions 2, 6 and 11 of the style bible at once (04 §1.3), the module-break frame is the plain Chaulukya toran (saurashtra Template I), and the camera never enters the mandir. Cost: one Drik day-page per festival and the native reader's pass. Risks: the utsav year is a tempting *structure* and W-04 forbids a structure that is not one place, one object, one turn per module, so the festivals may only be the opener and the frame, never the spine; a festival enters only when `kept_by_her = yes` (O-39/O-50/O-56); and no era may rise above about 25% of runtime (W-39), which a festival-led film tends to break for the present. I would not choose B over the main concept; I would let it supply the title card and one module's opener.

### 2.2 Reverence rules every shot obeys

These extend 04 §1.2 and the hindu-visual R1 to R20 and never loosen them. The full lists are pushtimarg T2 (VR-1 to VR-3, PR1 to PR25) and saurashtra's Reverence section; the agents run them as `auto`, `agent` or `maker` checks in every G2 pack, and Fable reads every flagged brief and every deity shot.

1. **The svarupa is never a character** (PR1, PR3). No Shrinathji, Bal Krishna, Thakorji, Mahaprabhuji or Yamunaji figure moves, blinks, speaks, walks, morphs or is lip-synced, by hand or by AI; eyes are never drawn or opened ("opening the eyes is the pran pratishtha of the picture", the painters' own rule). An agent cannot draw a svarupa correctly even if allowed: the shringar is fixed for 365 days and only a hereditary painter at the temple that day knows it (PR2).
