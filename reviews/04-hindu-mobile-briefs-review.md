## Part 0 — What the three new decisions change before any track starts

### 0.1 The maker's rule, read strictly and applied kindly

D-28 is a rule about *motifs and world*, and it is the maker's to set. The team applies it as written: Hindu gods, epics, history, temple architecture, art traditions, Sanskrit and her regional language are welcome; nothing Mughal, Islamic or Christian, ever; anything shared or ambiguous is flagged and left out by default. Three readings follow that every track must hold:

1. **Personal before pan-Hindu.** Charter Principle 2 already says a Hindu motif earns its place by being *hers*. The maker has not yet told us her region, language, sampradaya, family deity, temple or festivals (O-02, O-10 are still pending). Until those ten lines arrive (W-57, due Sun 25 Oct), every track writes **decision tables keyed on the unknowns**, not a single "Indian" answer. A Tamil Shaiva household and a Maithil Shakta household share almost nothing in colour, script, floor art, calendar, deity or song.
2. **"Shared" is a category, not a loophole.** Many things Hindus use daily have a shared or non-Hindu origin (the harmonium, the paisley, mehndi, cusped arches, the word "Hindustani" in Hindustani music, the Persianate conventions behind Rajput and Pahari painting). The rule is: name the origin honestly in the research, flag it, default to *out*, and put the exception to the maker in one line each. Never present a shared motif as Hindu to get it past the rule, and never quietly include it because "she would not mind". The maker decides; the team's job is to make the flag accurate.
3. **Dharmic is not the same as Hindu.** Sikh, Jain and Buddhist art and texts are close cousins and will surface constantly in searches (Gurbani in Punjabi devotional music, Jain miniature painting, Ajanta murals). They are *not* covered by the maker's definition unless the maker says so. Treat them as a fourth flag category ("dharmic, not Hindu; ask") rather than silently including or excluding them. The same goes for Adivasi art (Warli, Gond, Bhil, Saura): often sold as "Indian folk art", not automatically Hindu, with their own deities; flag and ask.

A fourth reading is mine to raise as advisor, under Principle 5 (dignity in front of a public room): **her devotion, not a position.** The room is mostly strangers, possibly outside India. Contemporary temple-politics imagery, party colours, slogans and maps carry a meaning in a mixed public room that has nothing to do with her life. The film shows what she keeps, sings and lights. Any exception is the maker's, decided like a hard chapter (a written line, 72 hours, default out).

### 0.2 D-27 is a second screen, not a second film

"Work beautifully on a phone" means the *same* 16:9 picture (W-15) must read at 6 inches: text and captions legible at arm's length, dark passages not swallowed by a bright room, fine line work not turned to mush by a messaging app, and a file that survives being sent. It does not mean a vertical re-edit; an art-directed 16:9 film reframed to 9:16 is a second film we cannot afford (W-01, D-24). The phone track must prove or refute that in numbers, but the lean is: one frame, designed phone-safe; a second burn-in of captions sized and placed for phones; two phone files; and a keepsake that is the film itself on her phone, put there by cable, never by cloud (D-15, W-25).

Two things in the existing plan break under D-27 and must be re-decided, not patched:

- **The companion is "the offline scrubber page on the USB she takes home" (W-30, format Decision 11).** A USB stick is not a phone deliverable. Whether her phone can read a USB drive (Android OTG; iPhone 15 or later with USB-C) and whether a self-contained HTML page opened from a phone's Files app actually plays video are open questions the `mobile-delivery` track must answer *on her phone model*, which nobody has asked for yet (new maker question, 0.5).
- **Burned-in captions inside the 90% box at 8% line height (W-18, TD-3)** were sized for a TV at three metres. On a phone the viewer may tap "fill" and the 16:9 picture is cropped to the phone's taller screen: a 20:9 phone showing a 16:9 picture full-bleed loses about 10% of the picture height top *and* bottom (computed: 16:9 scaled to fill a 20:9 width is 11.25 units tall against a 9-unit screen). A caption sitting at the 5% margin is then cut in half. The phone file needs its captions higher and probably larger; the venue file keeps W-18. Captions are a render step, so a second burn-in is cheap; the decision is where the line sits.

## Part 1 — Track `hindu-visual`: Hindu visual language for an animated life film

### 1.1 Refined brief

Build the visual world for an animated life film of a Hindu woman from India so that every tradition, set and symbol can be traced to *her* region, lineage and hands, within D-28. The output is not a survey of Indian art; it is a decision kit: a scored matrix of six to ten Hindu painting, craft and performance traditions as *animation styles* (what each looks like, where it is from, what it depicts, how it has been animated, and how it moves and reads at 10 metres and at 6 inches); a temple-architecture vocabulary for sets, frames and transitions keyed on Nagara, Dravida and Vesara so the right one is chosen once her region is known; an iconography do/don't checklist that a Sonnet implementor and the maker can apply shot by shot; an ambiguous-motif exclusion list with the reason for each entry; a rights route for every image source (public-domain artists, museum open-access collections with their *actual* licence text, living folk artists and realistic commissioning); and a `hindu_idiom` style-bible block that slots into cinematic-quality T2 as decision 11 without disturbing decisions 1 to 10 (W-38). The track must also settle how AI image and video tools are and are not used for deities, in line with D-16 and the charter's "free of AI artifacts" bar.

The existing style bible stays in charge: palette per era from her scans (decision 1), her own hand-made material as the reconstruction marker (decisions 2 and 6), hands as the grammar (W-33), one built set (W-34). The Hindu idiom dresses the world, the titles, the cards, the transitions and the deity imagery; it does not restyle real faces (D-16, W-40) and it does not replace the object clock as the transition device (W-32). Where a Hindu craft *is* what her hands made (kolam, alpana, embroidery), decision 2 and decision 11 become one thing, and the track should say so.

### 1.2 Must-answer questions

1. For each of the candidate traditions (Pahari/Kangra and Rajput Hindu court painting, Tanjore and Mysore, Kerala temple murals, Pattachitra, Madhubani/Mithila, Srikalahasti Kalamkari, Phad, Cheriyal, Kalighat, Nathdwara pichwai, Raja Ravi Varma, Tholu Bommalata and Togalu Gombeyaata shadow puppetry, and the floor arts kolam, rangoli, alpana, mandana, aipan, muggu, jhoti): what is its region and its Hindu subject matter, which sampradaya does it serve, what are its line, palette and figure conventions, and which of those conventions break when the image moves (a Pattachitra border that must stay still; a Kalamkari that is drawn, not painted; a Tanjore relief that catches light)?
2. Which of them has been animated to a standard, by whom, and what did they do with motion (cut-out, line-draw, parallax, painted-on-glass, stop-motion)? Name the exemplars and anti-exemplars with dates: *Ramayana: The Legend of Prince Rama* (1992), *Sita Sings the Blues* (2008; style reference only; check its licence status and the controversy around it before citing it as a model), *Bombay Rose* (2019), Nina Sabnani's folk-art animations at IDC IIT Bombay, Studio Eeksaurus, *Arjun: The Warrior Prince* (2012), *The Legend of Hanuman* (2021–), *Mahavatar Narsimha* (2025), Amar Chitra Katha, *Little Krishna*, *Chhota Bheem* as the floor to stay above. All of these are leads from memory; verify titles, years and studios.
3. How does each tradition read on a 6-inch phone after a messaging app's recompression: which lose their line (Madhubani hatching, Pattachitra fill), which hold (Kalighat's bold brush, Kerala murals' five-colour blocks, shadow puppets' silhouettes), and what minimum stroke width and detail scale does each need at 1080p (cinematic-quality decision 3 says 2 px; the phone probably needs 3)?
4. What is the architectural vocabulary (shikhara, vimana, gopuram, mandapa, garbhagriha, kalasha, amalaka, torana, dvarapala, stepwell tiers) for Nagara, Dravida and Vesara, with one clean public-domain or open-access reference image per element, so that a set or a frame device can be chosen in one table once O-36 is answered, and which elements are shared with Indo-Islamic building (jaali, chhatri, cusped arch, dome) and therefore flagged?
5. For the deities most likely to be hers (Ganesha, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Durga, Kali, Parvati, Shiva, Vishnu and his avataras, Krishna, Rama with Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman, Murugan, Ayyappa, Venkateswara, Vitthal, Jagannath, Annapurna, and the regional mother goddesses): what are the attributes, hand positions (mudras), mounts (vahanas), colours, number of arms and their contents *per form and region*, the sampradaya marks (urdhva pundra versus tripundra), and the well-known regional variants (Jagannath's abstract form; Ganesha's trunk direction and the folk rules around it; Gajalakshmi; Durga on the lion versus regional tiger forms)? Which temple bodies assert control over their deity's image (the Tirumala and Jagannath administrations are leads to verify)?
6. What do practitioners and devotional animators treat as disrespectful, and how do they handle it: feet toward or on a deity image, footwear, placement near the floor or below the fold, deity images on things that get discarded, cutting a deity at the neck by the frame, dissolving or wiping a deity away as a transition, comic or casual treatment, distortion, sexualisation, AI alteration of a murti? Write the respect rules as shot rules the validator and the maker can check.
7. For AI image and video generation of deities: what are the documented failure modes (miscounted arms, swapped attributes between deities, garbled ornaments and script, wrong vahana, feet and hands malformed, "glossy" gold), what community reactions have there been to AI deity images (leads: the 2025 "Ghibli-style" trend; objections by Hindu organisations; the Nathdwara and Tirumala positions), and what is the safe route (none in the public cut; hand-drawn or agent-drawn vector symbols; tracing from public-domain plates with the source logged)?
8. Rights, per source: which museum open-access programmes hold South Asian material and under what *exact* terms (Smithsonian Open Access, the Met, Cleveland, Art Institute of Chicago, LACMA, Philadelphia, Rijksmuseum, Wellcome Collection with its Kalighat holdings, Harvard, the San Diego Museum of Art's Binney collection; the British Museum and V&A as non-commercial or restricted; Wikimedia Commons file by file; Google Arts & Culture as view-only)? For Raja Ravi Varma (died 1906, so his works are public domain) which *reproductions* carry a museum claim, and how does the premiere country treat photographs of public-domain 2D art (the US position after Bridgeman v. Corel; the UK position since 2023; India's position)? UNVERIFIED leads, all of them; the researcher reads the licence page and saves a dated excerpt in `research/sources/`.
9. Commissioning a living traditional artist: realistic costs and lead times for a single Pattachitra, Madhubani, Tanjore or Kalamkari piece (via craft councils, cooperatives and direct contact; the GI status of these crafts and what it does and does not restrict), what a name-free brief looks like ("a painting of Annapurna in the Thanjavur manner, 30 by 40 cm") so nobody is brought in (D-03, D-07), and the fact that in India buying the painting does not transfer the copyright: an assignment or a licence for film use must be in writing (Copyright Act 1957 s.19 is the lead; not legal advice; add to the T10 consult list).
10. Which of all this can be built by agents as seekable SVG scenes (Route A, W-23) rather than painted: kolam and rangoli as parametric line-draw; torana and kalasha as vector; shadow-puppet cut-outs as layered SVG with hinge points; and which needs a human hand in VectorCraft or on paper?

### 1.3 Watch-outs

1. **Generic "India".** Taj Mahal silhouettes, snake charmers, saffron sunsets with a drone shot of a temple, incense-smoke stock footage, lens flare on a diya, a "curry" display font, Bollywood poster framing, "Incredible India" grading. The test is W-38's: if the shot would work for another family, it is out. Add these to cinematic-quality T5 (banned cheap tells).
2. **Ambiguous motifs presented as Hindu.** Cusped and multifoil arches, onion domes, jaali screens, chhatris, Persianate miniature conventions (the Mughal and Deccani ancestry of Rajput and Pahari painting is real; the *subject matter* of Kangra is Vaishnava, which is why it can stay with a flag), paisley and buta, mehndi, Indo-Saracenic palaces and railway stations, the Hawa Mahal's façade. Also the colonial-era Christian layer in popular prints: cherubs and winged figures in some Ravi Varma Press oleographs, gold-disc halos drawn like a church icon rather than a prabhavali (the flame arch), candles where diyas belong, church-shaped bells where a ghanta belongs, winged apsaras (they have none). Every exclusion-list entry carries a one-line origin note so the maker can overrule with knowledge.
3. **Wrong iconography.** The wrong tilak on the wrong deity, Vishnu's attributes in the wrong hands, Saraswati with a sitar, Ganesha with two whole tusks, a left-handed conch, a four-armed Hanuman, a pichwai Shrinathji in a Shaiva household, a Bengal Kali for a Tamil family, a lion for a goddess who rides a tiger in her region. The checklist must be per form, and the implementor's brief must name the form.
4. **"Public domain" scans that are not.** A Wikimedia file tagged PD-Art whose upload is a museum photograph with a claim in the UK; a Google Arts & Culture zoom; a Pinterest repost of a living artist's Pattachitra; an Amar Chitra Katha panel; a photograph of a temple murti taken where photography is forbidden; a temple trust's copyrighted calendar art. Every image in the film has a T8 row with the licence text saved and dated (W-46).
5. **AI deities.** No generated deity figure in the public cut, however good (D-16's spirit, Principle 6's "free of AI artifacts", and community sensitivity). Deity imagery comes from public-domain plates, open-access museum images, the maker's own photographs taken where permitted, or vector symbols drawn by agents and checked against the iconography sheet. Any AI assistance on a deity image (clean-up, upscaling) is logged on the L-ladder (W-40) and reviewed by the maker as proxy.
6. **The swastika.** It is a sacred Hindu symbol drawn at every threshold and in every puja. In a mostly public room outside India, or in any room with people who do not know it, it reads as something else. This is the maker's call alone (O-44 informs it). Default: use it only in its ritual form and context (kumkum-red, dotted, on a kalash or a threshold, inside a rangoli, right-facing) and only when the room is in India or the maker decides the room will read it correctly; otherwise leave it out and let Om, the lotus, the kalash and the diya carry the threshold.

### 3.3 Watch-outs

2. **The Mughal-court and church layers in the sound.** The sitar, the tabla, the khayal, the shehnai, the santoor and the harmonium are everywhere in Indian devotional practice and have a shared or non-Hindu history. 3. **Phone speakers.** A tanpura drone and a low pakhawaj vanish on a phone speaker; the conch, the bell, the bansuri and the voice survive. The mix must pass music-sound's mono check *and* a phone-speaker check at 50% volume, which Part 4 specifies.
4. **AI vocals in sacred languages.** Mispronounced Sanskrit and garbled deity names are both a tell and an offence. No AI vocal of any devotional text. W-44 already bans AI on the peak; this extends it.
5. **Fonts that look right and shape wrong.** A display font with a beautiful Devanagari sample can lack the conjunct your card needs; a Latin font without ṛ and ṃ silently substitutes glyphs from another face. Every title and caption string is rendered through the real pipeline and inspected at 200% before G1.
6. **Stale terms.** Every tool term, label policy and licensing society fact carries the date it was read; anything from before 2026-10-01 is re-read.

### 4.3 Watch-outs

1. **Deriving the phone file from the venue file.** Two lossy encodes in a row (venue H.264 to phone H.264) and a messaging recompression on top turn fine line art to mush. The phone ladder is encoded from the ProRes master (W-15), and the messaging copy is a separate encode, not a resize of P1.
2. **Captions cut by the fill crop.** The venue caption at the 5% margin is lost when the viewer fills a 20:9 screen (0.2). The phone file carries its own burn-in higher up; the venue file is unchanged. Test on the actual players, not on a diagram.
3. **The cold open goes black on a phone in daylight.** "Black and one true room sound" (W-36) is a feature in a dark hall and a dead phone in a bright kitchen. Either the phone file lifts the floor one step in Trim P (a third trim beside Trim V and Trim C, applied like them on top of the one grade) or the first sound is strong enough to hold her. The track decides and tells cinematic-quality.
4. **A cloud copy by accident.** A test phone with Google Photos or iCloud backup on uploads the slice the moment it lands in the gallery. "Send as document to myself" puts family media on WhatsApp's servers. Both breach D-15 and D-02 before the reveal. Hygiene is check 1 of the protocol and a P-LOCK item.
5. **Stale app facts.** WhatsApp, Telegram and iCloud limits and behaviours change every few months and differ by platform and region. Every number carries the date it was read and the platform; anything that cannot be read is UNVERIFIED and tested empirically on the project phone instead.
6. **Forwarding changes the rights picture.** After the reveal, a WhatsApp forward is a public distribution the rights log did not model: Tier B stills of relatives who have not yet signed (W-46, W-47) travel with it. The keepsake for *her* is one copy on her phone; copies for relatives wait for the ledger (W-48), and releases 4d–4g gain a phone-sharing line.
